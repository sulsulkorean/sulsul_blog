The Korean words a tourist actually uses are short, single nouns: **화장실** (*hwajangsil*, toilet), **이거** (*igeo*, this), **물** (*mul*, water), **역** (*yeok*, station), **출구** (*chulgu*, exit), and a handful more. You don't need grammar to use them. Say the word, then add one of three endings — **주세요** (*juseyo*, "please give me"), **어디예요?** (*eodiyeyo?*, "where is it?") or **있어요?** (*isseoyo?*, "do you have it?") — and a single word becomes a polite, complete request. Twenty words and three endings will carry you from the airport to your last dinner.

> **The trick in one line**
> - Word + **주세요** → "___, please." (**물 주세요** — "Water, please.")
> - Word + **어디예요?** → "Where is ___?" (**화장실 어디예요?** — "Where's the toilet?")
> - Word + **있어요?** → "Do you have ___?" (**봉투 있어요?** — "Do you have a bag?")

## Why single words work so well in Korea

Korean drops a lot of what English keeps. Subjects, "the," "a," even the little particles that mark objects — in everyday speech people leave them out. A Korean shopper really does say **물 주세요**, not a longer textbook sentence. So when you walk up with one word and a polite ending, you are not speaking broken Korean. You are speaking the way people already talk at a counter.

The other reason is that staff in tourist areas hear the same few questions all day. The moment they catch **화장실** or **얼마**, they know what you want. Your job is just to land the key word clearly.

**Comfort and trouble**

| Korean | Sound | Meaning |
|---|---|---|

Notice how many are borrowed from English — 버스, 택시, 카드, 메뉴, 와이파이. Say them the Korean way, with even beats and no strong stress: *beo-seu*, not "BUS." That small change is often the difference between being understood and being asked to repeat.

## Three endings that turn a word into a request

**주세요 — when you want something.** This is the pattern behind most of a trip. **이거 주세요** while pointing at a menu photo. **물 주세요** at a table. **영수증 주세요** at the register. If you want to soften it, slip **좀** (*jom*) in the middle: **물 좀 주세요** sounds less like an order and more like "could I get some water."

**어디예요? — when you need to find something.** **화장실 어디예요?** is the single most-asked question by visitors in Korea, and for good reason. It works for anything with a location: **역 어디예요?**, **약국 어디예요?**, **3번 출구 어디예요?** Expect the answer to be a pointing hand plus **저기요** (*jeogiyo*, "over there") or **쭉 가세요** (*jjuk gaseyo*, "go straight").

**있어요? — when you're not sure they have it.** **와이파이 있어요?** at a cafe. **봉투 있어요?** at the convenience store. **영어 메뉴 있어요?** at a restaurant. The answer is either **네, 있어요** ("yes, we have it") or **없어요** (*eopseoyo*, "we don't").

And one bonus that stands alone: **얼마예요?** (*eolmayeyo?*), "how much is it?" Point at the item and say it.

## A short scene: one word at a time

You land, take the train into the city, and step out at your stop. Here is how a first afternoon sounds with nothing but the list above.

At the station, you stop a staff member: **실례합니다, 5번 출구 어디예요?** — "Excuse me, where's exit 5?" They point: **저기요.**

At the convenience store, you hold up a bottle: **이거 얼마예요?** The clerk says the price and asks **봉투 필요하세요?** ("Do you need a bag?"). You answer **아니요, 괜찮아요** — "No, it's fine."

At a small restaurant, you sit down and say **메뉴 주세요**, then point at a photo: **이거 하나 주세요** — "One of this, please." Halfway through: **물 좀 주세요.** Before you leave: **화장실 어디예요?**

That is a full afternoon in Korean, and you never built a sentence longer than four words.

## Small slips that trip up tourists

**Saying the word with a question in English.** "Toilet? 화장실?" with rising English intonation can confuse people. Add **어디예요?** instead and let the ending carry the question.

**Forgetting 이거.** Visitors try to pronounce the name of every dish on the menu. You don't have to. **이거** plus a finger does the job in any restaurant, market or shop.

**Dropping 요 with strangers.** **물 줘** (*mul jwo*) is what you'd say to a close friend. With staff, always end with **주세요** or **요** — it is the word-level version of "please."

**Mixing up 역 and 출구.** At big stations you're already "at the station" — what you actually need is the exit number. Asking for the 출구 gets you a far more useful answer.

**Freezing when they answer in a full sentence.** You will catch one or two words — **저기**, **없어요**, a number. That is enough. Reply **네, 감사합니다** (*ne, gamsahamnida*), "Yes, thank you," and follow the hand.
