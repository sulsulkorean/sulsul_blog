**애교** (*aegyo*) means acting cute on purpose — a sweet, babyish voice, a pout, a little gesture, all done to charm someone or get your way. An aegyo line is an ordinary Korean sentence said in that voice: **해 줘잉~** (*hae jwoing*), "do it for me, pleeease"; **고마워용** (*gomawoyong*), a cutesy "thank you"; **나 삐졌어** (*na ppijyeosseo*), "I'm sulking now." You hear these in every K-drama couple scene and every time an idol is asked on a variety show to **애교 보여 주세요** (*aegyo boyeo juseyo*), "show us some aegyo." The good news for learners: aegyo is not a separate vocabulary. It is a handful of sound tricks you put on top of Korean you already know.

> - Do some aegyo! — **애교 부려 봐!** (*aegyo buryeo bwa!*)
> - Show us some aegyo, please — **애교 보여 주세요** (*aegyo boyeo juseyo*)
> - Do it for me~ — **해 줘잉~** (*hae jwoing*)
> - Thank you~ — **고마워용** (*gomawoyong*)
> - I missed you~ — **보고 싶었쪄** (*bogo sipeotjjeo*)
> - I'm sulking — **나 삐졌어** (*na ppijyeosseo*)
> - Hug me — **안아 줘** (*ana jwo*)
> - Hmph! — **힝** (*hing*)
> - The finger-on-cheeks move — **뿌잉뿌잉** (*ppuing ppuing*)
> - She's full of aegyo — **애교가 많아요** (*aegyoga manayo*)

## Where the word 애교 comes from

**애교** is written 愛嬌 in Chinese characters: "love" plus "charm." Korean treats it as something you *do*, not something you *are*, so it takes the verb **부리다** (*burida*), "to put on, to show off." **애교 부리지 마** (*aegyo buriji ma*) is "stop acting cute," usually said by someone who is secretly enjoying it.

To describe a person, use **애교가 많다** (*aegyoga manta*), "has a lot of aegyo," or the opposite, **애교가 없다** (*aegyoga eopda*), "has no aegyo." Both come up constantly on dating shows, because whether your partner has aegyo is treated as a real personality trait in Korea, a bit like being "affectionate" in English. The bigger family of cute words — **귀여워**, **심쿵**, **귀요미** — is in [Korean slang for cute](/korean-slang-for-cute).

## The four sound tricks behind every aegyo line

Once you notice these, you can turn almost any sentence into aegyo.

| Trick | Normal | Aegyo version | What changes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 요 → 용 | 고마워요 | **고마워용** | A final ㅇ makes it bouncy |
| Add 잉 | 해 줘 | **해 줘잉~** | A whiny, nasal tail |
| Baby talk (ㅅ, ㅆ → ㅊ, ㅉ) | 보고 싶었어 | **보고 싶었쪄** | Sounds like a toddler's lisp |
| Stretch the vowel | 오빠 | **오빠아~** | Rising, drawn-out pitch |

The baby-talk trick is the one that sounds strangest to learners. Korean toddlers often cannot say ㅅ cleanly, so adults imitate that: **사랑해** (*saranghae*) becomes **따랑해** (*ttaranghae*), and **했어** becomes **했쪄**. It sounds silly on purpose. That silliness is the point.

## Aegyo lines you hear in K-dramas

Most aegyo in dramas is between couples, and it follows a few fixed shapes.

**Asking for something.** Pattern 01, **(N) 주세요**, is the base of half of all aegyo. In polite form it is a request; drop 요, stretch it, and add 잉, and it becomes a plea. **안아 줘** (*ana jwo*), "hug me." **사 줘잉~** (*sa jwoing*), "buy it for me~." **같이 가 줘** (*gachi ga jwo*), "come with me." The base pattern, without the cute voice, is in [how to say please give me in Korean](/how-to-say-please-give-me-in-korean).

**Sulking.** **나 삐졌어** (*na ppijyeosseo*) means "I'm upset with you," but no one says it while truly angry — it is an invitation to be comforted. It usually comes with **흥** (*heung*) or **힝** (*hing*), the Korean "hmph," and a turned-away face. The right reply is **미안해, 화 풀어** (*mianhae, hwa pureo*), "sorry, don't be mad."

**Calling the other person.** **오빠아~** (*oppa-a*) and **자기야~** (*jagiya*), stretched out, are aegyo all by themselves. What those words mean without the voice is in [what does oppa mean in Korean](/what-does-oppa-mean-in-korean) and [what does jagiya mean](/what-does-jagiya-mean-in-korean).

**Fake hate.** **미워!** (*miwo*), "I hate you!", said with a pout, means the opposite. So does **몰라 몰라** (*molla molla*), "I don't know, I don't know," when someone is embarrassed after being complimented.

## Idol aegyo: 뿌잉뿌잉 and the 귀요미 song

Idols get asked for aegyo so often that a few routines became standard. **뿌잉뿌잉** (*ppuing ppuing*) is the sound you make while pressing two fists to your cheeks and twisting them. It has no meaning beyond "I am being cute right now."

The **귀요미 송** (*gwiyomi song*) is a counting routine every Korean fan recognizes: **일 더하기 일은 귀요미** (*il deohagi ireun gwiyomi*), "one plus one is a cutie," then up through six, with a hand gesture for each number. If an idol on a variety show starts counting on their fingers, this is what is coming.

When the host asks and the idol is shy, you will hear **아, 저 애교 없어요…** (*a, jeo aegyo eopseoyo*), "Oh, I don't have any aegyo…" — and then they do it anyway. More fandom vocabulary is in [K-pop words and what they mean in Korean](/kpop-words-meaning-in-korean).

## A short aegyo scene

A couple at a convenience store, late at night.

**Her:** 오빠아~ 나 배고파잉. — *oppa-a~ na baegopaing.* — "Oppa~ I'm hungry."
**Him:** 아까 먹었잖아. — *akka meogeotjana.* — "You just ate."
**Her:** 힝… 아이스크림 하나만 사 줘. — *hing… aiseukeurim hanaman sa jwo.* — "Hmph… just buy me one ice cream."
**Him:** 애교 부려도 안 돼. — *aegyo buryeodo an dwae.* — "Acting cute won't work."
**Her:** 나 삐졌어. — *na ppijyeosseo.* — "I'm sulking now."
**Him:** 알았어, 알았어. 하나만이야. — *arasseo, arasseo. hanamaniya.* — "Okay, okay. Just one."
**Her:** 고마워용! — *gomawoyong!* — "Thank youuu!"

Notice that every line is simple Korean. Without the voice, it is an ordinary conversation.

## Who you can (and can't) use aegyo with

Aegyo is for people you are close to: a partner, close friends, younger siblings, sometimes your parents. Idols use it with fans because the relationship is built to feel close.

It does not belong with your boss, a shop clerk, a teacher, or anyone you have just met. Using **고마워용** at a hotel front desk would be as odd as baby talk at a job interview in English. With strangers, stay in plain polite Korean — **감사합니다**, **주세요** — and keep the aegyo for people who will laugh with you.

## Mistakes learners make with aegyo

- **Thinking 애교 means "cute."** It is the act, not the adjective. "She's cute" is **귀여워요**; "she's doing aegyo" is **애교 부려요**.
- **Using aegyo endings in polite situations.** **-용** and **-잉** sit on top of casual speech. Stick them on a formal sentence and it sounds mocking.
- **Overdoing the baby talk.** One cute ending is charming; a whole conversation of **했쪄** is exhausting, even to Koreans. Dramas use it as a punchline for that reason.
- **Missing the fake anger.** **미워** and **삐졌어** said with a smile are affection. Answering them seriously kills the moment.
