The rain starts, and within an hour the group chat has already decided where everyone is going. The usual explanation is that rain on a window sounds like batter hitting hot oil, and whether or not anyone believes it, the craving is real and it is nationwide. Two sentences cover the entire evening: **비 오는 날엔 파전이죠** (*bi oneun naren pajeonijyo*), "Rainy days are for pajeon," which is how the plan gets made, and **제가 따라 드릴게요** (*jega ttara deurilgeyo*), "Let me pour for you," which is how the table works once you sit down.

> - Make the plan: **비 오는 날엔 파전이죠** (*bi oneun naren pajeonijyo*), "Rainy days are for pajeon."
> - Order the food: **해물파전 하나 주세요** (*haemul-pajeon hana juseyo*), "One seafood pajeon, please."
> - Order the drink: **막걸리 한 병이요** (*makgeolli han byeongiyo*), "A bottle of makgeolli."
> - Check the bottle: **흔들어서 따라요?** (*heundeureoseo ttarayo?*), "Do I shake it before pouring?"
> - Pour for the table: **제가 따라 드릴게요** (*jega ttara deurilgeyo*), "Let me pour for you."

## How does a rainy evening actually run?

The order barely changes from table to table, and the whole thing takes about as long as the rain does.

1. **비 오는 날엔 파전이죠** in the chat, which in Korean is less a suggestion than a statement everyone already agrees with.
2. **해물파전 하나 주세요** as soon as you sit, because the pan takes a while and nothing else arrives before it does.
3. **막걸리 한 병이요** in the same breath — ordering the food first and the drink second is the normal sequence, not a rule anyone will enforce.
4. **흔들어서 따라요?** when the bottle lands, since the good part has settled to the bottom and the top pours out thin and clear.
5. **제가 따라 드릴게요** before you fill your own glass, which is the part of the evening people notice.

| Moment | Say this |
|---|---|
| Making the plan | 비 오는 날엔 파전이죠 (*bi oneun naren pajeonijyo*) |
| Ordering the food | 해물파전 하나 주세요 (*haemul-pajeon hana juseyo*) |
| Ordering the drink | 막걸리 한 병이요 (*makgeolli han byeongiyo*) |
| The bottle arrives | 흔들어서 따라요? (*heundeureoseo ttarayo?*) |
| Filling the glasses | 제가 따라 드릴게요 (*jega ttara deurilgeyo*) |

## What do Korean speakers say back?

Answers come fast and short, and half of them are questions thrown straight back at you while somebody is already reaching for the bottle.

You: **비 오는 날엔 파전이죠** — *bi oneun naren pajeonijyo* — "Rainy days are for pajeon."  
Them: **그러니까요. 지금 갈까요?** — *geureonikkayo. jigeum galkkayo?* — "Right? Shall we go now?"  
You: **해물파전 하나랑 막걸리 한 병이요** — *haemul-pajeon hanarang makgeolli han byeongiyo* — "One seafood pajeon and a bottle of makgeolli."  
Them: **막걸리는 어떤 걸로 드려요?** — *makgeollineun eotteon geollo deuryeoyo?* — "Which makgeolli would you like?"  
You: **아무거나 괜찮아요** — *amugeona gwaenchanayo* — "Anything is fine."  
Them: **네, 잠시만요** — *ne, jamsimanyo* — "Sure, one moment."  
You: **흔들어서 따라요?** — *heundeureoseo ttarayo?* — "Do I shake it before pouring?"  
Them: **네, 살살 흔드셔야 돼요** — *ne, salsal heundeusyeoya dwaeyo* — "Yes, shake it gently."

**막걸리는 어떤 걸로 드려요?** catches people out, because most shops carry more than one and expect you to have a preference. **아무거나 괜찮아요** is a complete, polite answer and nobody thinks less of you for it. ## What usually goes wrong on a rainy-day table?

The first thing is the name of the food. **전** (*jeon*) is the whole family of pan-fried batter dishes, **부침개** (*buchimgae*) is the everyday word for the same idea, and **파전** (*pajeon*) is specifically the scallion one — the seafood version is **해물파전**, and the kimchi version is **김치전** (*kimchijeon*). Saying **전 주세요** is a little like asking for "fried food," so name the one you want.

The second is the bottle. Makgeolli separates as it stands, so the cloudy part everyone came for is sitting at the bottom while the top half looks like water. A gentle tilt or a slow shake mixes it — a hard shake gets you a fountain, since it is still fermenting in there. The third is pouring order: you fill other people's glasses before your own, and when someone older is pouring for you, you hold your glass with **두 손으로** (*du soneuro*), with two hands. That last habit is the one worth practising out loud, because it comes with a small phrase every time.
