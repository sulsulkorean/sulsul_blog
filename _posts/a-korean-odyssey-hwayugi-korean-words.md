The K-drama known in English as **A Korean Odyssey** is called **화유기** (*hwa-yu-gi*) in Korean. The title is a play on **서유기** (*seo-yu-gi*), the Korean name for the Chinese classic *Journey to the West*. Swap the first syllable 서 ("west") for 화 ("flower") and the "journey to the west" becomes a "journey of flowers." That one-letter change tells you what the drama is: the old monkey-king story, moved to modern Seoul, with a love story in the middle.

> **Quick answer**
> - **화유기** (*hwa-yu-gi*) — the Korean title of A Korean Odyssey
> - **서유기** (*seo-yu-gi*) — Journey to the West, the story it remakes
> - **손오공** (*son-o-gong*) — the monkey king, Sun Wukong in Chinese
> - **요괴** (*yo-goe*) — a demon or monster, the word the whole cast is built from

## One syllable away from 서유기

Old book titles in Korean often end in **기** (*gi*), "a record" or "a tale." **서유기** breaks down as 서 (west) + 유 (travel) + 기 (record): "the record of travelling west." **화유기** keeps 유 and 기 and changes only the front, so a Korean viewer reads the title and gets the joke at once.

You do not need the Chinese characters to use any of this. What matters is that 서유기 is a story nearly every Korean knows from childhood, through picture books and cartoons. When a Korean friend hears "A Korean Odyssey," they may pause. When they hear **화유기**, they will nod. Use the Korean title when you talk about the show:

- **화유기 봤어요?** (*hwa-yu-gi bwa-sseo-yo?*) — "Have you seen A Korean Odyssey?"
- **화유기 너무 재밌었어요.** (*hwa-yu-gi neo-mu jae-mi-sseo-sseo-yo*) — "A Korean Odyssey was so good."

## Who 손오공, 저팔계, 사오정, and 삼장 are

The main characters carry the Korean names of the *Journey to the West* cast, so the names are a ready-made vocabulary list.

- **손오공** (*son-o-gong*) — the monkey king. Korean kids also know this name from *Dragon Ball*, because Goku is called 손오공 in Korea. The drama plays on how cocky and quick the monkey king is.
- **저팔계** (*jeo-pal-gye*) — the pig spirit. In the drama he runs his own business and loves to eat and flirt, which fits the old character.
- **사오정** (*sa-o-jeong*) — the river spirit. Outside the drama, 사오정 is also a joke name: Koreans grew up on "사오정 jokes" about a character who mishears everything. If a friend misses your point three times, someone may laugh and say **사오정이야?** — "Are you 사오정?" Say it to friends only.
- **삼장** (*sam-jang*) — the monk the others must protect, often called **삼장법사** (*sam-jang-beop-sa*). In A Korean Odyssey, 삼장 is the heroine, 진선미 (*jin-seon-mi*), a woman who has been able to see ghosts since she was a child.

Then there is **우마왕** (*u-ma-wang*), the Bull Demon King. 마왕 (*ma-wang*) on its own means "demon king," and fans use it as a nickname for a charming villain.

## 요괴 and 귀신: two words for things that aren't human

These are the two words you will hear most, and they are not the same.

**요괴** (*yo-goe*) is a demon or monster: a creature with power, often old and tricky. 손오공, 저팔계, and 우마왕 are all 요괴. **귀신** (*gwi-sin*) is a ghost, the spirit of a dead person. 진선미 sees 귀신, and that is why her life is hard before the story even starts.

Both words come up in normal Korean, not only in fantasy shows:

- **귀신 봤어?** (*gwi-sin bwa-sseo?*) — "Did you see a ghost?" Said to a friend who looks pale or jumps at nothing.
- **무서워.** (*mu-seo-wo*) — "I'm scared." The most useful line in any horror or fantasy episode.
- **이 집 귀신 나올 것 같아.** (*i jip gwi-sin na-ol geot ga-ta*) — "This house looks like it has ghosts."

## 금강고 and 운명: the bracelet and the word for fate

In the old story, the monkey king wears a gold band on his head so the monk can control him. In 화유기, that band becomes a bracelet called **금강고** (*geum-gang-go*), and it ties 손오공 to 진선미: when she calls his name, he has to come.

That set-up gives fans two words worth knowing. **운명** (*un-myeong*) is fate or destiny, and K-dramas love it: **운명이야** (*un-myeong-i-ya*), "It's fate." **지켜 줄게** (*ji-kyeo jul-ge*), "I'll protect you," is the promise the whole bracelet stands for. Both sound natural between close friends, half-joking:

- **우리 만난 건 운명이야.** (*u-ri man-nan geon un-myeong-i-ya*) — "It was fate that we met." Said with a grin to a friend you met by chance.
- **걱정 마, 내가 지켜 줄게.** (*geok-jeong ma, nae-ga ji-kyeo jul-ge*) — "Don't worry, I've got you."

## Talking about the show with a Korean friend

Here is a short exchange you can actually have, using the words above:

You: **화유기 알아요?** — *hwa-yu-gi a-ra-yo?* — "Do you know A Korean Odyssey?"  
Friend: **네, 서유기 이야기죠?** — *ne, seo-yu-gi i-ya-gi-jyo?* — "Yes, it's the Journey to the West story, right?"  
You: **맞아요. 손오공이 진짜 멋있어요.** — *ma-ja-yo. son-o-gong-i jin-jja meo-si-sseo-yo.* — "Right. 손오공 is really cool."  
Friend: **저는 우마왕이 더 좋아요.** — *jeo-neun u-ma-wang-i deo jo-a-yo.* — "I like the Bull Demon King more."  
You: **좀 무서웠는데 재밌었어요.** — *jom mu-seo-wonn-neun-de jae-mi-sseo-sseo-yo.* — "It was a bit scary, but fun."

Notice that nobody says "A Korean Odyssey" or "Sun Wukong." In Korean, the Korean names do all the work.

## Where fans get these words wrong

**Using the English title.** "A Korean Odyssey" is an export title. Many Koreans will not connect it to the show. Say 화유기.

**Mixing up 요괴 and 귀신.** Calling 손오공 a 귀신 sounds like calling him a dead person's ghost. He is a 요괴. The heroine sees 귀신.

**Saying 사오정 to someone older.** The 사오정 joke is teasing. With a teacher, a boss, or anyone you speak 요 Korean with, keep it out.

**Saying 운명이야 too seriously.** With a friend and a smile, it is playful. Said straight to someone you just met, it sounds like a drama confession.

## Say the names before you need them

Read the names out loud, slowly, then at speed: 화유기, 서유기, 손오공, 저팔계, 사오정, 삼장. Then say the three lines that work outside the show: **귀신 봤어?**, **무서워**, **내가 지켜 줄게**. The next time you watch an episode, try to catch 요괴 and 운명 before the subtitle appears.
