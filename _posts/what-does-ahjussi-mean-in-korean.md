**아저씨** (*ajeossi*, usually spelled *ahjussi* by fans) means "mister." It is what you call a man who looks roughly forty or older and is not your relative — the taxi driver, the man fixing your door lock, the stranger on the bus whose bag is on your seat. Anyone can say it, male or female, young or old. Its partner word is **아줌마** (*ajumma*), "ma'am," for a married-looking or middle-aged woman. Both words are ordinary and both can sting, because they announce an age the listener may not feel. That is why a twenty-nine-year-old K-drama hero who gets called 아저씨 by a child clutches his chest like he has been shot.

> - The word: **아저씨** (*ajeossi*), "mister," a man of about your parents' age or a bit younger.
> - The partner: **아줌마** (*ajumma*), "ma'am," for a middle-aged woman.
> - The warmer choices: **사장님** (*sajangnim*) for a shop owner, **기사님** (*gisanim*) for a driver, **이모님** (*imonim*) for a woman serving food.
> - The very polite version: **아저씨** has no 님 form in daily use — you switch words instead.

## Where the word comes from, and what it still carries

아저씨 started as a family word. It once meant a father's younger brother or a close male relative of that generation — someone older, familiar, but not quite a parent. Over the last century it widened to cover any unrelated man of that rough age, and the family warmth mostly drained out of it. What is left is a neutral label: *a man, older than me, whose name I do not know*.

That neutrality is the point. Korean does not like bare "you," and it does not like calling a stranger nothing at all. 아저씨 fills the gap. A child shouting **아저씨!** at a delivery rider is not being rude; she is doing exactly what Korean asks her to do.

The trouble starts when the age guess is wrong. A man of thirty-two does not think of himself as an 아저씨 yet, and hearing it from a university student tells him the world has quietly moved him into a new box. Korean men joke about the first time it happened to them the way English speakers joke about their first grey hair.

## 아저씨 in K-dramas: the title that carries a story

Dramas lean on that sting, and they also lean on the opposite — a word that starts cold and turns tender.

The clearest example is the drama **나의 아저씨** (*naui ajeossi*), released in English as *My Mister*. The title literally means "my ahjussi." A young woman calls a tired middle-aged engineer 아저씨 the whole series long, and the word never changes. What changes is the **나의**, "my" — the stranger-label slowly becomes something she would protect. Korean viewers hear that whole arc in the title before episode one.

The film **아저씨** (*The Man from Nowhere*) does something similar: a girl calls her silent neighbour only 아저씨, because she does not know his name, and the title turns that anonymous word into the whole relationship.

Then there is the comic use. Watch any romantic comedy and you will hear a lead in his late twenties snap back:

Kid: **아저씨, 이거 떨어뜨렸어요.** — *ajeossi, igeo tteoreotteuryeosseoyo.* — "Mister, you dropped this."  
Him: **아저씨? 나 아저씨 아니야. 형이라고 해.** — *ajeossi? na ajeossi aniya. hyeongirago hae.* — "Mister? I'm not a mister. Call me hyung."

He is bargaining his way down the age ladder, from 아저씨 to **형**, the "older brother" word that sounds young and friendly. How 형 works on its own is explained in [what does hyung mean in Korean](/what-does-hyung-mean-in-korean).

## 아줌마: same job, sharper edge

**아줌마** does for women what 아저씨 does for men, but it cuts deeper. It does not only say "middle-aged"; it brings an image with it — the permed, practical, line-cutting auntie of a thousand comedy sketches. A woman in her thirties can hear it and feel she has been reduced to a stereotype. Calling a young-looking woman 아줌마 is one of the faster ways to sour a conversation in Korea.

The fuller form **아주머니** (*ajumeoni*) is softer and older-fashioned, and it is safer if you must use the word at all. But most Koreans skip both and pick a title that flatters:

- At a market stall or small restaurant: **이모님** (*imonim*), literally "auntie on mum's side." Warm, friendly, used constantly.
- At a shop she owns: **사장님** (*sajangnim*), "boss." Almost nobody is offended at being promoted.
- With a friend's mother: **어머님** (*eomeonim*), "mother," with respect.

## When you, a visitor, should say 아저씨

You will hear 아저씨 dozens of times a day. Saying it yourself is fine in a few places, and better avoided in others.

**Fine:** getting a stranger's attention when something is urgent — he dropped his wallet, your bag is caught in the subway door. **아저씨, 잠깐만요!** (*ajeossi, jamkkanmanyo!*), "Mister, wait a second!" is natural and nobody will mind.

**Better with a job title:** a taxi or bus driver is **기사님** (*gisanim*). Getting in and saying **기사님, 홍대역으로 가 주세요** (*gisanim, hongdaeyeogeuro ga juseyo*), "Driver, to Hongdae Station please," sounds polite and practised. 아저씨 works too, but it sounds like a local grandmother, not a guest.

**Better with 사장님:** anyone who owns or runs the place you are buying from. **사장님, 이거 얼마예요?** (*sajangnim, igeo eolmayeyo?*), "Boss, how much is this?" is the line market vendors expect.

**Not at all:** a man who might be under forty, a colleague, or anyone you will see again. Use his name with **씨** (*ssi*) or his job title. The full set of polite defaults for older people is laid out in [어르신, 할머니, 아버님: how to address older Koreans](/how-to-address-older-people-in-korean).

Here is a short exchange you could have on day one:

You: **저기요, 아저씨! 지갑 떨어졌어요!** — *jeogiyo, ajeossi! jigap tteoreojyeosseoyo!* — "Excuse me, mister! You dropped your wallet!"  
Him: **아이고, 고마워요, 학생.** — *aigo, gomawoyo, haksaeng.* — "Oh my, thank you, student."

Notice what he calls you back: **학생** (*haksaeng*), "student." Older Koreans use it for anyone who looks young, whether or not they are studying. It is the mirror of 아저씨 — a label by apparent age, with no offence meant.

## Mistakes learners make with 아저씨

**Spelling it into the wrong sound.** Fans write *ahjussi*, but the Korean is closer to *a-jeo-ssi*: the middle vowel is the "uh" in "fun," and the last syllable is a tense *ssi*, like a sharp hiss. Saying "ah-JOO-see" with a long *oo* is understood, but it marks you as having learned it from subtitles.

**Thinking 아저씨 is respectful because it is a title.** It is a label, not an honorific. To show respect you change the word — 기사님, 사장님, 선생님 — rather than decorate 아저씨.

**Using it on the man you are dating.** In dramas a heroine sometimes calls her much older love interest 아저씨 as a tease. In real life, that joke depends on a large age gap and a lot of affection. Without both, it just sounds like you think he is old. The love-life titles, from 오빠 to 자기야, are in [what does oppa mean in Korean](/what-does-oppa-mean-in-korean).

**Assuming the woman's version is equally safe.** It is not. If you remember only one rule from this post, make it this: when in doubt, say 이모님 or 사장님, never 아줌마.
