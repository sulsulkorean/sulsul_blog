**The English title you see on a K-drama is often not what the Korean title says.** *Crash Landing on You* is **사랑의 불시착**, "love's emergency landing." *When Life Gives You Tangerines* is **폭싹 속았수다**, Jeju dialect for "you worked so hard." *All of Us Are Dead* is **지금 우리 학교는**, "Our school right now is…" with the sentence cut off. Each Korean title is a small grammar lesson, and once you can read it, you hear a layer of the show that the English name drops.

> - **오징어 게임** (*o-jing-eo ge-im*) — "squid game." A real playground game.
> - **사랑의 불시착** (*sa-rang-e bul-si-chak*) — "love's emergency landing."
> - **폭싹 속았수다** (*pok-ssak so-gat-su-da*) — "you've worked so hard" (Jeju dialect).
> - **응답하라 1988** (*eung-da-pa-ra*) — "Answer, 1988!"
> - **지금 우리 학교는** (*ji-geum u-ri hak-gyo-neun*) — "Our school right now is…"

## Why the English title is different

A literal title often sounds flat or strange in English. "Strange Lawyer Woo Young-woo" would not sell a show the way *Extraordinary Attorney Woo* does. So the English name is a marketing choice, picked to fit the mood. The Korean title stays as the writer chose it, and that is where the wordplay, the dialect, and the grammar live.

That makes titles a good place to start reading Korean. They are short, you already know the story, and you see them every time you press play.

## Five titles built on one word

**오징어 게임 — Squid Game.** **오징어** means "squid," and **게임** is the English loanword "game." The name comes from a real children's game that Korean kids played in alleys and schoolyards decades ago, drawn in the dirt in a shape that looks like a squid. To a Korean viewer, the title sounds like childhood first, and that is the point of the horror.

**더 글로리 — The Glory.** Here Korean simply borrows English: **더** is "the," **글로리** is "glory." Many recent titles do this. Reading them in Hangul is easy practice for sounding out loanwords.

**킹덤 — Kingdom.** Same idea. **킹덤** is the English word written in Korean letters. The native word would be **왕국** (*wang-guk*), which you will hear inside the show.

**이태원 클라쓰 — Itaewon Class.** **이태원** is a real neighborhood in Seoul. **클라쓰** is "class," but spelled with a doubled **ㅆ** instead of the usual **클래스**. The tense *ss* makes it sound tougher, like saying "classsss" with attitude. It is a spelling Koreans use for style.

**스위트홈 — Sweet Home.** Another loanword, and an ironic one once you know the plot.

## The little word 의: "of," but said as 에

Three famous titles hang on one particle. **의** works like "of" or the English **'s**.

- **사랑의 불시착** — **사랑** (love) + **의** + **불시착** (an unplanned or emergency landing). "Love's emergency landing."
- **눈물의 여왕** — **눈물** (tears) + **의** + **여왕** (queen). "The queen of tears."
- **우리들의 블루스** — **우리들** (us, with the plural **들**) + **의** + **블루스** (blues). "Our blues."

Here is the part learners miss: when **의** means "of," Koreans almost always say it as **에** (*e*). So 사랑의 is *sa-rang-e*, and 눈물의 여왕 comes out as *nun-mu-re yeo-wang*. If you read every letter as written, *sa-rang-ui*, you will sound like you are reading a sign. Say it the drama way.

## Titles that are whole sentences

Some Korean titles are not names at all. They are lines someone might say.

**응답하라 1988 — Reply 1988.** **응답하다** means "to respond" or "to answer." The ending **-하라** is an old-fashioned, written command, the kind you see on slogans and radio calls. So the title is closer to "Respond, 1988!" or "1988, come in!" — as if calling to the past over a radio. In everyday speech you would never say 응답하라 to a friend. You would say **대답해** (*dae-dap-hae*), "answer me."

**지금 우리 학교는 — All of Us Are Dead.** **지금** is "now," **우리 학교** is "our school," and **는** marks the topic. Korean sentences put the verb last, so the title stops just before the verb. It reads like "Right now, our school is…" and leaves you to fill in the rest. That open ending is the hook.

**슬기로운 의사생활 — Hospital Playlist.** **슬기롭다** means "wise" or "sensible," and **의사생활** is "life as a doctor." Together: "A wise doctor's life." The English title points to the band the doctors play in. The Korean one points to how they live.

## Numbers and dialect

**스물다섯 스물하나 — Twenty-Five Twenty-One.** This title uses the native Korean numbers, not the Sino-Korean ones. **스물다섯** is 25 and **스물하나** is 21. Koreans count age with this system, so a title made of two ages has to use it. If you learn one thing from this show, learn that you say **저는 스물다섯 살이에요**, "I'm 25," and never *이십오 살*.

**폭싹 속았수다 — When Life Gives You Tangerines.** This one stumps even many Koreans from Seoul. It is Jeju Island dialect. **폭싹** means "completely" or "so much," and **속았수다** is the Jeju way of saying **수고하셨습니다**, "thank you for your hard work." So the title is a warm "you really went through so much" said to a whole life of effort. The English name borrows the Jeju tangerines instead.

**이상한 변호사 우영우 — Extraordinary Attorney Woo.** **이상한** usually means "strange" or "odd," and **변호사** is "lawyer." The Korean title owns a word that sounds a little unkind and makes it hers.

## Mistakes to avoid when you say these titles

**Reading 의 as *ui*.** Covered above, but it is the most common slip. In titles and in speech, "of" 의 is *e*.

**Taking 속았수다 as standard Korean.** In standard Korean, **속았다** means "I was fooled." A learner who knows that word may think the title means "totally tricked." In Jeju dialect it is the opposite of an insult: it is thanks.

**Using title grammar in conversation.** 응답하라 is a slogan ending. 지금 우리 학교는 is a fragment. They work as titles because they sound unusual. In real life, finish your sentences and use **-요**: **지금 학교에 있어요**, "I'm at school now."

**Expecting the subtitles to match the title.** English subtitles translate the line, not the wordplay. Turn on Korean subtitles (**한국어 자막**) once in a while and read the title card in Hangul. You will start catching the jokes the English hides.

## Say three titles out loud

Pick three: **사랑의 불시착**, **눈물의 여왕**, and **스물다섯 스물하나**. Say each one three times. Make 의 into *e* on the first two, and keep **스물** smooth and soft on the last one, with no hard stop between the numbers. When you can say them without looking, you can talk about your favorite shows with any Korean friend: **사랑의 불시착 봤어요?**, "Have you seen Crash Landing on You?"
