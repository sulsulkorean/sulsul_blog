The **주민센터** (*jumin senteo*, community service center) is the small neighbourhood office at the bottom of your district's paperwork chain — the place you go for an address registration, a certificate, a stamp, a form you did not know existed. The sign outside may say **행정복지센터** (*haengjeong bokji senteo*) instead; it is the same office. Two patterns from the SULSUL workbook get you from the door to the right counter. Pattern 47 — **(V)려고 / 하러** — is how you state your purpose in one breath the moment someone looks up: **전입신고 하러 왔어요** (*jeonipsingo hareo wasseoyo*), "I came to register my address." Pattern 41 — **(V)아/어야 해요** — is how you ask what the office needs from you: **뭘 가져와야 해요?** (*mwol gajyeowaya haeyo?*), "What do I have to bring?"

> - State why you came: **전입신고 하러 왔어요** (*jeonipsingo hareo wasseoyo*), "I came to register my address."
> - Ask which counter: **몇 번 창구로 가야 해요?** (*myeot beon changguro gaya haeyo?*), "Which window do I go to?"
> - Ask what to bring: **뭘 가져와야 해요?** (*mwol gajyeowaya haeyo?*), "What do I have to bring?"
> - Check where to write: **여기에 쓰면 돼요?** (*yeogie sseumyeon dwaeyo?*), "Do I write it here?"
> - Ask when it is ready: **언제 나와요?** (*eonje nawayo?*), "When will it be ready?"

## How do you open at the counter?

Nobody at a 주민센터 asks you an open question. They wait for you to say what you came for, and the whole visit is faster if that is the first thing out of your mouth. Pattern 47 turns any errand into one sentence: take the verb, add **-하러 왔어요** or **-(으)려고 왔어요**, and stop.

You: **안녕하세요. 전입신고 하러 왔어요.** — *annyeonghaseyo. jeonipsingo hareo wasseoyo.* — "Hello. I came to register my address."  
Them: **네, 번호표 뽑고 잠시만 기다려 주세요.** — *ne, beonhopyo ppopgo jamsiman gidaryeo juseyo.* — "Sure, take a number and wait a moment."  
You: **몇 번 창구로 가야 해요?** — *myeot beon changguro gaya haeyo?* — "Which window do I go to?"

**번호표** (*beonhopyo*) is the ticket from the machine by the door, and taking one before you queue is the whole etiquette of the room. Swap the verb and the same opening covers everything else: **등본 떼러 왔어요** (*deungbon tteoreo wasseoyo*), "I came to get a residence certificate," or **서류 발급받으려고 왔어요** (*seoryu balgeupbadeuryeogo wasseoyo*), "I came to have a document issued." The numbers on the ticket and the window are read with the Sino-Korean set that [Korean numbers for paying and counting in Korea](/korean-numbers-paying-counting-korea) lays out.

## How do you ask what the office needs from you?

This is Pattern 41, and it is the single most useful question in any Korean office. **-아/어야 해요** means "have to" — not "should," not "could," but the version that gets you a list you can act on. In speech most people say **-아/어야 돼요** (*-aya/eoya dwaeyo*) instead; the two are interchangeable, so use whichever comes out.

You: **뭘 가져와야 해요?** — *mwol gajyeowaya haeyo?* — "What do I have to bring?"  
Them: **신분증만 있으면 돼요.** — *sinbunjeungman isseumyeon dwaeyo.* — "Just your ID is enough."  
You: **외국인등록증도 괜찮아요?** — *oegugin deungnokjeungdo gwaenchanayo?* — "Is a residence card okay too?"

**신분증** (*sinbunjeung*) is the umbrella word for ID, and **외국인등록증** (*oegugin deungnokjeung*) is the residence card most foreign residents carry. If a form is put in front of you, two short questions handle it: **여기에 쓰면 돼요?** (*yeogie sseumyeon dwaeyo?*), "Do I write it here?" and **도장 필요해요?** (*dojang piryohaeyo?*), "Do I need a stamp?" And when the answer comes back faster than you can follow, **천천히 말해 주세요** (*cheoncheonhi malhae juseyo*) is the sentence to keep loaded — [what to say when Korean is too fast](/korean-phrases-when-korean-is-too-fast) is built around exactly that moment.

## What usually goes wrong at the 주민센터?

The first thing is arriving with the wrong thing, or nothing. A wasted trip is almost always a missing document, and one phone call prevents it: **거기 가기 전에 뭐가 필요해요?** (*geogi gagi jeone mwoga piryohaeyo?*), "What do I need before I come?" Ask the same question again at the counter before you leave, in the form that matters for next time — **다음에 또 와야 해요?** (*daeume tto waya haeyo?*), "Do I have to come again?"

The second is queuing at the wrong window. A 주민센터 splits its counters by errand, and the ticket machine usually asks you to choose before it prints. If the screen is only in Korean, ask the person at the information desk rather than guessing — the phrasing in [how to politely ask for help in Korean](/politely-ask-for-help-in-korean) works word for word here. There is also a **무인발급기** (*muin balgeupgi*), the self-service document kiosk in the lobby, and **이거 어떻게 써야 해요?** (*igeo eotteoke sseoya haeyo?*), "How do I use this?" gets someone to walk over.

The third is not knowing this office sits in the middle of a longer week. Most people reach it right after moving, with a lease in hand from [Korean phrases for talking to a real estate agent in Korea](/korean-phrases-real-estate-agent-korea) and a building office to visit next, which [Korean phrases at the apartment management office in Korea](/korean-phrases-apartment-management-office-korea) covers. A bank errand usually follows — [how to exchange currency at a bank in Korea](/exchange-currency-bank-korea) — and a phone line, which is [Korean phrases for getting a SIM card in Korea](/korean-phrases-sim-card-korea). On the way out, **감사합니다. 수고하세요.** (*gamsahamnida. sugohaseyo.*) — "Thank you, keep up the good work" — is what everyone says to the staff, and it is worth having ready.
