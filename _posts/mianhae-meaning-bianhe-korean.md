**미안해** (*mianhae*) means "I'm sorry" in casual Korean. It is the apology you hear in almost every K-drama: a boyfriend outside a door in the rain, a sister after a fight, a best friend who forgot a birthday. It is the 반말 (casual) form, so characters say it to people they are close to or younger than. And if you typed "bianhe" into a search bar because that is what it sounded like, your ears were not wrong. At the start of a word, Korean **ㅁ** (*m*) is often said with very little air through the nose, so to English ears **미안해** can come out sounding like *bianhae*. The word is the same. Only the spelling in your head was off.

> - **미안해** (*mianhae*) — "I'm sorry," casual. Friends, siblings, partners, anyone younger.
> - **미안해요** (*mianhaeyo*) — the same apology with polite **요**.
> - **미안하다** (*mianhada*) — the blunt, plain form older men and stubborn characters use.
> - **미안** (*mian*) — the shortest: "my bad," "sorry," usually for small things.

## Why mianhae sounds like "bianhe"

Korean has a quirk that trips up almost every new listener. When **ㅁ** or **ㄴ** starts a word, many speakers — especially in Seoul — close the nose early. The sound slides toward a soft **b** or **d**. That is why **미안해** gets heard as *bianhae*, **너무** (*neomu*, "too much") as *deomu*, and **뭐** (*mwo*, "what") as something close to *bwo*.

In the middle of a sentence, the nasal sound comes back. Listen to **정말 미안해** (*jeongmal mianhae*, "I'm really sorry") and you will hear a clear **m**, because **미** is no longer at the very start. So if a character says "bianhae" alone and "jeongmal mianhae" a minute later, they did not change words. You just heard the same **ㅁ** in two positions.

When you say it yourself, aim for a normal **m**. Nobody will mishear a clear *mianhae*. A hard English *b*, on the other hand, sounds like a different word.

## The four ways K-drama characters say sorry with 미안

The root is **미안** (*mian*), "feeling uneasy toward someone." The ending tells you who is talking to whom.

- **미안** (*mian*) — quick and light. Someone bumps your arm at a 포장마차, says **아, 미안**, and moves on.
- **미안해** (*mianhae*) — the standard casual apology. This is the line in the big emotional scenes.
- **미안해요** (*mianhaeyo*) — polite but still warm. A younger character to an older coworker they are close to, or a couple early in the relationship.
- **미안하다** (*mianhada*) — flat, plain style. A father who cannot say it softly, a rival who hates admitting it. It often sounds heavier than **미안해**, not lighter.

Notice what is missing: **죄송합니다** (*joesonghamnida*). That is the formal, deferential apology to a boss or a stranger. In a drama, a character switching from **미안해** to **죄송합니다** with someone close is a signal — they are putting up a wall.

## The lines around 미안해 that you hear in every fight scene

An apology in a drama is rarely one word. These are the lines that come before and after it.

- **정말 미안해** (*jeongmal mianhae*) / **진짜 미안해** (*jinjja mianhae*) — "I'm really sorry."
- **다 내 잘못이야** (*da nae jalmosiya*) — "It's all my fault."
- **미안하다고 했잖아** (*mianhadago haetjana*) — "I said I'm sorry!" Usually shouted, which tells you the fight is not over.
- **미안하면 다야?** (*mianhamyeon daya?*) — "You think sorry fixes everything?" The classic reply from the person who is still angry.
- **사과해** (*sagwahae*) — "Apologize." **사과** is the noun for an apology (and, yes, also the word for apple).
- **미안해하지 마** (*mianhaehaji ma*) — "Don't feel sorry." What a kind character says when the other person keeps apologizing.

## A short scene: the apology after the fight

Here is a typical exchange between two friends the morning after an argument.

> **A:** 어제 일… 미안해. 내가 말이 심했어.
> *eoje il… mianhae. naega mari simhaesseo.*
> "About yesterday… I'm sorry. I said too much."
>
> **B:** 미안하면 다야? 나 진짜 속상했어.
> *mianhamyeon daya? na jinjja soksanghaesseo.*
> "You think sorry is enough? I was really hurt."
>
> **A:** 알아. 다 내 잘못이야. 정말 미안해.
> *ara. da nae jalmosiya. jeongmal mianhae.*
> "I know. It's all my fault. I'm really sorry."
>
> **B:** …됐어. 이번만 봐 준다.
> *…dwaesseo. ibeonman bwa junda.*
> "…Fine. I'll let it go this once."

**됐어** (*dwaesseo*) is worth knowing on its own. Said softly, it means "it's okay, forget it." Said sharply, it means "enough, I don't want to hear it." The tone decides.

## Answering when someone says 미안해 to you

If a friend apologizes, these are the natural replies:

- **괜찮아** (*gwaenchana*) — "It's okay." The default.
- **아니야, 괜찮아** (*aniya, gwaenchana*) — "No, it's fine." Softer, brushes it off.
- **됐어, 신경 쓰지 마** (*dwaesseo, singyeong sseuji ma*) — "Forget it, don't worry about it."
- **나도 미안해** (*nado mianhae*) — "I'm sorry too." The line that usually ends the drama fight.

With someone you speak to politely, add **요**: **괜찮아요**, **저도 미안해요**.

## Mistakes learners make with mianhae

**Saying 미안해 to the wrong person.** It is casual. To a shop worker, a taxi driver or anyone older you do not know, it can sound rude. Use **죄송합니다** there, or at least **미안해요**.

**Spelling it "bianhe" and pronouncing it that way.** The search term is fine, but a strong *b* sounds wrong. Start with a light **m** and let the rest flow: *mi-an-hae*, three beats, no stop between **미** and **안**.

**Treating 미안하다 as softer.** In English, "sorry" is short and soft. **미안하다** is the plain style, and from a parent or boss it can sound gruff — not gentle, just less padded.

**Using 미안해 when you only need to get past someone.** On a crowded subway, Koreans say **잠시만요** (*jamsimanyo*), "just a moment," not an apology. Save **미안해** for when you actually did something.

## Say it until the ㅁ feels natural
