**Dakchyeo (닥쳐)** means "shut up" in Korean. It is a blunt, casual command, pronounced roughly *dak-cheo*, and it is the word K-drama characters snap when an argument has gone past the point of politeness. It is rude. Between very close friends it can be a joke, but said to anyone else it starts a fight. If you only want someone to be quiet, the everyday line is **조용히 해** (*jo-yong-hi hae*), or **조용히 해 주세요** (*jo-yong-hi hae ju-se-yo*) when you need to be polite.

> - **닥쳐.** — Shut up. (casual, rude)
> - **입 닥쳐.** — Shut your mouth. (stronger)
> - **입 다물어.** — Close your mouth. (casual, cold)
> - **시끄러워.** — You're being loud. / Quiet. (casual, annoyed)
> - **그만해.** — Stop it. (casual, firm)
> - **조용히 해 주세요.** — Please be quiet. (polite)

## Where 닥쳐 comes from

The dictionary form is **닥치다** (*dak-chi-da*), "to shut one's mouth." Add the casual command ending and 닥치 + 어 shrinks into 닥쳐, a form that gives an order to someone you are not bothering to respect.

Korean has a second, unrelated verb with the same spelling. **닥치다** can also mean "to be close at hand," as in **시험이 코앞에 닥쳤어요** ("the exam is right around the corner"). So if you see the word in a news headline about a coming storm, nobody is being told to shut up. The phrase **닥치는 대로** ("whatever comes along") uses this second meaning too.

## Why it sounds like "dak-cheo"

You will see this word spelled *dakchyeo*, *dakchyo*, and *dakcheo* in subtitles and fan posts. All three point to 닥쳐. The written syllable 쳐 is pronounced like 처 in normal speech, so there is no real "y" sound in the middle. The final ㄱ in 닥 is also clipped short, with no puff of air after it. Say *dak*, stop the sound in your throat, then land hard on *cheo*. In a drama, the second syllable usually gets all the force.

## How strong is it, really?

Think of these lines as a ladder. Each step leaves less room for the other person.

1. **조용히 해** — "Be quiet." Plain and common. A parent to a noisy kid, or a friend during a movie.
2. **시끄러워** — "You're loud." It describes the noise but works as a complaint, and it is often a brush-off between siblings.
3. **입 다물어** — "Close your mouth." Colder and more controlled, the line of someone who has stopped shouting because they are truly angry.
4. **닥쳐** — "Shut up." Openly rude, a clear attack on the person.
5. **입 닥쳐** — "Shut your mouth." Adding 입 ("mouth") points straight at the person's mouth and turns up the insult.

There is also a formal version that surprises learners: **닥치세요** (*dak-chi-se-yo*) and the stiffer **닥치십시오**. They carry polite endings on top of a rude word, and the clash is the point. Calm, formal contempt is often scarier on screen than yelling.

## 닥치고: "shut up and..."

The word also links to a second action as **닥치고** (*dak-chi-go*), "shut up and..." This is a pattern worth recognizing because you will hear it often:

- **닥치고 따라와.** — "Shut up and follow me."
- **닥치고 들어.** — "Shut up and listen."
- **닥치고 먹어.** — "Shut up and eat."

The last one can even be affectionate between friends, or a playful way to say a dish is so good there is nothing to discuss. Online, 닥치고 sometimes works as a strong recommendation in the same spirit, like telling friends to stop debating and just go. You may also see **ㄷㅊ** in chats, the first consonants of 닥쳐, usually teasing rather than furious.

## A short scene: siblings at the kitchen table

> **동생:** 언니, 어제 그 남자 또 만났지? 엄마한테 말한다?
> *You saw that guy again yesterday, didn't you? Should I tell Mom?*
>
> **언니:** 시끄러워. 밥이나 먹어.
> *Quiet. Just eat your food.*
>
> **동생:** 사진도 봤는데? 머리 스타일 진짜 이상하던데.
> *I saw his photo, too. His hair is really weird.*
>
> **언니:** 닥쳐.
> *Shut up.*
>
> **동생:** 와, 지금 나한테 닥치라고 했어?
> *Wow, did you just tell me to shut up?*
>
> **언니:** 그래, 했어. 닥치고 먹어.
> *Yes, I did. Shut up and eat.*

The sister starts with 시끄러워 and only reaches for 닥쳐 once her patience is gone. Notice 닥치라고 했어, too. That is how you report the word: "you told me to shut up." It is a useful way to recognize the verb when it does not look like 닥쳐 at all. Because these two are siblings, this reads as bickering rather than a break in the relationship. The exact same words to a coworker would be a very different scene. For more of the words around it, see [Korean bad words in K-dramas](/korean-slang-bad-words).

## What to say instead

Most of the time, what you actually want is quiet, not a fight. These lines get you there:

- **조용히 해 주세요.** — "Please be quiet." Clear and polite, fine with a stranger.
- **죄송한데, 조금만 조용히 해 주시겠어요?** — "Sorry, could you keep it down a little?" The softest choice, good for a neighbor or a stranger on a train.
- **잠깐만, 내 말 좀 들어 봐.** — "Hold on, listen to me for a second." For a friend who keeps talking over you.
- **그 얘기는 그만하자.** — "Let's stop talking about that." When the topic is the problem, not the volume.

## Mistakes learners make with 닥쳐

**Thinking 요 fixes it.** The polite ending does not make it polite. 닥치세요 to a shop clerk is a shock, not a courtesy, because the verb itself is the insult.

**Copying it as a joke too early.** Korean friends do say **닥쳐 ㅋㅋ** while laughing, but that comfort takes a long friendship. A newer friend, especially an older one, may not read it as a joke, and the age gap makes it land harder. For how levels of respect shape what you can say, see [how to be polite in Korean](/how-to-be-polite-in-korean).

**Mixing up 다물어 and 닥쳐.** 입 다물어 means closing your lips and is still harsh, but it is less of an open insult. Neither one belongs in a conversation with a teacher, a boss, or a stranger.

**Reaching for it when you mean "no way."** In English, "shut up!" can mean "I can't believe it!" 닥쳐 doesn't carry that meaning. For happy disbelief, say **진짜?** or **헐, 대박!** instead.

## Say the polite version out loud

You will probably never need 닥쳐 outside a drama, and recognizing it is enough. The line worth having ready is the soft one, because it comes up in real life and it is long enough to trip over. Say **죄송한데, 조금만 조용히 해 주시겠어요?** out loud three times, slowly at first, until it stops feeling like a mouthful.
