The K-drama lines fans remember are almost never long speeches. They are two or three words, said at the exact second the music swells: **가지 마** (*ga-ji ma*, "Don't go"), **다친 데 없어?** (*da-chin de eop-seo?*, "Are you hurt anywhere?"), **너 미쳤어?** (*neo mi-chyeo-sseo?*, "Are you crazy?"), **우리 헤어지자** (*u-ri he-eo-ji-ja*, "Let's break up"), and **나 안 취했어** (*na an chwi-hae-sseo*, "I'm not drunk"). If a drama has a wrist grab, a near-miss, a fight, a breakup, or a drunk walk home, one of these lines is in it.

> - **가지 마** (*ga-ji ma*) — "Don't go." The wrist-grab line.
> - **다친 데 없어?** (*da-chin de eop-seo?*) — "Are you hurt anywhere?" Right after the rescue.
> - **너 미쳤어?** (*neo mi-chyeo-sseo?*) — "Are you out of your mind?" The fight.
> - **우리 헤어지자** (*u-ri he-eo-ji-ja*) — "Let's break up." Episode 12, usually in the rain.
> - **나 안 취했어** (*na an chwi-hae-sseo*) — "I'm not drunk." Said by someone very drunk.

Every one of these is casual speech (반말), because the characters are close. That matters later, when you try them on real people. First, the scenes.

## The wrist grab: 가지 마

Someone turns to leave. A hand catches their wrist. The camera holds. Then: **가지 마.**

The grammar is tiny. **가다** means "to go," and **-지 마** means "don't." Put them together and you get "don't go." The same ending gives you a whole family of drama commands:

- **울지 마** (*ul-ji ma*) — "Don't cry."
- **걱정하지 마** (*geok-jeong-ha-ji ma*) — "Don't worry."
- **나 두고 가지 마** (*na du-go ga-ji ma*) — "Don't leave me behind."

The follow-up line is almost always **나 좀 봐** (*na jom bwa*), "Look at me." The **좀** softens it into something closer to "just look at me for a second."

In real life, 가지 마 shows up when a friend gets up to leave a party early. Say it with a laugh and a long vowel — **벌써 가? 가지 마~** ("Leaving already? Don't go~") — and it lands as affectionate, not dramatic.

## The near-miss: 다친 데 없어?

A car swerves, a shelf falls, a bike nearly clips the heroine. The male lead pulls her out of the way, and before anything else, he asks: **다친 데 없어?**

Word for word, it is "Is there a hurt place?" — **다친** (hurt) + **데** (place) + **없어?** (there isn't?). Korean asks "is there nowhere you got hurt?" where English asks "are you okay?" The usual reply is a shaky **응, 괜찮아** ("Yeah, I'm fine"), followed by the first long look between them.

This is one of the most useful lines on the page, because real people say it constantly. If a stranger trips on the subway stairs, the polite version works perfectly:

- **다친 데 없으세요?** (*da-chin de eop-seu-se-yo?*) — "Are you hurt anywhere?" Polite, for strangers and elders.

The other near-miss line is a shout: **조심해!** (*jo-sim-hae!*), "Watch out!" — or **조심하세요!** to someone you don't know.

## The fight: 너 미쳤어? and 정신 차려

When a character does something reckless, the friend or sibling explodes: **너 미쳤어?** — "Are you crazy?" If the lecture continues, it ends with **정신 차려** (*jeong-sin cha-ryeo*), literally "pick up your spirit," meaning "get a grip" or "snap out of it."

Here is where fans get surprised. Young Koreans use **미쳤다** (*mi-chyeot-da*, "it's crazy") as praise:

- **이 노래 미쳤다.** (*i no-rae mi-chyeot-da*) — "This song is insane." (good)
- **오늘 비주얼 미쳤다.** (*o-neul bi-ju-eol mi-chyeot-da*) — "The visuals today are unreal."

So **미쳤다** about a song, a stage, or a meal is a compliment. **너 미쳤어?** aimed at a person is a real accusation. The target decides the meaning.

## The villain's line: 감히 and the envelope

No K-drama trope is better known than the rich mother sliding an envelope across a café table. The line changes from show to show, but it is built from the same pieces:

- **감히** (*gam-hi*) — "How dare (you)." **감히 나한테?** means "How dare you, to me?"
- **이 돈 받고 우리 아들 앞에서 사라져.** (*i don bat-go u-ri a-deul a-pe-seo sa-ra-jyeo*) — "Take this money and disappear from my son's life."

Notice **우리 아들**, literally "our son." Koreans say **우리** ("our") for family members even when they mean "my," and in this scene it sounds extra possessive.

Between friends, **감히** is now mostly a joke. If your friend eats the last piece of chicken, **감히 내 치킨을?** ("How dare you, my chicken?") gets a laugh.

## The confession and the breakup: 좋아해 and 헤어지자

The confession is shorter than English viewers expect: **나 너 좋아해** (*na neo jo-a-hae*), "I like you." In dramas, **좋아해** usually comes first and **사랑해** waits for later episodes — "like" is the brave, scary step.

Then the other side of the story:

- **우리 헤어지자.** (*u-ri he-eo-ji-ja*) — "Let's break up."
- **우리 그만하자.** (*u-ri geu-man-ha-ja*) — "Let's stop (this)." Softer, and more common in real life.
- **기다려 줄게.** (*gi-da-ryeo jul-ge*) — "I'll wait for you." The answer that keeps the second half of the season going.

The **-자** ending means "let's," which is why the breakup line sounds so final: it is not a question. The **-줄게** ending means "I'll do it for you," and it appears in another classic, **내가 지켜 줄게** (*nae-ga ji-kyeo jul-ge*), "I'll protect you."

## The drunk walk home: 나 안 취했어

Two bottles of soju in, the heroine stands up, sways, and insists: **나 안 취했어.** "I'm not drunk." Ten seconds later she is on someone's back for the piggyback ride home, mumbling the line that starts the romance.

This one translates directly, and it works just as well as a joke in real life — exactly as unconvincing as it is on screen. Other lines from the same scene:

- **한 잔 더!** (*han jan deo!*) — "One more glass!"
- **업어 줄게.** (*eo-beo jul-ge*) — "I'll carry you on my back."

## Where drama lines go wrong in real life

**Using 반말 with strangers.** Every line above is casual. Said to a shop owner or someone older, **가지 마** or **조심해** can sound rude. Add **-세요** when in doubt: **가지 마세요**, **조심하세요**, **다친 데 없으세요?**

**Saying 너 too much.** Dramas say **너** ("you") constantly because the characters are close or furious. In everyday Korean, people drop "you" or use the person's name. Calling a new acquaintance **너** feels like a challenge.

**Copying the volume.** Drama fights are shouted for the camera. **정신 차려** said calmly between friends is a gentle tease; said at drama volume, it is a scene.

**Reading 미쳤어 one way.** As above, the same word is an insult or a compliment depending on what it is about.

## Saying the lines out loud

Recognizing **다친 데 없어?** when it hits the screen is easy. Saying **다친 데 없으세요?** to a real person who just tripped — fast, without stopping to build the sentence — is the hard half. Lines only stick when your mouth has said them a few times.
