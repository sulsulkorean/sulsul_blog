If you grew up speaking Tagalog, K-dramas already sound a little like home. **언니** (*eon-ni*) and **누나** (*nu-na*) are your **ate**. **오빠** (*o-ppa*) and **형** (*hyeong*) are your **kuya**. And the line a Korean mom says before anything else — **밥 먹었어?** (*bap meo-geo-sseo?*) — is word for word **"Kumain ka na?"**

> - **언니 / 누나** — ate — "older sister"
> - **오빠 / 형** — kuya — "older brother"
> - **밥 먹었어?** — Kumain ka na? — "Have you eaten?"
> - **진짜?** — Talaga? — "Really?"
> - **아이고** — Hay naku! — "Oh my, good grief"

Below: the family words, the Korean version of **po** and **opo**, the reactions you hear in every episode, the sweet lines from the romance scenes, and three sound-alikes that mean something completely different.

## Ate and kuya in Korean: four words instead of two

In Tagalog, **ate** and **kuya** work for anyone older — your real sister, your cousin, the girl at the sari-sari store. Korean does the same thing, but the word changes with **who is speaking**.

| Korean | Who says it | Tagalog | English |
|---|---|---|---|
| **언니** (*eon-ni*) | a girl, to an older woman | ate | older sister |
| **누나** (*nu-na*) | a boy, to an older woman | ate | older sister |
| **오빠** (*o-ppa*) | a girl, to an older man | kuya | older brother |
| **형** (*hyeong*) | a boy, to an older man | kuya | older brother |
| **동생** (*dong-saeng*) | anyone, about someone younger | bunso, nakababata | younger sibling |

So a female lead calls her best friend's older sister **언니**, and her little brother calls the same woman **누나**. Same person, two words.

Here is where Tagalog and Korean split: a Korean girl calls her **boyfriend** **오빠**. Calling your boyfriend **kuya** would be strange in Manila, but in Seoul **오빠** sounds sweet and a little playful. That is why fans scream it at concerts.

The parents are easy: **엄마** (*eom-ma*) is **nanay** or **mama**, and **아빠** (*a-ppa*) is **tatay** or **papa**. **할머니** (*hal-meo-ni*) is **lola**, and **할아버지** (*ha-ra-beo-ji*) is **lolo**.

## Po and opo in Korean: the little 요 at the end

Filipino kids learn early: add **po** when you talk to someone older, and say **opo** instead of **oo**. Korean has the same habit, and it lives at the end of the sentence.

- **고마워** (*go-ma-wo*) — "Salamat" — to a close friend
- **고마워요** (*go-ma-wo-yo*) — "Salamat po" — everyday polite
- **감사합니다** (*gam-sa-ham-ni-da*) — "Maraming salamat po" — formal, to a boss or elder

The yes-words line up too:

| Korean | Tagalog | When |
|---|---|---|
| **응** (*eung*) | oo | to friends |
| **네** (*ne*) | opo | to anyone older or a stranger |
| **아니** (*a-ni*) | hindi | no, casual |
| **아니요** (*a-ni-yo*) | hindi po | no, polite |

Think of **요** as Korean **po**. Leave it off with a stranger, and it lands the way a missing **po** does with your tita — not a crime, but people notice.

## Talaga? Grabe! The K-drama reactions in Tagalog

These are the words you hear even when the subtitles are off.

- **진짜?** (*jin-jja?*) — **Talaga?** — "Really?"
- **대박!** (*dae-bak!*) — **Grabe! Ang galing!** — "Amazing!"
- **헐** (*heol*) — **Ha?! Ano raw?** — "Wait, what?"
- **아이고** (*a-i-go*) — **Hay naku!** / **Aray!** — the sigh for a sore back, a big bill, or a silly friend
- **어떡해** (*eo-tteo-ke*) — **Paano na 'to?** — "What do I do now?"
- **괜찮아?** (*gwaen-chan-a?*) — **Okay ka lang?** — "Are you okay?"
- **괜찮아** (*gwaen-chan-a*) — **Okay lang** / **Ayos lang** — "It's fine."
- **화이팅!** (*hwa-i-ting!*) — **Laban! Kaya mo 'yan!** — "You've got this!"
- **잠깐만** (*jam-kkan-man*) — **Teka lang** — "Wait a second."

Notice how **괜찮아** does double duty, just like **okay lang**. Asked with a rising voice, it is a question. Said flat, it is the answer — even when the character is clearly not okay.

## Kumain ka na? How Koreans say "I care"

Filipino families show love with food before words. Korean families do too, and the lines match almost one to one.

![Friends greeting each other warmly on a Seoul street](/assets/blog/scenes/greeting.jpg)

- **밥 먹었어?** (*bap meo-geo-sseo?*) — **Kumain ka na?** — It means "I was thinking about you."
- **밥 먹었어요?** (*bap meo-geo-sseo-yo?*) — **Kumain ka na po?** — the polite version
- **많이 먹어** (*ma-ni meo-geo*) — **Kain ka pa** — "Eat more." Every grandma's line.
- **잘 먹겠습니다** (*jal meok-ge-sseum-ni-da*) — said before eating, a thank-you to whoever cooked
- **조심해** (*jo-sim-hae*) — **Ingat ka** — "Take care."
- **잘 자** (*jal ja*) — **Good night, tulog na** — "Sleep well."

A short exchange between two friends:

> **A:** 밥 먹었어? (*bap meo-geo-sseo?*) — Kumain ka na?
> **B:** 아니, 아직. 너는? (*a-ni, a-jik. neo-neun?*) — Hindi pa. Ikaw?
> **A:** 나도. 같이 먹자! (*na-do. ga-chi meok-ja!*) — Ako rin. Sabay na tayo!

## Mahal kita in Korean: the romance-scene lines

The love words in K-dramas come in steps, just like in a teleserye.

- **좋아해** (*jo-a-hae*) — **Gusto kita** — "I like you." The first confession.
- **사랑해** (*sa-rang-hae*) — **Mahal kita** — "I love you."
- **보고 싶어** (*bo-go si-peo*) — **Miss na kita** — "I miss you." Literally "I want to see you."
- **가지 마** (*ga-ji ma*) — **Huwag kang umalis** — "Don't go." The airport scene.
- **내 거야** (*nae geo-ya*) — **Akin ka** — "You're mine."
- **자기야** (*ja-gi-ya*) — **Mahal / Babe** — the pet name couples use

## Three sound-alikes that fool Filipino ears

**나** (*na*) means **"I"** or **"me."** In Tagalog, **na** means "already," so **나 가** (*na ga*) can sound like "aalis na." It actually means "I'm going." Close, but the **나** is the person, not the timing.

**아니** (*a-ni*) means **"no,"** but to a Tagalog ear it sounds like **ano** — "what?" When a Korean friend says **아니, 아니**, they are not asking a question. They are saying "no, no, that's not it."

**오빠 to strangers.** Filipinos call any older man **kuya** — the jeepney driver, the guard, the delivery guy. In Korean, a girl calling a stranger **오빠** sounds flirty. For a shop owner, use **사장님** (*sa-jang-nim*). To get someone's attention, say **저기요** (*jeo-gi-yo*) — the Korean "excuse me po."

## Same heart, different word order

Tagalog often puts the verb first: **"Kumain ako ng kanin."** Korean saves the verb for the very end:

> **나는 밥을 먹었어.** (*na-neun ba-beul meo-geo-sseo.*)
> I — rice — ate.

The trick is to hold the action until the last word. Small markers like **은/는** and **을/를** do the job Tagalog gives to **ang** and **ng** — they tell you who is doing what, so the order can move without the meaning breaking.
