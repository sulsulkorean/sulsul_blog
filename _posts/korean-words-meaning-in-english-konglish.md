Many Korean words you hear in K-dramas *are* English words — just not with the English meaning. **파이팅** (*paiting*, from "fighting") means **"you can do it!"**, **서비스** (*seobiseu*, "service") means **"free, on the house,"** and **썸** (*sseom*, "some") means **the stage before dating** when two people like each other but nobody has said it yet. Koreans call this borrowed-and-changed English **콩글리시** (*kongeulrisi*, Konglish). If you hear an English-sounding word in Korean and the subtitle doesn't match, you are probably hearing one of these.

> - **파이팅 / 화이팅** (*paiting / hwaiting*) — "go for it!", "you've got this"
> - **서비스** (*seobiseu*) — something extra, free of charge
> - **썸** (*sseom*) — the "almost dating" stage; **썸 타다** = to be in that stage
> - **스킨십** (*seukinsip*) — physical affection: holding hands, hugs, kisses
> - **핸드폰** (*haendeupon*) — cell phone
> - **노트북** (*noteubuk*) — laptop
> - **원샷** (*wonsyat*) — "bottoms up," finish the glass in one go
> - **아이쇼핑** (*aisyoping*) — window shopping
> - **셀카** (*selka*) — selfie (from "self camera")
> - **오버하다** (*obeohada*) — to overreact, to be too much

## Why English words change meaning in Korean

When a word moves into Korean, it keeps its sound but gets reshaped to fit Korean life. Three things usually happen.

**The word gets shortened.** "Self camera" became **셀카**. "Mental collapse" became **멘붕** (*menbung*, from 멘탈 붕괴). "Part-time job" went through German *Arbeit* and became **아르바이트**, then just **알바** (*alba*).

**The meaning slides sideways.** "Service" in a restaurant became the free extra the owner gives you, because that *is* good service. "Fighting" in a sports cheer became general encouragement for exams, job interviews, or a hard day.

**The word gets a Korean verb attached.** English nouns become Korean verbs with **하다** or **타다**: **오버하다** (to overdo it), **썸 타다** (to ride a "some"). Once a word takes Korean grammar, it is Korean.

That is why an English speaker can hear a word clearly and still miss the point. The sound is familiar. The job it does is new.

## The ones you hear in every K-drama

**파이팅!** — The most famous. A friend says it before your interview, a mother says it at the school gate, a whole office says it with fists raised before a big project. Spelled 파이팅 in official Korean, but you will see **화이팅** everywhere on screen and on signs. Reply with the same word, or with **고마워** (*gomawo*, thanks).

**썸** — A romance word. **우리 썸 타는 중이야** (*uri sseom taneun jungiya*) means "We're in the talking stage." The person you are in this stage with is your **썸남** (*sseomnam*, "some man") or **썸녀** (*sseomnyeo*, "some woman"). Half of every romance drama happens in 썸.

**스킨십** — Not skincare. It means touch between people. A character might say **스킨십이 너무 빨라** (*seukinsibi neomu ppalla*), "The physical stuff is moving too fast." It is also used for warm touch between family or friends, like a parent who hugs a lot.

**원샷!** — At a company dinner or a celebration, someone raises a glass and shouts this. It means drink it all at once. In English it sounds like "one shot," but nobody is counting shots.

**오버하지 마** (*obeohaji ma*) — "Don't overreact" or "Don't be so dramatic." Very common between siblings and best friends.

## Konglish you will need on a trip

These come up the moment you land.

**서비스예요** (*seobiseu-yeyo*) — "This one's on the house." At a fried chicken place or a small restaurant, the owner may put down an extra drink or side dish and say this. The polite answer is **감사합니다!** (*gamsahamnida*). You can even ask with a smile at a market: **서비스 없어요?** (*seobiseu eopseoyo?*), "No little extra?"

**핸드폰** — "Hand phone." If you lose your phone, this is the word: **핸드폰을 잃어버렸어요** (*haendeuponeul ireobeoryeosseoyo*), "I lost my phone." Younger people also say **폰** or **휴대폰**.

**노트북** — A laptop, not a paper notebook. Ask a cafe **노트북 써도 돼요?** (*noteubuk sseodo dwaeyo?*), "Can I use my laptop here?" A paper notebook is **공책** (*gongchaek*).

**콘센트** (*konsenteu*) — A power outlet. The word came into Korean through Japanese, and English speakers rarely guess it. **콘센트 있어요?** — "Is there an outlet?" Say "outlet" and most staff won't follow.

**아이쇼핑** — "Eye shopping," window shopping. If a shop clerk asks if you need help, **그냥 아이쇼핑이에요** (*geunyang aisyopingieyo*) means "Just looking."

**더치페이** (*deochipei*) — "Dutch pay," splitting the bill. **더치페이 할까요?** — "Shall we split it?"

## A short scene: two friends at a chicken restaurant

Mina: **오늘 면접 어땠어?** — *oneul myeonjeop eottaesseo?* — "How was your interview today?"
Jun: **몰라, 멘붕이었어.** — *molla, menbung-ieosseo.* — "I don't know, I totally panicked."
Mina: **오버하지 마. 잘했을 거야. 파이팅!** — *obeohaji ma. jalhaesseul geoya. paiting!* — "Don't be dramatic. I'm sure you did well. You've got this!"
Owner: **이거 서비스예요.** — *igeo seobiseu-yeyo.* — "This one's on the house."
Both: **우와, 감사합니다!** — *uwa, gamsahamnida!* — "Wow, thank you!"
Mina: **자, 원샷!** — *ja, wonsyat!* — "Okay, bottoms up!"

Five Konglish words in six lines, and not one of them means what an English dictionary says.

## How to say them so Koreans understand

Konglish words follow Korean pronunciation, not English. If you say them in a native English accent, Koreans often won't recognize them. Three rules help:

1. **Add the extra vowel.** Korean doesn't end most syllables with hard consonants the way English does, so words get an **으** (*eu*) sound: "service" becomes **서-비-스** (*seo-bi-seu*), three even beats.
2. **No F, no V.** **F** becomes **ㅍ** (p) or **ㅎ** (h): fighting → **파이팅** or **화이팅**. **V** becomes **ㅂ** (b): service → 서**비**스.
3. **Keep it flat.** English stresses one syllable. Korean gives each syllable about the same weight. Say **노-트-북**, not "NOTE-book."

## Mistakes English speakers make

1. **Using the English meaning.** Telling a Korean coworker "I need a notebook" when you mean paper will get you a laptop discussion.
2. **Saying "fighting" with an English F.** It sounds like you are talking about an actual fight. Use the Korean sound: **화이팅**.
3. **Using 스킨십 for a hug from a stranger.** It describes close relationships. For strangers, there is no natural use.
4. **Assuming every English-sounding word is Konglish.** **커피**, **버스**, **택시** mean just what they mean in English. The tricky ones are the ones where the meaning moved.
