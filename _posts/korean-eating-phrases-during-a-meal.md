The Korean eating phrases you will use most are not the famous before-and-after lines — they are the small reactions you say while the food is still on the table. **맛있어요** (*masisseoyo*) means "It's delicious." **너무 매워요** (*neomu maewoyo*) means "It's too spicy." **배불러요** (*baebulleoyo*) means "I'm full." And when you want more of something, add **더 주세요** (*deo juseyo*), "more, please," to the thing you want: **김치 좀 더 주세요**, "a little more kimchi, please." Those four lines, plus a couple of answers to what Koreans say *to you* while you eat, cover almost every meal in Korea.

> **Korean eating phrases, the short version**
> - It's good: **맛있어요** — *masisseoyo*
> - It's really good: **진짜 맛있어요** — *jinjja masisseoyo*
> - Too spicy: **너무 매워요** — *neomu maewoyo*
> - More, please: **(N) 좀 더 주세요** — *jom deo juseyo*
> - I'm full: **배불러요** — *baebulleoyo*

## Why one adjective ending does most of the work

In the SULSUL workbook, Pattern 11 — **(A)아/어요** — is the pattern for "describing your mood, the weather, the taste of food." Korean adjectives behave like verbs, so you do not need "is." 맛있다, "to be tasty," becomes **맛있어요**, "(it's) tasty." 맵다, "to be spicy," becomes **매워요**. That single ending turns any taste word into a full, polite sentence.

That is why eating Korean feels easy once it clicks. You are not building sentences; you are picking one adjective and saying it with 요 on the end. Here are the taste words you will reach for most:

| Korean | Say it | Meaning | When you say it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 맛있어요 | *masisseoyo* | It's delicious | First bite, every time |
| 매워요 | *maewoyo* | It's spicy | 떡볶이, 김치찌개, 불닭 |
| 짜요 | *jjayo* | It's salty | Stews that have boiled down |
| 달아요 | *darayo* | It's sweet | 불고기, 양념치킨, desserts |
| 싱거워요 | *singgeowoyo* | It's bland, needs salt | Soups like 설렁탕 before you season them |
| 뜨거워요 | *tteugeowoyo* | It's hot (temperature) | A stone bowl straight off the fire |
| 시원해요 | *siwonhaeyo* | It's refreshing | Hot soup, oddly — more below |

Two of these trip people up. **매워요** is spicy-hot, but **뜨거워요** is temperature-hot; mixing them up makes a server bring water when you wanted to wait for the bowl to cool. And **시원해요** literally means "cool," yet Koreans say it about a steaming bowl of 해장국. It means the broth feels clean and satisfying, the way a cold drink feels on a hot day.

## Turning the reaction up or down

A plain 맛있어요 is fine, but Koreans rarely leave it plain. Put a strength word in front:

- **진짜 맛있어요** (*jinjja masisseoyo*) — "It's really delicious." The most natural compliment at any table.
- **너무 맛있어요** (*neomu masisseoyo*) — "It's so delicious." 너무 technically means "too much," but in speech it is just strong praise.
- **좀 매워요** (*jom maewoyo*) — "It's a bit spicy."
- **너무 매워요** (*neomu maewoyo*) — "It's too spicy." With a taste you don't want, 너무 goes back to meaning "too."
- **하나도 안 매워요** (*hanado an maewoyo*) — "It's not spicy at all."

If you only remember one, make it 진짜 맛있어요. Said with a small nod, it is the line that makes a restaurant owner smile — and Pattern 17's reaction words, **대박** (*daebak*), "amazing," work the same way between friends.

## Asking for more without stopping the meal

Korean meals come with refillable side dishes, and you are expected to ask. This is Pattern 01, **(N) 주세요**, with **더** (*deo*), "more," slipped in before it. Add **좀** (*jom*), "a little," and it becomes softer and more natural:

- **김치 좀 더 주세요** (*gimchi jom deo juseyo*) — "A little more kimchi, please."
- **반찬 좀 더 주세요** (*banchan jom deo juseyo*) — "More side dishes, please."
- **물 좀 주세요** (*mul jom juseyo*) — "Some water, please."
- **공기밥 하나 더 주세요** (*gonggi-bap hana deo juseyo*) — "One more bowl of rice, please."
- **앞접시 주세요** (*apjeopsi juseyo*) — "A small plate for sharing, please."

Many places have a self-serve station marked **셀프** (*selpeu*) for side dishes and water. If you see that sign, the refill is yours to fetch. Getting the server's attention in the first place is covered in [how to call a waiter in Korean](/how-to-call-a-waiter-in-korean).

## Asking them to do something to the food

Pattern 23, **(V)아/어 주세요**, "please do V for me," handles anything you need done at the table. It is the same 주세요, now after a verb:

- **잘라 주세요** (*jalla juseyo*) — "Please cut it." Servers cut 냉면 noodles and grilled meat with scissors.
- **데워 주세요** (*dewo juseyo*) — "Please heat it up."
- **덜 맵게 해 주세요** (*deol maepge hae juseyo*) — "Please make it less spicy." Best said when you order, not after.
- **불 좀 줄여 주세요** (*bul jom jullyeo juseyo*) — "Please turn the heat down." Useful at a table with its own burner.
- **포장해 주세요** (*pojanghae juseyo*) — "Please pack it to go." The full routine is in [to-go box in Korean](/to-go-box-in-korean-leftovers).

## What Koreans say to you while you eat — and how to answer

Half of eating Korean is answering. Hosts, older coworkers, and restaurant owners will throw these at you:

> **Host:** 많이 드세요.
> **You:** 네, 감사합니다. 잘 먹을게요.
>
> **Host:** 입에 맞아요?
> **You:** 네, 진짜 맛있어요.
>
> **Host:** 더 드세요. 많이 남았어요.
> **You:** 아니에요, 배불러요. 너무 많이 먹었어요.

**많이 드세요** (*mani deuseyo*) means "Eat a lot" — it is hospitality, not an instruction. **입에 맞아요?** (*ibe majayo?*) means "Does it suit your taste?" The answer they hope for is 네, followed by 맛있어요. And when the food keeps coming, **배불러요** is the polite way out. Add **너무 많이 먹었어요** (*neomu mani meogeosseoyo*), "I ate too much," and nobody will push a fourth helping on you.

If you can't eat something at all, keep it light and true: **매운 거 잘 못 먹어요** (*maeun geo jal mot meogeoyo*), "I can't handle spicy food very well." That uses Pattern 07, **못**, and it lands as an honest limit rather than a complaint. For real allergies, use the stronger lines in [Korean phrases for food allergies and diet restrictions](/korean-phrases-food-allergies-korea).

## Mistakes people make at the Korean table

**Saying 매워요 for a hot bowl.** If the soup burns your tongue but has no chili, it is **뜨거워요**. Mixing them up gets you the wrong fix.

**Saying 배고파요 when you mean full.** **배고파요** (*baegopayo*) is "I'm hungry"; **배불러요** is "I'm full." They both start with 배, "stomach," so they are easy to swap when you are nervous. Hungry before, full after.

**Complaining with 맛없어요.** **맛없어요** (*madeopseoyo*), "It's not tasty," is grammatically perfect and socially rough. If a dish is not for you, say **좀 짜요** or **저한테 좀 매워요** (*jeohante jom maewoyo*), "It's a bit spicy for me." Describe the taste; don't judge the cook.

**Staying silent when the host asks.** A quiet nod reads as "not great." Even a short **맛있어요!** is better than nothing.

**Forgetting 좀.** **김치 더 주세요** is correct, but **김치 좀 더 주세요** sounds warmer. That one syllable is what makes requests sound native.

## The meal, start to finish

You open with **잘 먹겠습니다**, react through the meal with **맛있어요** and **좀 매워요**, ask for refills with **좀 더 주세요**, wave off seconds with **배불러요**, and close with **잘 먹었습니다**. ## Say it with your mouth full of practice, not food

맛있어요 looks simple, but the 있 in the middle comes out as a double ㅆ sound, and 배불러요 has two ㄹ sounds that run together. Under the pressure of a host watching you take the first bite, those are exactly the syllables that blur. Saying them out loud a few times before dinner is what makes them come out clean at the table.
