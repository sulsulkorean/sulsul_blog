The plain Korean word for friend is **친구** (*chin-gu*), and it works everywhere. But Korean slang doesn't stop at one word. Young Koreans sort their friends into boxes, and each box has its own short term: **찐친** (*jjin-chin*) is a real, close friend, **베프** (*be-peu*) is a best friend, **절친** (*jeol-chin*) is a very close friend, **남사친** (*nam-sa-chin*) and **여사친** (*yeo-sa-chin*) are a guy friend or girl friend with no romance, and **불알친구** (*bu-ral-chin-gu*) is a friend you've known since you were small. K-drama subtitles flatten almost all of these into "my friend" or "my best friend," so the label a character picks, and the fight it starts, often gets lost.

> - **친구** — friend (the neutral word for anyone your age)
> - **찐친** — a real friend, the one who sees you at your worst
> - **베프** — best friend (from English "best friend")
> - **절친** — a very close friend (a little more grown-up than 베프)
> - **남사친 / 여사친** — a guy friend / girl friend, just friends
> - **불알친구** — a friend since early childhood (blunt, guys mostly)
> - **동창** — an old classmate, close or not

## Why one word for friend is not enough in Korean

Start with the catch: in Korean, **친구** usually means someone your own age. A person two years older who you're close to isn't quite your 친구. They're your **언니**, **오빠**, **형**, or **누나**, and calling them 친구 can sound like you forgot the age gap. Our posts on [what noona means](/what-does-noona-mean-in-korean) and [what oppa means](/what-does-oppa-mean-in-korean) explain that side of it.

So when Koreans want to say how close a same-age friend is, they don't add "really" or "best" in front of 친구 as often as English does. They reach for a new word. Most of these words are clipped, the way Korean slang loves to be: two syllables taken from a longer phrase and glued together. Once you see how they're built, you can read new ones on your own.

## 찐친: the friend who's seen everything

**찐** (*jjin*) comes from **진짜**, "real," pronounced with a harder sound for emphasis. **찐친** is the real friend, as opposed to the people you just know. It's the friend you can show up to in sweatpants, who sends you an ugly photo of yourself with no apology.

- **우리 찐친이야.** — We're real friends.
- **찐친만 아는 모습** — the side of me only real friends know

That second line shows up all the time under celebrity photos and variety-show clips. When two idols are caught teasing each other with zero manners, fans comment **찐친 바이브** ("real-friend vibe"). The joke is that real closeness in Korea looks a little rude from the outside.

## 베프 and 절친: the best friend words

**베프** is "best friend," cut down to its first two sounds: 베스트 프렌드 → 베프. It's casual, a bit young, and very common in texts. **절친** comes from **절친한 친구**, "an extremely close friend," and sounds slightly more grown-up. A thirty-something office worker in a drama is more likely to say 절친 than 베프.

- **얘가 제 베프예요.** — This is my best friend.
- **고등학교 때부터 절친이에요.** — We've been close friends since high school.

You'll also see **베프 인증** ("best friend proof"), a post showing matching items or an old photo together. On social media, being someone's 베프 is something you prove.

## 남사친 and 여사친: just friends, or are they?

These two are where the drama lives. **남사친** comes from **남자 사람 친구**, literally "male person friend," and **여사친** from **여자 사람 친구**, "female person friend." The extra word 사람 is the whole point. **남자친구** already means boyfriend, so adding 사람 says "a male human who is my friend, nothing more."

- **그냥 남사친이야.** — He's just a guy friend.
- **여사친이랑 단둘이 영화 보는 거 괜찮아?** — Is it okay to see a movie alone with a girl friend?

That second question is a real argument Korean couples have, and K-dramas build whole episodes on it. A romantic comedy where two childhood friends slowly fall for each other is often described as **남사친 여사친 로맨스**. When one character insists **우리 그냥 친구야** ("we're just friends"), the audience knows exactly where the story is going.

## 불알친구, 동창, and the friends from the past

**불알친구** is the friend you've known since you were tiny, usually used by men about other men. It's built on a crude word, so it's funny and warm among guys but not something to say to your friend's parents. A softer version is **소꿉친구** (*so-kkup-chin-gu*), a friend you played house with as a kid, and that's the word you'll hear for the childhood-friends couple in a romance drama.

**동창** (*dong-chang*) is a classmate from the same school, close or not. **동창회** is a class reunion, a classic K-drama setting for awkward run-ins with a first love. **동갑** (*dong-gap*) means "same age," and you'll hear it right before two strangers decide to drop formal speech: **어, 우리 동갑이네! 말 놓을까?** — "Oh, we're the same age! Want to talk casually?"

## A scene: three labels in one argument

Here's how these words collide in a typical drama fight between a couple and the friend in the middle.

> **지은:** 어제 누구랑 있었어?
> *Who were you with yesterday?*
>
> **민호:** 준서랑. 내 불알친구 알잖아.
> *With Junseo. You know, my friend since forever.*
>
> **지은:** 사진에 여자도 있던데?
> *There was a girl in the photo, though.*
>
> **민호:** 아, 수아? 그냥 여사친이야. 동창이고.
> *Oh, Sua? She's just a girl friend. We went to school together.*
>
> **지은:** 그냥 여사친이 새벽 두 시까지 있어?
> *A "just friend" stays until two in the morning?*
>
> **민호:** 진짜 찐친이라니까. 너도 남사친 있잖아!
> *I'm telling you, she's a real friend. You have guy friends too!*

Every label in that scene is doing work. 불알친구 says "harmless, known forever." 여사친 tries to say "nothing going on." And 찐친 is the last card Minho plays to make Sua sound safe. The English subtitle says "friend" five times. The Korean says five different things.

## Mistakes learners make with friend words

- **Calling an older person 친구.** If someone is clearly older, 친구 can sound like you're ignoring the age gap. Ask their age first, or use 언니, 오빠, 형, or 누나 once you're close.
- **Using 불알친구 in mixed company.** It's affectionate, but the first word is crude. Use **소꿉친구** or **어릴 때부터 친구** ("friends since we were kids") instead.
- **Saying 남자친구 for a guy friend.** 남자친구 means boyfriend. If you mean a friend who's a guy, say **남사친** casually or **남자 사람 친구** if you want to be crystal clear.
- **Saying 베프 at a job interview.** These are casual words. In formal settings, **친한 친구** ("close friend") is the safe choice.

## The phrases you'll actually use

You'll need a few lines around these words more than the words alone.

- **우리 친해요.** — We're close.
- **친한 친구예요.** — We're close friends. (works anywhere)
- **친구 할래요?** — Want to be friends?
- **말 편하게 해도 돼요.** — You can talk to me casually.
- **너 내 찐친 맞지?** — You're my real friend, right? (playful, said before asking a favor)

That last one is a classic setup line. Anyone who hears it knows a favor is coming next. ## Say them like you mean them

Read the scene above out loud, both parts. Notice how **그냥 여사친이야** comes out fast and flat, like it's no big deal, while **진짜 찐친이라니까** leans hard on the first syllable of 진짜 and 찐. Then try introducing a friend in two ways: **얘 내 베프야** with a grin to a friend, and **제 친한 친구예요** in a polite voice to someone older. The next time a drama character says "he's just a friend," you'll hear which word they really chose.
