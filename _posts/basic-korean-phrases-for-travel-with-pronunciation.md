If you only have room for a few Korean lines before a trip, take these six: **안녕하세요** (*an-nyeong-ha-se-yo*, "hello"), **감사합니다** (*gam-sa-ham-ni-da*, "thank you"), **이거 주세요** (*i-geo ju-se-yo*, "this one, please"), **얼마예요?** (*eol-ma-ye-yo?*, "how much is it?"), **화장실 어디예요?** (*hwa-jang-sil eo-di-ye-yo?*, "where is the restroom?"), and **있어요?** (*i-sseo-yo?*, "do you have it?"). Notice that two of them are already spelled one way and said another. 감사합니다 is written with a ㅂ but spoken with an *m*, and 있어요 is written 있-어-요 but comes out as *i-sseo-yo*. Get those sound shifts right and a Korean shopkeeper hears a sentence, not a string of syllables.

> - Hello: **안녕하세요** — *an-nyeong-ha-se-yo*
> - Thank you: **감사합니다** — sounds like *gam-sa-ham-ni-da*
> - This one, please: **이거 주세요** — *i-geo ju-se-yo*
> - How much?: **얼마예요?** — *eol-ma-ye-yo?* (rise at the end)
> - Where is ___?: **___ 어디예요?** — *eo-di-ye-yo?*
> - Do you have ___?: **___ 있어요?** — sounds like *i-sseo-yo?*
> - Sorry / excuse me: **죄송합니다** — sounds like *joe-song-ham-ni-da*

## Why the spelling and the sound don't match

Hangul is very close to phonetic, but Korean speakers smooth the edges between syllable blocks. Two rules cover almost every surprise in a beginner's travel list.

The first is the slide. When a block ends in a consonant and the next block starts with the silent ㅇ, the consonant slides forward. 있어요 becomes [이써요], 맛있어요 ("it's delicious") becomes [마시써요], and 괜찮아요 ("it's okay") becomes [괜차나요] because the ㅎ disappears on the way. Read them block by block and you will sound like a robot reading a sign.

The second is the nose. A ㅂ sitting right before ㄴ turns into an *m*. That is why every formal "-합니다" ending is pronounced *-ham-ni-da*. 감사합니다, 죄송합니다, 실례합니다 — all three end in the same *-ham-ni-da*. Romanizations like *gamsahabnida* are technically spelling the letters, not the sound, so if a phrasebook gives you that version, say the *m*.

## The four patterns behind almost every travel phrase

Most of what a tourist needs to say fits into four frames. Learn the frame once and swap the noun.

**[Noun] 주세요** — "please give me." This is the workhorse for cafes, restaurants, and shops: **물 주세요** (*mul ju-se-yo*, "water, please"), **영수증 주세요** (*yeong-su-jeung ju-se-yo*, "receipt, please"), **봉투 주세요** (*bong-tu ju-se-yo*, "a bag, please"). Put **좀** (*jom*) in the middle — **물 좀 주세요** — and it softens from a request into a polite ask.

**[Noun] 있어요?** — "do you have…?" Before you hunt through a store, ask: **우산 있어요?** (*u-san i-sseo-yo?*, "do you have umbrellas?"), **카드 돼요?** is the cousin for "do you take cards?" The answer is either 있어요 or **없어요** (*eop-seo-yo*, "we don't"), which is also said with a slide: [업써요].

**[Place] 어디예요?** — "where is…?" **역 어디예요?** (*yeok eo-di-ye-yo?*, "where is the station?"), **출구 어디예요?** (*chul-gu*, "exit"). If the word ends in a consonant, some people say 어디 있어요? instead; both land fine.

**[Verb] 고 싶어요** — "I want to…" **명동 가고 싶어요** (*myeong-dong ga-go si-peo-yo*, "I want to go to Myeongdong"). Point at a map while you say it and a stranger will walk you halfway there.

## A short scene: one street, four phrases

Here is how the same handful of lines carries you through ten minutes in Seoul.

> **You:** 안녕하세요. 이거 있어요?
> *an-nyeong-ha-se-yo. i-geo i-sseo-yo?* — Hello. Do you have this? (showing a photo)
>
> **Clerk:** 네, 있어요. 이쪽이에요.
> *ne, i-sseo-yo. i-jjo-gi-e-yo.* — Yes, we do. It's over here.
>
> **You:** 얼마예요?
> *eol-ma-ye-yo?* — How much is it?
>
> **Clerk:** (points to the price on the register)
>
> **You:** 이거 주세요. 봉투도 주세요.
> *i-geo ju-se-yo. bong-tu-do ju-se-yo.* — This one, please. A bag too, please.
>
> **You:** 감사합니다! 아, 지하철역 어디예요?
> *gam-sa-ham-ni-da! a, ji-ha-cheol-lyeok eo-di-ye-yo?* — Thank you! Oh, where's the subway station?

Look at 지하철역: the spelling says *jihacheol-yeok*, but the sound picks up an extra *l* and comes out *ji-ha-cheol-lyeok*. You don't need to know the rule's name. Just copy the sound, the way you would copy a song lyric.

## Melody: where the sentence goes up and where it drops

A Korean question is mostly told apart from a statement by pitch, not word order. **있어요** said flat and dropping means "we have it." **있어요?** with the last syllable lifted means "do you have it?" Same three syllables. If a clerk looks confused, the fix is usually to lift the 요 higher, not to add words.

Particles like 은, 는, 이, 가 are the shadows of a sentence — let them go quiet and lean on the noun. **화장실**이 어디예요? sounds natural with the weight on 화장실. Tourists who punch every syllable the same sound like they are reading a ticket machine.

## Small slips that make people ask you to repeat

- **Saying *gamsahabnida* with a hard b.** Close your lips into an *m*: *gam-sa-HAM-ni-da*.
- **Splitting 있어요 into three beats.** Glide it: *i-sseo-yo*.
- **Ending 얼마예요? flat.** Without the rise it can sound like you are announcing a price.
- **Dropping 요.** 이거 줘 is what you say to a sibling. With strangers keep 주세요 and the 요 endings.
- **Over-apologizing.** 죄송합니다 is for real mistakes. To get past someone in a crowd, **실례합니다** or a quick **잠시만요** (*jam-si-man-yo*, "one moment") is the natural choice.

## Building your own travel list from here

Every phrase above is a frame with an empty slot. Before your trip, write down the five nouns you will actually need — your hotel's neighborhood, the station near it, a food you want to try, a medicine you take, the word for your charger — and drop each one into 주세요, 있어요?, 어디예요?, and 고 싶어요. That gives you twenty sentences built from things you will really ask for, instead of a hundred phrases you will never say.
