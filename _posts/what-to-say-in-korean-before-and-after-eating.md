Before the first bite you say **잘 먹겠습니다** (*jal meokgesseumnida*) — literally "I will eat well." When the meal is over you say **잘 먹었습니다** (*jal meogeosseumnida*) — "I ate well." Same verb, same 잘, one flipped into the past. Those two lines are not decoration. They are the opening and closing brackets of a Korean meal, and a table full of Koreans will notice the silence if you skip them. The line on your way out of a restaurant is the second one again, often stretched slightly to **맛있게 먹었습니다** (*masitge meogeosseumnida*), "I ate deliciously," aimed at whoever is nearest the register.

> - Before eating: **잘 먹겠습니다** (*jal meokgesseumnida*)
> - After eating: **잘 먹었습니다** (*jal meogeosseumnida*)
> - Casual, with friends: **잘 먹을게** / **잘 먹었어** (*jal meogeulge / jal meogeosseo*)
> - Said *to* the eater: **맛있게 드세요** (*masitge deuseyo*), "Enjoy your meal."
> - Walking out of a restaurant: **맛있게 먹었습니다** (*masitge meogeosseumnida*).

## What 잘 먹겠습니다 is actually doing

The English subtitle usually reads "thank you for the food," and that is the function, but it is not the grammar. 잘 means "well," 먹다 is "to eat," and **-겠-** is the piece that announces an intention about to happen. So you are saying "I am going to eat well" — a small promise to the person who cooked it, ordered it, or is paying for it.

That last part is the rule that decides when you say it. **잘 먹겠습니다 points at a person.** If someone is feeding you, they get the line. Eating alone with a bowl of ramyeon at midnight, most Koreans say nothing at all.

Pronunciation trips people at the end, not the start: **습니다** comes out as *seum-ni-da*, never *seup-ni-da*. Five flat beats — *jal · meok · ge · sseum · ni · da* — with no stress dropped on any one of them.

## Who you say it to, and how casual you go

| Situation | What you say | Romanization |
|---|---|---|
| Your friend's parents cooked | 잘 먹겠습니다 | *jal meokgesseumnida* |
| Your boss is paying | 잘 먹겠습니다 | *jal meokgesseumnida* |
| A friend your age is treating | 잘 먹을게 | *jal meogeulge* |
| Slightly polite, casual company | 잘 먹을게요 | *jal meogeulgeyo* |
| You are the one feeding people | 맛있게 드세요 | *masitge deuseyo* |

**맛있게 드세요** is the reply, and it is the one learners forget exists. Servers say it when the food lands. Hosts say it before they sit down. If you ever pay for a Korean friend's lunch, that is your line, and **많이 드세요** (*mani deuseyo*), "eat a lot," is the warmer version of it.

There is one more piece of table choreography worth knowing: at a formal table, the oldest person lifts their spoon first. You say 잘 먹겠습니다, then you wait half a second. At [a company dinner in Korea](/korean-phrases-company-dinner-korea) that half-second matters more than your grammar does.

## 잘 먹었습니다 — the line that closes the meal

Say it while you are putting your spoon down, not later at the door. **잘 먹었습니다** is past tense, so it reports on the meal, and it carries the thank-you that English would deliver as a separate sentence. You can stack the real thank-you on top and nobody will find it excessive:

- **잘 먹었습니다. 정말 맛있었어요.** — *jal meogeosseumnida. jeongmal masisseosseoyo.* — "I ate well. It was really delicious."
- **배불러요. 진짜 잘 먹었어요.** — *baebulleoyo. jinjja jal meogeosseoyo.* — "I'm full. I really ate well."
- **어머니 음식 최고예요.** — *eomeoni eumsik choegoyeyo.* — "Your mother's cooking is the best."

If the food came out of a friend's kitchen, that third line does more work than the first two. Korean hosts answer compliments by shrinking them — **아니에요, 뭘요** (*anieyo, mwolyo*), "No, it's nothing" — and the polite move is to compliment once more anyway. ## What to say when you are leaving a restaurant

Nothing formal is required, but silence feels cold, and Koreans almost always say something on the way past the counter. Pick one:

| Korean | Romanization | When it fits |
|---|---|---|
| 잘 먹었습니다 | *jal meogeosseumnida* | Always safe, anywhere |
| 맛있게 먹었습니다 | *masitge meogeosseumnida* | Small restaurant, owner is right there |
| 감사합니다 | *gamsahamnida* | Chain restaurant, busy staff |
| 수고하세요 | *sugohaseyo* | "Keep up the good work" — casual, common |
| 안녕히 계세요 | *annyeonghi gyeseyo* | "Goodbye" to the person staying |

**맛있게 먹었습니다** is the one that gets a real smile in a small family-run place, because it is a review, not a formality. And if you need to interrupt the meal for something — the staff reaching for a plate you are not finished with — the sentence is **아직 먹고 있어요** (*ajik meokgo isseoyo*), "I'm still eating." The rest of the mid-meal requests live in [Korean phrases for ordering at a restaurant in Korea](/korean-phrases-ordering-restaurant-korea).

## Reuse the pattern: 먹기 전에 and 먹고 나서

Once these two lines are in your mouth, the grammar underneath them opens up. **(V)기 전에** means "before doing," and **(V)고 나서** means "after doing" — the same before-and-after frame, now attached to any verb.

- **밥 먹기 전에 손 씻어요.** — *bap meokgi jeone son ssiseoyo.* — "I wash my hands before eating."
- **밥 먹고 나서 커피 마실래요?** — *bap meokgo naseo keopi masillaeyo?* — "Want coffee after we eat?"
- **자기 전에 약 먹어야 해요.** — *jagi jeone yak meogeoya haeyo.* — "I have to take medicine before sleeping."

Notice that **밥** (*bap*) means "rice," but in these sentences it means "a meal." That slide is everywhere in Korean — **밥 먹었어요?** ("Did you eat?") is a standard greeting, closer to "how are you" than to a real question about food.

## Mistakes that show up at the table

**Saying 잘 먹겠습니다 after the meal.** The 겠 makes it a plan. Used at the end, it sounds like you are announcing a second dinner.

**Saying it to nobody.** Alone at a food court, the line lands strangely. It belongs to a person who fed you.

**Translating "bon appétit" onto yourself.** **맛있게 드세요** is said *to* the person eating. Saying it about your own plate is like wishing yourself a nice meal out loud.

**Skipping the reply when you are the host.** If a guest says 잘 먹겠습니다 and you say nothing, the bracket never closed. **네, 맛있게 드세요** takes one second.

**Mixing registers at a formal table.** **잘 먹을게** to your friend's father is too flat. When you are not sure which rung you are on, [how to greet someone at different Korean politeness levels](/greet-korean-politeness-levels) lays the ladder out in order.
