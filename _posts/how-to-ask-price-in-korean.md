The line you want is **이거 얼마예요?** (*igeo eolmayeyo?*), "How much is this?" Point at the thing, say it, and you are done — it works at a traditional market stall, a cafe counter, a clothing shop, a ticket window. If the item is across the room, swap the first word: **저거 얼마예요?** (*jeogeo eolmayeyo?*), "How much is that one?" And if you are already holding the thing or the context is obvious, **얼마예요?** on its own is a complete, polite question. This is Pattern 15 in the SULSUL workbook — a question word plus **예요/해요** — doing the simplest job it has. The part nobody warns you about is what happens two seconds later, when the answer comes back as a number in the number system you did not study.

> - The default: **이거 얼마예요?** (*igeo eolmayeyo?*) — "How much is this?"
> - For something further away: **저거 얼마예요?** (*jeogeo eolmayeyo?*) — "How much is that?"
> - Getting attention first: **저기요, 이거 얼마예요?** (*jeogiyo, igeo eolmayeyo?*) — "Excuse me, how much is this?"
> - The total: **다 해서 얼마예요?** (*da haeseo eolmayeyo?*) — "How much altogether?"
> - Per item: **하나에 얼마예요?** (*hanae eolmayeyo?*) — "How much for one?"

## Which version of the question do you actually say?

**얼마** (*eolma*) means "how much," and **예요** turns it into a question about price. That is the whole grammar. What changes between situations is the front of the sentence, not the back.

In a shop where nobody is looking at you yet, open with **저기요** (*jeogiyo*), "excuse me" — it is the standard way to flag a staff member, and it is not rude. At a market stall or a small family-run place you can go one better and call the owner **사장님** (*sajangnim*), literally "boss," which is the polite catch-all title for anyone running a business: **사장님, 이거 얼마예요?**

If you want a slightly softer, more grown-up register, **가격이 어떻게 돼요?** (*gagyeogi eotteoke dwaeyo?*), "What is the price?" uses the same indirect **어떻게 돼요** frame Korean uses for personal information. You will also hear **얼마죠?** (*eolmajyo?*) and **얼마인가요?** (*eolmaingayo?*), both gentler-sounding variants of the same question. Among close friends it drops to **얼마야?** (*eolmaya?*). The formal **얼마입니까?** (*eolmaimnikka?*) is real Korean, but it is what staff say to you, not what you say to staff.

You: **저기요, 이거 얼마예요?** — *jeogiyo, igeo eolmayeyo?* — "Excuse me, how much is this?"  
Them: **그거 만 오천 원이에요.** — *geugeo man ocheon wonieyo.* — "That one's fifteen thousand won."  
You: **아, 네. 이거 주세요.** — *a, ne. igeo juseyo.* — "Ah, okay. I'll take this one."

**이거 주세요** (*igeo juseyo*) is Pattern 01, the single most useful sentence in Korean, and it is how the price question almost always ends.

## Catching the answer, which is the real difficulty

Money in Korean runs on Sino-Korean numbers — **일, 이, 삼** (*il, i, sam*) — not the native **하나, 둘, 셋** set. That much most learners know. What ambushes people is **만** (*man*), which is ten thousand, not a thousand. Korean groups large numbers in units of four digits, so a price that feels like "fifteen thousand" in English arrives as **만 오천**, "one man five thousand."

| Won | Korean | Romanization |
|---|---|---|
| 1,000 | 천 원 | *cheon won* |
| 3,500 | 삼천오백 원 | *samcheonobaek won* |
| 10,000 | 만 원 | *man won* |
| 15,000 | 만 오천 원 | *man ocheon won* |
| 50,000 | 오만 원 | *oman won* |

Two habits make the answer land. First, listen for **만** as the hinge: whatever comes before it is the ten-thousands. Second, expect the word **원** to be dropped. Shopkeepers routinely say **삼천이요** (*samcheoniyo*) or **만 오천이요** (*man ocheoniyo*) with the currency left off entirely, and that clipped version is what you will hear most. The whole two-number-system problem is unpacked in [Korean numbers for paying and counting in Korea](/korean-numbers-paying-counting-korea).

When you miss it, do not nod. Pattern 22 exists for exactly this: **다시 말해 주세요** (*dasi malhae juseyo*), "Please say that again," or **천천히 말해 주세요** (*cheoncheonhi malhae juseyo*), "Please say it slowly." The move that never fails is **여기 좀 적어 주시겠어요?** (*yeogi jom jeogeo jusigesseoyo?*), "Could you write it down here?" Vendors reach for a calculator or a phone screen without being asked half the time anyway.

## Asking about totals, units, and the price you did not expect

One question rarely covers a real purchase. These four carry the rest of it.

**다 해서 얼마예요?** (*da haeseo eolmayeyo?*) — "How much for everything?" Use it once your items are on the counter, or when a stall owner has been piling things into a bag.

**하나에 얼마예요?** (*hanae eolmayeyo?*) — "How much each?" Markets price by the pile, the bundle, or the kilo, so the sticker you are looking at may not be a single-item price. The counter changes with the thing: **한 개에** (*han gaee*) for objects, **한 잔에** (*han jane*) for drinks, **한 장에** (*han jange*) for flat things like tickets and photocards.

**이거 세일 가격이에요?** (*igeo seil gagyeogieyo?*) — "Is this the sale price?" Useful when two numbers are printed on the same tag.

**카드 돼요?** (*kadeu dwaeyo?*) — "Can I pay by card?" In Korea the answer is almost always yes, including for small amounts, but old market stalls are the exception and will say **현금만 돼요** (*hyeongeumman dwaeyo*), "cash only."

Them: **다 해서 이만 삼천 원이에요.** — *da haeseo iman samcheon wonieyo.* — "Twenty-three thousand won altogether."  
You: **카드 돼요?** — *kadeu dwaeyo?* — "Can I pay by card?"  
Them: **네, 여기 놔 주세요.** — *ne, yeogi nwa juseyo.* — "Yes, tap it here."

If the number is higher than you hoped, the polite reaction is **좀 비싸네요** (*jom bissaneyo*), "That's a bit pricey" — Pattern 94's cushion adverb **좀** keeping it light rather than confrontational. Whether you can push further depends entirely on where you are standing; the rules for that are in [Korean phrases for bargaining in markets](/korean-phrases-bargaining-markets), and the clothing-shop version is in [what to say when buying clothes in Korea](/buying-clothes-in-korea).

## What usually goes wrong

**Saying 몇 원이에요?** It looks logical — **몇** is "how many" — but Korean does not ask for a price that way. It is **얼마**, always.

**Confusing 얼마 with 얼마나.** **얼마나** (*eolmana*) asks about extent or duration: **얼마나 걸려요?** (*eolmana geollyeoyo?*), "How long does it take?" Attach it to a price and you have said something that makes no sense. Price is bare **얼마**.

**Using native numbers for money.** **두 천 원** is not a thing. Counters like **두 개** take native numbers; won takes Sino-Korean. The two sets never cross.

**Hearing 만 as a thousand.** This is the expensive one. Hear **삼만 원** as three thousand and you will hand over a tenth of what you owe and look very confused. Anchor on **만 = 10,000** until it is automatic.

**Bargaining in the wrong building.** In a department store, a chain cafe, or Olive Young, prices are fixed and asking for a discount is genuinely awkward. Traditional markets, Dongdaemun stalls, and street vendors are where the question belongs.

**Freezing after the answer.** You asked, they answered, and now there is a silence. Bank two replies so there is never a gap: **이거 주세요** to buy, **좀 더 볼게요** (*jom deo bolgeyo*), "I'll look around a bit more," to leave politely.
