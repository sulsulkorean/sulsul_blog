The basic Korean phrases a tourist really needs are fewer than you think. With **안녕하세요** (hello), **감사합니다** (thank you), **이거 주세요** (this one, please), **___ 어디예요?** (where is ___?), **___ 있어요?** (do you have ___?), and **카드 돼요?** (can I pay by card?), you can get from Incheon Airport to a hotel bed and a hot dinner without switching to English once.

> - **안녕하세요** — *an-nyeong-ha-se-yo* — hello (to anyone)
> - **감사합니다** — *gam-sa-ham-ni-da* — thank you
> - **이거 주세요** — *i-geo ju-se-yo* — this one, please
> - **___ 어디예요?** — *eo-di-ye-yo* — where is ___?
> - **___ 있어요?** — *i-sseo-yo* — do you have ___? / is there ___?
> - **카드 돼요?** — *ka-deu dwae-yo* — do you take cards?

Most phrase lists hand you fifty lines sorted by grammar. That is not how a trip works. A trip is a string of small counters and doors, one after another. So this guide follows your first 24 hours in order, and at each stop you get the one or two lines that do the job — plus the line the Korean person will most likely say back, because that is the part tourists freeze on.

## Before you land: the two endings that carry everything

Almost every polite sentence a tourist says ends in one of two ways.

**주세요** (*ju-se-yo*) means "please give me." Put any noun in front and it becomes a request: **물 주세요** (water, please), **영수증 주세요** (a receipt, please), **봉투 주세요** (a bag, please). If you do not know the noun, point and say **이거 주세요** — "this, please."

**요** (*yo*) at the end of a sentence is the polite marker. **있어요**, **돼요**, **어디예요** — all of them end in 요. Keep it on every sentence with strangers and you will never sound rude, even if the middle of the sentence is shaky.

The question version is the same sentence with your voice going up at the end. **있어요.** (there is) becomes **있어요?** (is there?). No extra word needed — just the rising tone.

## At the airport: getting out and getting connected

The first thing most tourists need after immigration is a SIM card or Wi-Fi, then the way into the city.

- **유심 있어요?** (*yu-sim i-sseo-yo?*) — Do you have SIM cards?
- **와이파이 비밀번호 뭐예요?** (*wa-i-pa-i bi-mil-beon-ho mwo-ye-yo?*) — What is the Wi-Fi password?
- **공항철도 어디예요?** (*gong-hang-cheol-do eo-di-ye-yo?*) — Where is the airport railroad?
- **택시 타는 곳 어디예요?** (*taek-si ta-neun got eo-di-ye-yo?*) — Where is the taxi stand?

What you will hear back: **저쪽이요** (*jeo-jjo-gi-yo*, "over there") with a pointing hand, or **쭉 가세요** (*jjuk ga-se-yo*, "go straight"). You do not need to understand more than the hand and the word 쭉.

If you take a taxi, show the address on your phone and say **여기로 가 주세요** (*yeo-gi-ro ga ju-se-yo*) — "please go here." It is the same 주세요 ending, attached to a verb.

## On the subway: buying a card and finding your stop

![A traveler checking the subway map at a Seoul station, about to ask which line to take](/assets/blog/scenes/transport.jpg)

Seoul's subway signs are in English too, so the phrases you need here are for the moments the signs do not cover.

- **교통카드 어디서 사요?** (*gyo-tong-ka-deu eo-di-seo sa-yo?*) — Where do I buy a transit card?
- **충전해 주세요** (*chung-jeon-hae ju-se-yo*) — Please top it up. (Most stations have a machine for this, and convenience store clerks will do it at the counter.)
- **이거 명동 가요?** (*i-geo myeong-dong ga-yo?*) — Does this go to Myeongdong?
- **몇 번 출구예요?** (*myeot beon chul-gu-ye-yo?*) — Which exit number?

The exit number is the most useful thing you can ask. Big stations have ten or more exits, and Korean directions are given as "exit 3, then walk straight." If someone tells you **3번 출구** (*sam-beon chul-gu*), you are set.

## At the hotel: checking in without the script

Hotel front desks in big cities usually speak English, but guesthouses and small hotels often do not. Three lines cover check-in.

- **예약했어요** (*ye-ya-kae-sseo-yo*) — I have a reservation. Follow it with your name: **이름은 ___예요.**
- **짐 맡겨도 돼요?** (*jim mat-gyeo-do dwae-yo?*) — Can I leave my luggage?
- **체크아웃 몇 시예요?** (*che-keu-a-ut myeot si-ye-yo?*) — What time is check-out?

What you will hear back: **여권 주세요** (*yeo-gwon ju-se-yo*) — "your passport, please." Notice it is the same 주세요 you have been using. Once you hear it from the other side of the counter, the pattern sticks.

## At the convenience store: your first small win

The convenience store is where most tourists say their first real Korean sentence, because it is quick, low-stakes, and open all night.

- **이거 얼마예요?** (*i-geo eol-ma-ye-yo?*) — How much is this?
- **카드 돼요?** (*ka-deu dwae-yo?*) — Do you take cards?
- **봉투 주세요** (*bong-tu ju-se-yo*) — A bag, please. Bags are not given automatically.
- **데워 주세요** (*de-wo ju-se-yo*) — Please heat it up. For a lunch box or a frozen kimbap.

What you will hear back: **봉투 필요하세요?** (*bong-tu pi-ryo-ha-se-yo?*, "do you need a bag?"), and **영수증 드릴까요?** (*yeong-su-jeung deu-ril-kka-yo?*, "do you want the receipt?"). The easy answers are **네** (yes) and **아니요, 괜찮아요** (no, I'm fine).

## At dinner: ordering, asking, paying

By evening you are hungry and tired, which is exactly when a short script helps.

- **두 명이요** (*du myeong-i-yo*) — Two people. Hold up two fingers as you say it.
- **저기요!** (*jeo-gi-yo!*) — Excuse me! The way to call staff in a Korean restaurant.
- **이거 하나 주세요** (*i-geo ha-na ju-se-yo*) — One of this, please. Point at the menu.
- **안 맵게 해 주세요** (*an maep-ge hae ju-se-yo*) — Not spicy, please.
- **계산할게요** (*gye-san-hal-ge-yo*) — I'd like to pay.

What you will hear first, before you even sit down: **몇 분이세요?** (*myeot bu-ni-se-yo?*) — "how many people?" That is why **두 명이요** comes first on the list.

On the way out, say **잘 먹었습니다** (*jal meo-geot-seum-ni-da*) — "I ate well." Staff love hearing it from a visitor.

## A short scene: one line at each stop

Here is the whole day as a traveler named Sam lives it.

> **Airport counter:** 안녕하세요. 유심 있어요? — *Hello. Do you have SIM cards?*
> **Staff:** 네, 며칠 쓰세요? — *Yes, for how many days?*
> **Sam:** 오 일이요. — *Five days.*
> 
> **Subway station:** 저기요, 이거 명동 가요? — *Excuse me, does this go to Myeongdong?*
> **Passerby:** 네, 4호선이에요. — *Yes, it's Line 4.*
> 
> **Convenience store:** 이거 데워 주세요. 카드 돼요? — *Please heat this up. Do you take cards?*
> **Clerk:** 네, 돼요. — *Yes, we do.*

Sam used maybe eight different words all day. The endings — 주세요, 있어요?, 돼요?, 이에요 — did the rest.

## Four slips tourists make, and the fix for each

**Dropping the 요.** **있어?** and **돼?** are casual — fine with friends, a little blunt with a shop clerk. Keep 요 on everything until someone tells you to drop it.

**Saying 안녕히 가세요 when you are the one leaving.** That means "go in peace" and is said to the person leaving. When you walk out of a shop, say **안녕히 계세요** ("stay in peace") — or just **감사합니다**, which works every time.

**Reading 있어요 as "it-sseo-yo."** The ㅆ sound is tense and slides into the next syllable: *i-sseo-yo*, not *it-so-yo*. Clerks understand either, but the smooth version gets an answer instead of a puzzled look.

**Freezing when you don't understand the reply.** Use **다시 한 번만요?** (*da-si han beon-man-yo?*, "one more time?") or **천천히 말해 주세요** (*cheon-cheon-hi mal-hae ju-se-yo*, "please speak slowly"). Koreans slow down right away, and often switch to gestures.
