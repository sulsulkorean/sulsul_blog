"Korean language" in Korean is **한국어** (*hangugeo*). **한국** (*hanguk*) is Korea, and **어** (*eo*) is the suffix for a language, the same one in **영어** (*yeongeo*, English) and **일본어** (*ilboneo*, Japanese). In conversation, Koreans just as often say **한국말** (*hangungmal*), where **말** (*mal*) means "speech" or "words." The one word that does *not* mean the language is **한글** (*hangeul*). That's the alphabet, the letters on the sign, not the language spoken out loud.

> - The Korean language (neutral, written, classroom): **한국어** (*hangugeo*)
> - The Korean language (spoken, everyday): **한국말** (*hangungmal*)
> - The Korean alphabet only: **한글** (*hangeul*)
> - "I speak a little Korean": **한국어 조금 해요** (*hangugeo jogeum haeyo*)
> - "Do you speak English?": **영어 할 줄 아세요?** (*yeongeo hal jul aseyo?*)

## 한국어 vs 한국말: same language, different feel

Both words are correct, and a Korean listener won't stop to think about which one you chose. The difference is register, not meaning.

| Word | Romanization | Where you see or hear it |
|---|---|---|
| 한국어 | *hangugeo* | Textbooks, TOPIK, app menus, "Korean" on a language setting |
| 한국말 | *hangungmal* | Chatting with a shop owner, a friend's grandmother, a taxi driver |
| 한글 | *hangeul* | The writing system: "Can you read 한글?" |
| 국어 | *gugeo* | "National language," the school subject Korean kids study |

Pronunciation is where learners slip. **한국어** is spelled *han-guk-eo*, but the ㄱ slides across into the next syllable, so it comes out *han-gu-geo*. **한국말** changes even more: the ㄱ before ㅁ turns nasal, so you hear *han-gung-mal*, not *han-guk-mal*. Say *hanguk-mal* with a hard k and you'll be understood, but it sounds read off a page.

The 어 / 말 swap works for a few other languages too: **일본어** and **일본말**, **중국어** and **중국말**. Older Koreans lean toward the 말 versions. English stays **영어** in every register.

## Why 한글 is not "Korean"

English speakers often say "I'm learning Hangul" when they mean "I'm learning Korean." In Korean that sentence sounds like you're only learning the letters, which you can do in an afternoon. Keep them apart:

- **한글 읽을 수 있어요.** — *hangeul ilgeul su isseoyo.* — "I can read Hangul."
- **한국어 공부하고 있어요.** — *hangugeo gongbuhago isseoyo.* — "I'm studying Korean."

So when a menu has no pictures and you can sound out every letter but understand none of it, that's the exact gap between 한글 and 한국어.

## The survival lines: telling people how much Korean you speak

This is what the word is really for on a trip. The first thing someone will want to know when you open your mouth is how much Korean they can use with you. Pattern 13 in the SULSUL workbook, **(V)ㄹ/을 줄 알아요 / 몰라요**, "know how to / don't know how to," does most of the work.

| You want to say | Korean | Romanization |
|---|---|---|
| I speak a little Korean | 한국어 조금 해요 | *hangugeo jogeum haeyo* |
| I'm not good at Korean | 한국어 잘 못해요 | *hangugeo jal motaeyo* |
| I only speak a little | 한국어 조금밖에 못 해요 | *hangugeo jogeumbakke mot haeyo* |
| I can speak Korean | 한국어 할 줄 알아요 | *hangugeo hal jul arayo* |
| Do you speak English? | 영어 할 줄 아세요? | *yeongeo hal jul aseyo?* |
| Slowly, please | 천천히 말해 주세요 | *cheoncheonhi malhae juseyo* |

**조금밖에 못 해요** uses Pattern 66, **밖에 + negative**, "only … (and not more)." It's more honest than **조금 해요**, and it sets the speed of the conversation for you. When the reply still comes back at full speed, the follow-up lines are in [Korean phrases when someone speaks too fast](/korean-phrases-when-korean-is-too-fast).

Notice **영어 할 줄 아세요?** has **-시-** inside it (아**세**요). You're asking about a stranger, so the verb lifts them up. **영어 할 줄 알아요?** to a pharmacist is understandable, but flatter than you mean it to be.

## A short exchange at a market stall

Here is how these lines actually land in one conversation:

**Vendor:** 뭐 찾으세요? — *mwo chajeuseyo?* — "What are you looking for?"
**You:** 죄송해요, 한국어 조금밖에 못 해요. — *joesonghaeyo, hangugeo jogeumbakke mot haeyo.* — "Sorry, I only speak a little Korean."
**Vendor:** 아, 괜찮아요! 영어 조금 해요. — *a, gwaenchanayo! yeongeo jogeum haeyo.* — "Oh, it's fine! I speak a little English."
**You:** 아니에요, 한국어로 해 주세요. 천천히요. — *anieyo, hangugeoro hae juseyo. cheoncheonhiyo.* — "No, please say it in Korean. Slowly."

That last line, **한국어로 해 주세요**, is the one that turns a shopping trip into practice. **로** means "in" or "by means of," so **한국어로** is "in Korean," the same piece you use in **한국어로 뭐예요?** to ask for a word you don't know. That question has [its own full walkthrough](/how-to-say-in-korean-hangugeoro-mwoyeyo).

## When someone says your Korean is good

It will happen after about four words. **한국어 잘하시네요!** (*hangugeo jalhasineyo!*), "You speak Korean well!" The Korean reflex is to push the compliment away, and Pattern 18, **맞아요 / 아니에요**, gives you the shape:

- **아니에요, 아직 많이 부족해요.** — *anieyo, ajik mani bujokaeyo.* — "No, I still have a long way to go."
- **아니에요, 열심히 배우고 있어요.** — *anieyo, yeolsimhi baeugo isseoyo.* — "Not really, I'm working hard at it."
- **감사합니다! 더 열심히 할게요.** — *gamsahamnida! deo yeolsimhi halgeyo.* — "Thank you! I'll keep at it."

Saying **네, 맞아요** ("Yes, that's right") is grammatical but reads as a little cocky. The modest answer is the one that gets the warmest laugh.

## Mistakes with the word itself

**Saying 한글 for the language.** "한글 잘해요" sounds like you have nice handwriting. Use 한국어 or 한국말.

**Saying 한국 alone.** **한국** is the country. "한국 조금 해요" doesn't mean anything. The 어 or 말 has to be there.

**Pronouncing every letter.** *Han-guk-eo* with a hard stop is the spelling, not the sound. Let it run: *han-gu-geo*.

**Using 한국말 in a formal document.** On a form, a CV, or a [part-time job interview](/korean-phrases-part-time-job-interview-korea), write **한국어 능력** (*hangugeo neungnyeok*), "Korean ability." 한국말 belongs to talk, not to paperwork.

**Asking 영어 해요? to everyone.** It works, but it's blunt. **영어 할 줄 아세요?** costs two more syllables and sounds like you were raised well.
