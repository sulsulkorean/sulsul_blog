To address an older person in Korean, you use a title, never the word "you." The safe choices are **어르신** (*eoreusin*, "respected elder") for anyone clearly elderly, **할머니** (*halmeoni*, "grandmother") and **할아버지** (*harabeoji*, "grandfather") for people around your grandparents' age, **어머님** (*eomeonim*) and **아버님** (*abeonim*), "mother / father," for people around your parents' age, and **선생님** (*seonsaengnim*) for doctors, teachers, and anyone in a professional role. Then you soften every verb with **-(으)세요**: **앉으세요** (*anjeuseyo*, "please sit"), **괜찮으세요?** (*gwaenchaneuseyo?*, "are you all right?"). Pick the title, add the ending, and you will sound respectful even with a beginner's vocabulary.

> - **어르신** (*eoreusin*) — respected elder. Safe for any senior you don't know.
> - **할머니 / 할아버지** (*halmeoni / harabeoji*) — grandma / grandpa. Warm, used for strangers too.
> - **어머님 / 아버님** (*eomeonim / abeonim*) — mother / father. For people your parents' age.
> - **선생님** (*seonsaengnim*) — teacher, doctor, or any respected professional.
> - **사장님** (*sajangnim*) — the owner, at shops and restaurants.
> - **-(으)세요** — the polite verb ending that shows respect to the listener.

## Why Korean has no safe word for "you"

English uses "you" for a child and a grandmother alike. Korean does have words for "you" — **너** (*neo*), **당신** (*dangsin*) — but none of them work with an older stranger. 너 is for close friends and younger people. 당신 sounds cold, and in an argument it sounds like a challenge. So Koreans fill that spot with a title, and they repeat it where English would say "you":

- **할머니, 어디 가세요?** (*halmeoni, eodi gaseyo?*) — "Where are you going, ma'am?" (literally "Grandmother, where are you going?")
- **선생님, 이거 먹어도 돼요?** (*seonsaengnim, igeo meogeodo dwaeyo?*) — "Doctor, can I eat this?"

Once you stop looking for a word for "you," Korean politeness gets much easier.

## Which title fits which person

Age is the main thing you are guessing, and Koreans guess it too. When in doubt, aim a little younger — nobody minds being called 어머님 instead of 할머니.

| Who you're talking to | What to call them |
|---|---|
| A clearly elderly stranger, formal setting | 어르신 (*eoreusin*) |
| An older woman, grandmother's age | 할머니 (*halmeoni*) |
| An older man, grandfather's age | 할아버지 (*harabeoji*) |
| A woman around your parents' age | 어머님 (*eomeonim*) |
| A man around your parents' age | 아버님 (*abeonim*) |
| A doctor, teacher, pharmacist, instructor | 선생님 (*seonsaengnim*) |
| A shop or restaurant owner of any age | 사장님 (*sajangnim*) |

**어르신** is the most respectful and the most neutral — it works for men and women and never guesses wrong about gender. You will hear it from staff at banks, hospitals, and community centers. It sounds a little formal in casual talk, so with a friendly market vendor, 할머니 is warmer.

**어머님 / 아버님** are what a polite younger adult uses with a middle-aged stranger, for example at a friend's house, or when helping someone with a heavy bag.

## The other half: -(으)세요 on your verbs

A title alone is not enough. If you say **할머니, 앉아** (*halmeoni, anja*), you have just ordered your grandmother to sit. Korean shows respect inside the verb, and the beginner-friendly way to do it is **-(으)세요**:

- 앉다 (sit) → **앉으세요** (*anjeuseyo*) — "Please sit down."
- 가다 (go) → **가세요** (*gaseyo*) — "Please go / you're going."
- 괜찮다 (be okay) → **괜찮으세요?** (*gwaenchaneuseyo?*) — "Are you all right?"
- 조심하다 (be careful) → **조심하세요** (*josimhaseyo*) — "Please be careful."

Add **-으세요** after a final consonant (앉 → 앉으세요) and **-세요** after a vowel (가 → 가세요). The same ending works as a question when your voice goes up, so one form covers both "please do" and "are you doing?"

A few everyday verbs change into a special honorific word. These three come up constantly with older people:

- 먹다 (eat) → **드세요** (*deuseyo*) — "Please eat / have some."
- 있다 (be somewhere) → **계세요** (*gyeseyo*) — "Please stay / are you there?"
- 자다 (sleep) → **주무세요** (*jumuseyo*) — "Please sleep / good night."

Saying **먹으세요** to a grandmother is understood, but **드세요** is what she expects to hear.

## Five situations you will actually meet

**Giving up your seat on the subway or bus.** Seats near the doors are for seniors, and offering one is normal. Stand up and say: **어르신, 여기 앉으세요.** (*eoreusin, yeogi anjeuseyo*) — "Please sit here." If they refuse with **괜찮아요**, offer once more; a second refusal is real.

**Asking an older person for directions.** Start with a softener and the title: **저, 할머니, 혹시 시청역이 어디예요?** (*jeo, halmeoni, hoksi sicheongyeogi eodiyeyo?*) — "Excuse me, grandma, do you know where City Hall Station is?" Finish with **감사합니다** (*gamsahamnida*), the more formal thank-you.

**At a market stall run by an older vendor.** **할머니, 이거 얼마예요?** (*halmeoni, igeo eolmayeyo?*) — "How much is this?" Market grandmas often call you 아가씨 or 총각 back; that is affection, not rudeness.

**At a friend's family dinner.** Use 어머님 and 아버님 for your friend's parents. Before eating, wait for the oldest person to lift their spoon, then say **잘 먹겠습니다** (*jal meokgetseumnida*). When you pass food, add **드세요**: **아버님, 이것 좀 드세요.** — "Please have some of this."

**Helping someone who looks unwell.** **어르신, 괜찮으세요? 도와드릴까요?** (*eoreusin, gwaenchaneuseyo? dowadeurilkkayo?*) — "Are you all right? Can I help you?" 도와드릴까요 is the humble form of "shall I help," used when the helper is younger.

## Mistakes that sound rude without meaning to

**Using 아저씨 and 아줌마 too freely.** These mean "mister" and "ma'am," and you will hear them everywhere. But 아줌마 in particular can sound dismissive to a woman who does not see herself as middle-aged, and both can feel blunt. 사장님 for shop owners and 어머님 / 아버님 elsewhere are kinder defaults.

**Calling a stranger 할머니 too early.** A woman in her fifties called "grandmother" may laugh, but she will notice. If you cannot tell, use 어머님, or skip the title and start with **저기요** (*jeogiyo*), "excuse me."

**Saying 당신.** It appears in song lyrics and between married couples, and in a street argument. To an older stranger it sounds hostile. Repeat the title instead.

**Asking 몇 살이에요?** Asking an older person's age with 몇 살 is too casual. The respectful version is **연세가 어떻게 되세요?** (*yeonsega eotteoke doeseyo?*). More in our guide on [how to ask someone's age in Korean](/how-to-ask-age-in-korean).

**Dropping 요 at the end.** Even with a title in front, **할머니, 고마워** sounds like talking to a child. Keep the 요, or go one step higher: **감사합니다**.

## Say it out loud before your trip

Pick two lines and practice them until they come out without thinking: **어르신, 여기 앉으세요** and **할머니, 이거 얼마예요?** The title and the -(으)세요 ending are a package — say them together every time, and they will stick together when a real grandmother is standing in front of you.
