A beginner Korean sentence has one rule that does most of the work: **the verb goes last, and the sentence ends in 요.** "I drink coffee" is **저는 커피를 마셔요** (*jeoneun keopireul masyeoyo*), literally "I coffee drink." Once that shape feels normal, you don't need new grammar to make new sentences. You change the noun in the middle, or you change the ending on the verb, and the same frame says "I drank coffee," "I'll drink coffee," "I don't drink coffee," and "Do you drink coffee?" Below are the four parts of the frame, then 20 sentences built from it that you can actually say in Korea this week.

> - I'm Emma. — **저는 엠마예요.** (*jeoneun emmayeyo*)
> - I'm a student. — **저는 학생이에요.** (*jeoneun haksaeng-ieyo*)
> - I drink coffee. — **커피 마셔요.** (*keopi masyeoyo*)
> - I don't eat meat. — **고기 안 먹어요.** (*gogi an meogeoyo*)
> - I went to Busan. — **부산에 갔어요.** (*busane gasseoyo*)
> - I'll go tomorrow. — **내일 갈 거예요.** (*naeil gal geoyeyo*)
> - Do you like kimchi? — **김치 좋아해요?** (*gimchi joahaeyo?*)

## The shape: who, what, then the verb

English runs subject, verb, object. Korean runs subject, object, verb:

| English | Korean order | Korean |
|---|---|---|
| I drink coffee. | I · coffee · drink | 저는 커피를 마셔요. |
| I study Korean. | I · Korean · study | 저는 한국어를 공부해요. |
| I eat bibimbap in Seoul. | I · Seoul-in · bibimbap · eat | 저는 서울에서 비빔밥을 먹어요. |

Two small tags hold this together. **는/은** after a word marks "as for me," the topic. **를/을** after a word marks the thing being acted on. You will hear them everywhere, but here's the relief for beginners: in spoken Korean both get dropped all the time. **커피 마셔요** is a complete, natural sentence. And because the person you're talking to already knows you mean yourself, **저는** usually disappears too. If you only remember "verb last," people will understand you.

## Ending 1: 이에요/예요 — saying what something is

The simplest Korean sentence is "A is B." That's **Pattern 05** in the SULSUL workbook: noun + **이에요** after a consonant, noun + **예요** after a vowel.

1. **저는 학생이에요.** — "I'm a student."
2. **저는 캐나다 사람이에요.** — "I'm Canadian." (사람 = person)
3. **이거 뭐예요?** — "What is this?"
4. **여기 처음이에요.** — "It's my first time here."

Number 4 is worth gold in Korea. Say it to a taxi driver, a shop owner or a host, and people slow down and help.

## Ending 2: 해요 and friends — saying what you do

For actions, the polite present tense ends in **아요/어요/해요**. **Pattern 06**, (N) 해요, is the easiest door in: stick 해요 after a noun of activity and it becomes a verb.

5. **한국어 공부해요.** — "I study Korean."
6. **운동해요.** — "I work out."
7. **커피 마셔요.** — "I drink coffee." (마시다 → 마셔요)
8. **한국 음식 좋아해요.** — "I like Korean food."
9. **서울에 살아요.** — "I live in Seoul." (살다 → 살아요)

Notice the **에** in number 9 and the **에서** earlier. 에 marks where you are or where you're going; 에서 marks where an action happens (**Pattern 26**). **카페에서 공부해요** — "I study at a café."

## Ending 3: changing the time — 았어요 and ㄹ 거예요

Now keep the sentence and move it in time. Past tense adds **았/었** before 요 (**Pattern 08**). Future adds **ㄹ/을 거예요** (**Pattern 09**).

10. **어제 명동에 갔어요.** — "I went to Myeongdong yesterday."
11. **비빔밥 먹었어요.** — "I ate bibimbap."
12. **진짜 맛있었어요.** — "It was really delicious."
13. **내일 부산에 갈 거예요.** — "I'll go to Busan tomorrow."
14. **이따 전화할 거예요.** — "I'll call later."

Time words like **어제** (yesterday), **오늘** (today), **내일** (tomorrow) sit near the front of the sentence. The verb, as always, stays at the end.

## Ending 4: saying no with 안

To make a sentence negative, put **안** right before the verb (**Pattern 07**). Nothing else moves.

15. **고기 안 먹어요.** — "I don't eat meat."
16. **술 안 마셔요.** — "I don't drink alcohol."
17. **안 매워요.** — "It's not spicy."

The workbook pairs 안 with **못**, "can't." **못 먹어요** means you *can't* eat it, which is the kinder way to refuse food at a dinner, because it sounds like a fact about you rather than a judgment about the dish.

## Turning any of these into a question

Korean questions don't flip the word order. You keep the exact sentence and **raise your voice at the end**.

18. **커피 마셔요?** ↗ — "Do you drink coffee?"
19. **한국 음식 좋아해요?** ↗ — "Do you like Korean food?"
20. **어디 가요?** ↗ — "Where are you going?"

Number 20 adds a question word, **어디** (where). The others, **뭐** (what), **언제** (when), **누구** (who), and **왜** (why), slot into the same spot (**Pattern 15**). All five are laid out in [how to ask questions in Korean](/how-to-ask-questions-in-korean).

## A short conversation built only from these

At a guesthouse, meeting another traveler:

A: **안녕하세요. 여기 처음이에요?** — "Hi. First time here?"
B: **네, 처음이에요. 어제 왔어요.** — "Yes, first time. I came yesterday."
A: **아, 그래요? 내일 뭐 할 거예요?** — "Oh really? What will you do tomorrow?"
B: **경복궁에 갈 거예요. 같이 갈래요?** — "I'll go to Gyeongbokgung. Want to come along?"
A: **좋아요!** — "Sounds good!"

Every line there is one of the four endings above, plus a noun swapped in.

## The mistakes beginners make building sentences

**Putting the verb in the middle.** *저는 마셔요 커피* sounds like a word salad. Hold the verb until the end, even if it feels like you're keeping someone waiting.

**Dropping 요.** **먹어** instead of **먹어요** is fine with close friends and wrong with everyone else. Keep 요 as your default; [how to be polite in Korean](/how-to-be-polite-in-korean) explains when you can let it go.

**Over-using 저는.** Starting every sentence with "I" sounds stiff in Korean. Use it once to introduce yourself, then let it drop.

**Translating word for word from English.** "I am hungry" is not *저는 배고파 이에요.* It's just **배고파요.** Korean adjectives already work as verbs, so they take the 요 ending directly (**Pattern 11**).

## How to practice your first 20 sentences

Pick one verb you actually use, like 마시다 (drink), and run it through all four endings out loud: **마셔요, 마셨어요, 마실 거예요, 안 마셔요,** then **마셔요?** with your voice rising. Do the same with 먹다 (eat) and 가다 (go). That's fifteen sentences from three verbs, and you'll notice your mouth starts reaching for the ending before your brain does.
