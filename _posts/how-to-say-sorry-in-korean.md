To say sorry in Korean, say **죄송합니다** (*joesonghamnida*) to anyone you do not know — a stranger, a shop worker, a taxi driver, someone older than you. To a friend or someone younger, say **미안해요** (*mianhaeyo*) or **미안해** (*mianhae*). Unlike **감사합니다** and **고맙습니다**, which sit at the same height, these two are *not* interchangeable: **죄송** carries more deference than **미안**, so **죄송합니다** is the one that never misfires. And here is the part that surprises most travellers — the majority of the moments you *think* need an apology in Korea do not. Squeezing past someone, flagging down a server, getting off a packed subway car: those want **잠시만요** or **저기요**, not **죄송합니다**.

> - Sorry (to anyone) — **죄송합니다** (*joesonghamnida*)
> - Sorry (polite, a little softer) — **죄송해요** (*joesonghaeyo*)
> - Sorry (to friends, polite form) — **미안해요** (*mianhaeyo*)
> - Sorry (to friends) — **미안해** (*mianhae*)
> - Excuse me / coming through — **잠시만요** (*jamsimanyo*)
> - Excuse me (getting attention) — **저기요** (*jeogiyo*)
> - It's fine / no worries — **괜찮아요** (*gwaenchanayo*)

## 죄송 and 미안 are not the same height

Both words are Sino-Korean, and their literal meanings explain the gap. **죄송** is built from characters meaning *guilt* and *dread* — it says "I feel guilty and small about this." **미안** means *not at ease* — "I am uncomfortable about what I did to you." One confesses fault upward, the other admits discomfort sideways. That is why a junior employee apologizes to a manager with **죄송합니다** and would sound strange saying **미안합니다**, while a friend who says **죄송해** to another friend sounds like they are doing a bit.

| Say this | Romanization | To whom |
|---|---|---|
| **죄송합니다** | *joesonghamnida* | strangers, staff, elders, anyone at all |
| **죄송해요** | *joesonghaeyo* | polite but everyday — a neighbour, a familiar coworker |
| **미안합니다** | *mianhamnida* | formal in shape, rarely used to a real superior |
| **미안해요** | *mianhaeyo* | someone close but not a peer — a younger coworker |
| **미안해** | *mianhae* | friends, siblings, people younger than you |
| **사과드립니다** | *sagwadeurimnida* | written notices, official statements |

The step-down happens on the **미안** side of the family. **죄송해** exists on paper and nobody says it, the same way **감사해요** is technically possible and quietly avoided. So the honest rule for a visitor is short: use **죄송합니다** with everyone for the whole trip, and switch to **미안해** only with people you have started calling by name.

There is one more phrase worth recognizing even if you never need it: **잘못했어요** (*jalmothaesseoyo*), "I did wrong." That is not a scuffed shoe or a late arrival — it is an admission of actual fault, the phrase a child uses to a parent. Keep it out of your street vocabulary.

## 저기요, 잠시만요, 실례합니다 — the three that are not apologies

English collapses a lot of politeness into "sorry" and "excuse me," and this is where most travellers in Korea sound slightly off. Korean splits the job three ways.

**저기요** (*jeogiyo*) gets someone's attention. It is what you say to a server across a restaurant, to a shop worker stocking a shelf, to a stranger before you ask a question. Literally it means "over there," pointed politely at a person. In a restaurant **여기요** (*yeogiyo*), "over here," works the same way and is what most people actually call out at a busy table.

**잠시만요** (*jamsimanyo*) — or **잠깐만요** (*jamkkanmanyo*) — means "just a moment." This is the single most useful phrase on this page. It clears a path on a crowded subway, it holds someone still while you dig out your card, it buys you three seconds when a cashier asks a question you did not catch. Say it going through a crowd and people move without looking up.

**실례합니다** (*sillyehamnida*) means "pardon the rudeness." It is a notch more formal and belongs to the moment you interrupt something — stepping across a row of seated people, breaking into a conversation, entering an office. **실례지만** (*sillyejiman*), "excuse me, but," opens a question to a stranger.

You: **저기요, 이거 얼마예요?** — *jeogiyo, igeo eolmayeyo?* — "Excuse me, how much is this?"
Them: **아, 잠시만요.** — *a, jamsimanyo.* — "Ah, one moment."

And the thing nobody tells you: on a rush-hour Seoul subway, a light bump draws no apology at all, from anyone, in either direction. It is not rudeness — a packed car is understood as a place where contact is unavoidable. Apologizing for every brush marks you as a tourist more than staying quiet does. Save **죄송합니다** for when you actually land on someone's foot, and use **내릴게요** (*naerilgeyo*), "I'm getting off," to get to the door.

## Naming what you are sorry for: 늦어서 죄송합니다

A bare **죄송합니다** is complete, but attaching the reason is what makes it sound like a sentence instead of a reflex. Take the verb, attach **-아/어서** — the "because" ending, Pattern 35 in the SULSUL workbook — and finish with the apology.

- **늦어서 죄송합니다** — *neujeoseo joesonghamnida* — "Sorry I'm late."
- **기다리게 해서 죄송합니다** — *gidarige haeseo joesonghamnida* — "Sorry for making you wait."
- **연락이 늦어서 죄송합니다** — *yeollagi neujeoseo joesonghamnida* — "Sorry for the slow reply."
- **번거롭게 해서 죄송합니다** — *beongeorobge haeseo joesonghamnida* — "Sorry for the trouble."
- **한국어를 잘 못해서 죄송해요** — *hangugeoreul jal mothaeseo joesonghaeyo* — "Sorry my Korean isn't good."

Among friends the whole frame relaxes: **늦어서 미안해** (*neujeoseo mianhae*). The reason clause does not change — only the ending does.

The same **죄송** turns into a request softener with **-지만** or **-는데요**. **죄송하지만** (*joesonghajiman*) and **죄송한데요** (*joesonghandeyo*) both mean "sorry, but —" and they make the sentence that follows noticeably easier to say yes to. **죄송하지만 다시 말해 주세요** is the polite way to admit you missed something, which pairs with [what to say when Korean is too fast](/korean-phrases-when-korean-is-too-fast), and **죄송한데요, 화장실 어디예요?** is how most people open the question in [how to ask where the restroom is in Korean](/ask-restroom-korean).

## How it actually sounds

**죄송합니다** looks like six syllables of trouble and is mostly one tricky vowel. The **죄** is a rounded *jwe* — the "j" of "jam" with a quick "we" glued onto it — and in ordinary speech most Koreans flatten it toward *je* anyway, so *je-song-ham-ni-da* will pass anywhere. The **합니다** ending behaves exactly as it does in **감사합니다**: a **ㅂ** in front of a **ㄴ** becomes **ㅁ**, so it is *ham-ni-da*, never *hap-ni-da*.

**미안해요** has the opposite problem — it is easy to say and easy to rush. The **미안** is two clean syllables, *mi-an*, not "myan." Keep them separate and it sounds deliberate rather than tossed off.

As with gratitude, the body carries half the meaning. A small nod goes with **죄송합니다** to a stranger. A slightly deeper bend from the waist goes with a real apology to someone senior. Hands out of pockets, eyes down for a beat — the posture is what convinces, and it costs nothing to get right.

## When someone apologizes to you

The reply you want almost every time is **괜찮아요** (*gwaenchanayo*), "it's fine." It is the same word that answers "are you okay?", turns down an offer, and waves away trouble, and stretching the vowel a little makes it warm rather than clipped.

| Reply | Romanization | Use it when |
|---|---|---|
| **괜찮아요** | *gwaenchanayo* | the default "no worries" |
| **괜찮습니다** | *gwaenchanseumnida* | same thing, formal register |
| **아니에요** | *anieyo* | "not at all" — waving it off entirely |
| **별거 아니에요** | *byeolgeo anieyo* | "it's nothing" |

And one to have ready in the other direction: if you bump someone hard enough that they stumble, the useful sentence is not an apology but a question — **괜찮으세요?** (*gwaenchaneuseyo?*), "are you all right?" Checking lands better than apologizing.

## What usually goes wrong

**Using 죄송합니다 to call a server.** It reads as though you did something wrong, and the server will pause to figure out what. **저기요** or **여기요** is the phrase for getting attention.

**Saying 미안해 to anyone older or to staff.** It is casual speech pointed upward, which is the one direction Korean politeness does not forgive. **죄송합니다** to strangers, always.

**Apologizing for every subway bump.** Nobody else is, and doing it repeatedly turns a non-event into an event. **잠시만요** to move, silence for the rest.

**Reaching for 실례합니다 to squeeze past.** It is not wrong, just heavier than the moment. **잠시만요** is what people say.

**Piling apologies on top of each other.** Three **죄송해요** in a row does not read as three times sorrier — it reads as flustered, and it pulls the other person into reassuring you. One apology, then the reason, then move on.

**Saying 잘못했어요 for a small thing.** It means "I was in the wrong," and used over a spilled drink it lands far heavier than you intended.

**Skipping the nod.** A flat **죄송합니다** delivered with a still head reads as recited. The small bow is not decoration — it is the half of the apology people actually register. The same goes the other way in [how to say thank you in Korean](/how-to-say-thank-you-in-korean).
