A K-drama romance runs on about ten Korean words, and they arrive in the same order in almost every show: **첫눈에 반하다** (*cheonnune banhada*, to fall at first sight), **짝사랑** (*jjaksarang*, one-sided love), **썸** (*sseom*, the "something" before dating), **고백** (*gobaek*, the confession), **사귀자** (*sagwija*, "let's go out"), **연애** (*yeonae*, being in a relationship), and — usually around episode twelve — **헤어지자** (*heeojija*, "let's break up"). Once you know these, you can follow the plot by ear even when the subtitles smooth the words over. Below is each stage, the line characters actually say, and which ones work outside the screen.

> - **반하다** (*banhada*) — to fall for someone. 첫눈에 반했어 = "I fell for you at first sight."
> - **짝사랑** (*jjaksarang*) — loving someone who does not (yet) love you back.
> - **썸** (*sseom*) — the flirting stage before anyone has said anything official. 썸 타다 = to be in that stage.
> - **고백** (*gobaek*) — the confession. 고백하다 = to confess.
> - **사귀다** (*sagwida*) — to date, to be a couple. 우리 사귀자 = "Let's go out."
> - **연애** (*yeonae*) — dating life, a relationship. 연애하다 = to be in one.
> - **헤어지다** (*heeojida*) — to break up, and also simply to part ways for the night.
> - **다시 만나다** (*dasi mannada*) — to get back together. Literally "meet again."

## Why Korean names every stage

English mostly has "dating" and "not dating." Korean gives the in-between its own word, and K-drama writers build whole episodes around the gap between stages. When a friend asks **너네 사귀어?** (*neone sagwieo?*, "Are you two dating?") and the answer is **아니, 그냥 썸이야** (*ani, geunyang sseomiya*, "No, it's just a 썸 thing"), the audience knows exactly where the couple is: interested, flirting, nothing promised. The tension of the next three episodes comes from that single word.

This is also why the confession scene matters so much. In Korean dating culture, a relationship usually starts on a specific day with a specific sentence. Couples count their days together from that day — **100일** (*baegil*, the 100-day anniversary) is a real celebration, and dramas use it as a plot point. Without a 고백, there is no day one.

## Before anyone says anything: 반하다 and 짝사랑

The first stage is one-way. **반하다** is the moment something clicks — usually a slow-motion shot and an OST cue.

- **첫눈에 반했어.** (*cheonnune banhaesseo*) — "I fell for you at first sight."
- **나 저 사람한테 반한 것 같아.** (*na jeo saramhante banhan geot gata*) — "I think I've fallen for that person."

If it stays one-way, it becomes **짝사랑**. The second lead almost always lives here. You will hear **짝사랑 중이야** (*jjaksarang jungiya*, "I'm in a one-sided love") said to a best friend over soju, and the sadder **나만 좋아했나 봐** (*naman joahaenna bwa*, "I guess it was only me who liked you").

Both words are completely normal in real conversation. Telling a Korean friend **요즘 짝사랑 중이에요** gets you sympathy and a lot of questions.

## The flirting stage: what 썸 really means

**썸** comes from the English word "something," as in "there's something between them." It is the stage of late-night messages, "accidental" meetings, and sharing umbrellas. The verb is **썸 타다** (*sseom tada*) — literally "to ride the something."

- **우리 썸 타는 거 맞지?** (*uri sseom taneun geo matji?*) — "We're in a 썸, right?"
- **썸만 3개월째야.** (*sseomman sam gaewoljjaeya*) — "It's been three months of just 썸."

A person you are in this stage with is your **썸남** (*sseomnam*, 썸 + man) or **썸녀** (*sseomnyeo*, 썸 + woman). These are casual, young-sounding words — fine with friends, odd in a job interview.

## The confession: 고백 and the lines that start it

**고백** is the hinge of the whole romance. Characters say it plainly, because in Korean the plain version is the brave version:

- **좋아해.** (*joahae*) — "I like you." This is the classic first confession, not 사랑해. Saying 사랑해 on day one sounds too heavy.
- **나랑 사귈래?** (*narang sagwillae?*) — "Will you go out with me?"
- **우리 사귀자.** (*uri sagwija*) — "Let's go out." More decisive; the male lead's favourite.
- **오늘부터 1일이야.** (*oneulbuteo iririya*) — "From today, it's day one." The line that makes the relationship official.

That last one is very Korean and very real. People really do say 오늘부터 1일, and it tells you the counting has started.

The answers are short too: **나도 좋아해** (*nado joahae*, "I like you too") for yes, and **미안해, 난 친구로 지내고 싶어** (*mianhae, nan chinguro jinaego sipeo*, "Sorry, I want to stay friends") for the second lead's heartbreak.

## Inside the relationship: 연애 and couple talk

Once they are together, the words shift from feelings to routine. **연애** is the relationship itself: **연애한 지 얼마나 됐어?** (*yeonaehan ji eolmana dwaesseo?*, "How long have you been dating?"). The partner becomes **남자친구** / **여자친구**, shortened in speech to **남친** (*namchin*) and **여친** (*yeochin*).

Couple scenes are full of check-in lines: **보고 싶었어** (*bogo sipeosseo*, "I missed you"), **집에 잘 들어갔어?** (*jibe jal deureogasseo?*, "Did you get home safe?"), and **밥 먹었어?** (*bap meogeosseo?*, "Did you eat?") — which, in a romance, means "I'm thinking about you."

## The breakup and the reunion

Act three usually brings **헤어지자** (*heeojija*, "Let's break up"). The harder version is **우리 그만하자** (*uri geumanhaja*, "Let's stop this") — it sounds tired rather than angry, which is why it hurts more in the scene. The one who leaves often says **잘 지내** (*jal jinae*, "Take care"), and the camera holds on the one who stays.

Be careful with 헤어지다 in real life: it also means simply "to part ways," as in **역 앞에서 헤어졌어요** ("We split up in front of the station"). Context does the work — nobody thinks you broke up with your coworker.

Then, by the finale, **다시 만나자** (*dasi mannaja*, "Let's get back together") or **다시 시작하자** (*dasi sijakaja*, "Let's start again"). The reunion line is almost always quiet.

## Four mistakes fans make with romance words

**Using 사랑해 too early.** In dramas, 사랑해 is the payoff, not the opener. Start with 좋아해, or [read how Korean splits 좋아해 and 사랑해](/korean-words-for-love).

**Using 썸 as a noun for a person.** 썸 is the situation. The person is your 썸남 or 썸녀.

**Dropping 요 with someone you just met.** Every line above is in casual speech because drama couples are close. To someone you are not yet close to, add 요: **좋아해요**, **사귈래요?** The [guide to polite Korean](/how-to-be-polite-in-korean) explains when to switch.

**Copying titles as pet names.** 오빠 in a romance is a relationship position, not a cute word — see [what 오빠 really means](/what-does-oppa-mean-in-korean) before you use it.
