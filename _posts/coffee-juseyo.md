"Coffee juseyo" means "Coffee, please." Written in Korean, it's **커피 주세요**, and it sounds like *keo-pi ju-se-yo*. It's correct and polite, so you can say it to anyone serving you. Just expect one more question at a Korean café, because **커피** covers the whole menu and the barista still needs to know which drink you want. The line that usually ends the conversation faster is **아메리카노 한 잔 주세요** (*a-me-ri-ka-no han jan ju-se-yo*), "One Americano, please."

> **Coffee juseyo, the short version**
> - Meaning: "Coffee, please"
> - In Korean: **커피 주세요** — *keo-pi ju-se-yo*
> - Politeness: fine for cafés, restaurants, and shops
> - Likely follow-up: **어떤 커피로 드릴까요?** — "Which coffee would you like?"
> - Faster order: **아메리카노 한 잔 주세요** — "One Americano, please"

## What each word is doing

**커피** (*keo-pi*) is the English word "coffee," borrowed into Korean. **주세요** (*ju-se-yo*) is the request form of "to give," so the whole line is literally "coffee, please give." It's Pattern 01 in the SULSUL workbook, **(N) 주세요**, and it's about the most useful sentence a traveler can learn: put any noun in front, and you've made a polite request.

The word order is the part that feels backward at first. The thing you want comes first and the request comes last. You don't need a word for "I," and you don't need the object particle either. Grammar books would give you **커피를 주세요**, but at a counter people almost always drop the **를**. For more on the pattern itself, read [how to say please give me in Korean](/how-to-say-please-give-me-in-korean).

## Why it isn't quite "coffee" out loud

If you've only seen this phrase in Latin letters, the sound is where you can trip. Korean has no F sound, so "coffee" became *keo-pi*. Here's what to aim for, sound by sound:

| Written | Say it like | What to watch |
|---|---|---|
| 커 (*keo*) | "kuh" | A K with a puff of air, and a relaxed "uh," not "caw" |
| 피 (*pi*) | "pee" | A P with a puff of air, never an F |
| 주 (*ju*) | "joo" | A soft J that can sound close to "choo" |
| 세 (*se*) | "seh" | Short, like "set" without the T, not "say" |
| 요 (*yo*) | "yo" | Short and clean, not "yoh-oo" |

That soft ㅈ explains the spelling mess you'll find online. *Juseyo*, *jusaeyo*, *joo-say-yo*, and *chuseyo* are all trying to write the same word, and Korean speakers would only ever write **주세요**. If a barista hears you say "coffee" with an F, they'll almost certainly still understand. But the Korean sound will carry better in a loud café, and it's a small change that makes the rest of your sentence easier to catch.

Keep the end of the sentence level or falling. If your voice rises on **요**, it can sound like you're asking whether they'd like some coffee.

## The question you'll probably get back

Most cafés in Korea don't have one default "coffee" waiting in a pot. Espresso drinks are the norm, and the closest thing to plain coffee is the Americano. So a short exchange often goes like this:

> **You:** 커피 주세요.
> **Barista:** 어떤 커피로 드릴까요?
> **You:** 아메리카노요. 한 잔 주세요.
> **Barista:** 따뜻한 걸로 드릴까요, 아이스로 드릴까요?
> **You:** 아이스로 주세요.

**어떤 커피로 드릴까요?** (*eo-tteon keo-pi-ro deu-ril-kka-yo?*) means "Which coffee shall I get you?" **아메리카노요** (*a-me-ri-ka-no-yo*) is a quick, polite one-word answer, since adding **요** to a noun is enough at a counter. Asking for drip coffee is a good idea only if you see it on the menu, listed as **드립 커피** (*deu-rip keo-pi*) or, at many chains, **오늘의 커피** (*o-neu-rui keo-pi*), "today's coffee."

Here's the short menu you can swap into the sentence:

| On the menu | Say it | What you get |
|---|---|---|
| 아메리카노 | *a-me-ri-ka-no* | Espresso and water, the everyday order |
| 카페라테 | *ka-pe-ra-te* | Latte |
| 바닐라 라테 | *ba-nil-la ra-te* | Vanilla latte, usually sweet |
| 카푸치노 | *ka-pu-chi-no* | Cappuccino |
| 믹스커피 | *mik-seu-keo-pi* | Sweet instant coffee with cream and sugar |

The last one is worth knowing. If an older person offers you **커피**, or you find a free machine by the door of a neighborhood restaurant, it's often **믹스커피**. Don't expect an espresso drink. The order-to-pickup flow at a café, including size and for-here or to-go, is laid out in [what to say when ordering coffee in Korean](/ordering-coffee-in-korean).

## Where plain 커피 주세요 works as is

The short line suits places with only one coffee on offer. A diner, a guesthouse breakfast, or a restaurant serving coffee after a meal may need nothing more, and there **커피 좀 주세요** (*keo-pi jom ju-se-yo*) sounds a little softer. The syllable **좀** works like "a bit" or "if you could," and it takes the edge off any request.

To check first whether they have it at all, use Pattern 03: **커피 있어요?** (*keo-pi i-sseo-yo?*), "Do you have coffee?"

![A Korean convenience store with a self-serve coffee machine on the back counter](/assets/blog/scenes/convenience-store.jpg)

Convenience stores are the other case travelers run into, and they work differently. You usually don't ask a person to make your coffee. Instead, you pick up a cup or a bagged drink, pay at the counter, and then use the machine yourself. Stores vary, so these are good lines to have ready:

- **아이스 컵 어디 있어요?** (*a-i-seu keop eo-di i-sseo-yo?*) — "Where are the iced cups?"
- **커피 머신 어떻게 써요?** (*keo-pi meo-sin eo-tteo-ke sseo-yo?*) — "How do I use the coffee machine?"
- **따뜻한 커피 있어요?** (*tta-tteu-tan keo-pi i-sseo-yo?*) — "Do you have hot coffee?"

More on that counter is in the guide to [Korean phrases for the convenience store](/korean-phrases-convenience-store).

## Everything else you can ask for the same way

Once you've got your drink, the same pattern gets you the extras. Only the noun changes:

- **빨대 주세요.** (*ppal-dae ju-se-yo*) — "A straw, please."
- **얼음 더 주세요.** (*eo-reum deo ju-se-yo*) — "More ice, please."
- **뚜껑 주세요.** (*ttu-kkeong ju-se-yo*) — "A lid, please."
- **냅킨 좀 주세요.** (*naep-kin jom ju-se-yo*) — "Some napkins, please."
- **영수증 주세요.** (*yeong-su-jeung ju-se-yo*) — "The receipt, please."

To change the strength rather than ask for a thing, use Pattern 23, **(V)아/어 주세요**: **연하게 해 주세요** (*yeon-ha-ge hae ju-se-yo*) means "Please make it weak," and **진하게 해 주세요** (*jin-ha-ge hae ju-se-yo*) means "Please make it strong."

## When you're not asking a barista

Coffee comes up outside the counter too, and **주세요** isn't the right tool for those moments:

- **커피 마시고 싶어요.** (*keo-pi ma-si-go si-peo-yo*) — "I want to drink some coffee." This is Pattern 02, for telling a friend what you feel like.
- **커피 한잔 할래요?** (*keo-pi han-jan hal-lae-yo?*) — "Want to grab a coffee?" This is Pattern 25, and it's the usual friendly invitation.
- **커피 드릴까요?** (*keo-pi deu-ril-kka-yo?*) — "Would you like some coffee?" You'll hear this one as a guest, and you can answer **네, 감사합니다** (*ne, gam-sa-ham-ni-da*), "Yes, thank you."

## Mistakes people make with this phrase

**Using 줘 because a K-drama did.** You may hear **커피 줘** (*keo-pi jwo*) on screen. That's casual speech between close friends or family, and it comes across as rude to a barista. Stick with **주세요**.

**Saying "say" in 주세요.** *Joo-say-yo* is the most common English-speaker version. Shorten the middle to *seh* and the whole word gets quicker and more natural.

**Counting with the wrong numbers.** Cups take native Korean numbers: **한 잔** (*han jan*), **두 잔** (*du jan*), **세 잔** (*se jan*). Saying **일 잔** for one cup will sound off. The full system is in [Korean numbers for paying and counting](/korean-numbers-paying-counting-korea).

**Stopping when the barista asks something back.** Follow-up questions don't mean you got it wrong. They're a normal part of the order, and a one-word answer plus **요** is all you need.

## Make it automatic before your first café

**커피 주세요** takes two seconds to read. Saying it at a busy counter, with a line behind you and a question coming back at full speed, is harder. Say **아메리카노 한 잔 주세요** out loud a few times, then **아이스로 주세요**, until your mouth does it without you translating first.
