The basic Korean phrases you will actually use every day are not long sentences. They are five or six short lines that fill the gaps between bigger conversations: **괜찮아요** (*gwaenchanayo*), "it's okay"; **잠깐만요** (*jamkkanmanyo*), "one moment"; **알겠습니다** (*algetseumnida*), "got it"; **수고하세요** (*sugohaseyo*), the "take care" you say to anyone who is working; and **다녀오겠습니다** (*danyeoogetseumnida*), "I'm heading out." Learn those, plus the reaction **아, 그래요?** (*a, geuraeyo?*), and you can get through a normal Korean day without freezing in the small moments.

> - It's okay / No thanks / Are you okay? — **괜찮아요** (*gwaenchanayo*)
> - One moment / Excuse me, coming through — **잠깐만요** (*jamkkanmanyo*)
> - Got it — **알겠습니다** / **알겠어요** (*algetseumnida / algesseoyo*)
> - I'm not sure — **잘 모르겠어요** (*jal moreugesseoyo*)
> - Leaving a shop or office — **수고하세요** (*sugohaseyo*)
> - Leaving home — **다녀오겠습니다** (*danyeoogetseumnida*)
> - Coming home — **다녀왔습니다** (*danyeowatseumnida*)
> - I'm on my way — **지금 가는 길이에요** (*jigeum ganeun girieyo*)
> - Oh, really? — **아, 그래요?** (*a, geuraeyo?*)
> - That's a relief — **다행이에요** (*dahaengieyo*)
> - See you later — **이따 봐요** (*itta bwayo*)
> - Good night — **잘 자요** (*jal jayo*)

## Why these phrases come first

A beginner's list usually starts with hello, thank you, and sorry. Those matter, and they each have their own guide — [how to say how are you](/how-to-say-how-are-you-in-korean) and [how to say goodbye](/how-to-say-goodbye-in-korean) among them. But the words that fill a day are the ones in between. Someone holds a door, someone asks if you need a bag, someone is blocking the subway exit. In each of those moments, a Korean speaker says one short line without thinking, and a learner stands there building a sentence.

Most of these lines also do more than one job. That is what makes them worth learning first: one phrase, several situations.

## 괜찮아요 — the phrase with four meanings

**괜찮아요** literally means "it is fine," and the situation decides the rest:

| Situation | 괜찮아요 means | Tone |
|---|---|---|
| Cashier: "Do you need a bag?" | "No thanks." | Flat, with a small head shake |
| Someone bumps you and apologizes | "It's okay, don't worry." | Soft, quick |
| A friend trips on the stairs | "Are you okay?" — **괜찮아요?** | Rising, worried |
| Someone asks how the food is | "It's fine / pretty good." | Neutral |

The second meaning, the polite refusal, is the one that trips visitors up. When a convenience store clerk asks **봉투 필요하세요?** (*bongtu piryohaseyo?*), "Do you need a bag?", **괜찮아요** means no. It does not mean "yes, that would be fine." If you do want one, say **네, 주세요** (*ne, juseyo*). That is Pattern 01, **(N) 주세요**, doing its usual work. More of the checkout script is in [Korean phrases for the convenience store](/korean-phrases-convenience-store).

With friends, drop the 요: **괜찮아** (*gwaenchana*). As a question to a close friend, **괜찮아?**, it is one of the most common lines in any K-drama.

## 잠깐만요 and the phrases for small moments

**잠깐만요** means "just a moment," and Koreans use it in two very different places. On the phone or at a counter, it means "hold on, let me check." In a crowded subway car, said while you move toward the door, it means "excuse me, coming through." Nobody will think it rude; it is the normal way to get past people.

Two more short lines handle the moments when you do not fully understand. Both come from the textbook's conversation-help patterns:

- **잘 모르겠어요** (*jal moreugesseoyo*) — "I'm not sure / I don't really know." Pattern 21. Softer than a plain "I don't know," so it works with strangers.
- **다시 말해 주세요** (*dasi malhae juseyo*) — "Please say it again." Pattern 22. Add **천천히** (*cheoncheonhi*), "slowly," if the speed was the problem. If that happens often, [Korean phrases for when someone speaks too fast](/korean-phrases-when-korean-is-too-fast) goes further.

And when you did understand, **알겠습니다** (*algetseumnida*) is "understood" — the formal version you say to staff, a teacher, or someone older. **알겠어요** (*algesseoyo*) is the everyday polite version. **네** (*ne*) on its own works as a nod in sound form.

## Leaving and coming back: the greetings Koreans say at the door

Korean has fixed lines for leaving and returning, and English has no real match for them.

**다녀오겠습니다** (*danyeoogetseumnida*) means roughly "I will go and come back." You say it to whoever stays home when you leave in the morning. They answer **잘 다녀와요** (*jal danyeowayo*), "go and come back safe." When you return, you say **다녀왔습니다** (*danyeowatseumnida*), "I'm back," and the reply is **왔어요?** (*wasseoyo?*), "you're here?" Homestay families and Korean roommates notice when you use these, because it shows you understand how a Korean household works.

**수고하세요** (*sugohaseyo*) is the line for leaving a place where someone is working: a shop, a hair salon, a taxi, an office. It means something like "keep up the good work." At the end of a workday, colleagues say **수고하셨습니다** (*sugohasyeotseumnida*), "thank you for your hard work today." That is part of Pattern 91, the comfort-and-encouragement family that also includes **힘내세요** (*himnaeseyo*), "hang in there."

One caution: **수고하세요** sounds like you are speaking down to the person if you say it to your boss or a much older person. To them, say **감사합니다** or **안녕히 계세요** instead.

## Texting through the day: on my way, see you, good night

Pattern 93 is built for daily routines: **지금 (V) 가는/오는 길이에요**, "I'm on my way to / from (doing)." It is the first line most Koreans type when they are running late.

- **지금 가는 길이에요.** — *jigeum ganeun girieyo.* — "I'm on my way now."
- **퇴근하는 길이에요.** — *toegeunhaneun girieyo.* — "I'm on my way home from work."
- **벌써 먹었어요.** — *beolsseo meogeosseoyo.* — "I already ate." (The other half of Pattern 93.)

That last one answers the most Korean question of all: **밥 먹었어요?** (*bap meogeosseoyo?*), "Did you eat?" It is a greeting, not an invitation, so "yes, I did" is a complete answer.

To close the day: **이따 봐요** (*itta bwayo*), "see you later (today)"; **내일 봐요** (*naeil bwayo*), "see you tomorrow"; **잘 자요** (*jal jayo*), "sleep well." To someone older, **안녕히 주무세요** (*annyeonghi jumuseyo*) is the respectful good night.

## A normal day in six short lines

Here is how these phrases sound strung together. You are staying with a Korean host family.

**You (leaving):** 다녀오겠습니다! — *danyeoogetseumnida!* — "I'm heading out!"
**Host mom:** 잘 다녀와요. 밥 먹었어요? — *jal danyeowayo. bap meogeosseoyo?* — "Have a good day. Did you eat?"
**You:** 네, 벌써 먹었어요. — *ne, beolsseo meogeosseoyo.* — "Yes, I already ate."

At the convenience store:

**Clerk:** 봉투 필요하세요? — *bongtu piryohaseyo?* — "Do you need a bag?"
**You:** 괜찮아요. 수고하세요. — *gwaenchanayo. sugohaseyo.* — "No thanks. Have a good day."

Texting a friend at 6:58 for a 7:00 dinner:

**You:** 미안해요, 지금 가는 길이에요! — *mianhaeyo, jigeum ganeun girieyo!* — "Sorry, I'm on my way!"
**Friend:** 괜찮아요. 천천히 와요. — *gwaenchanayo. cheoncheonhi wayo.* — "No worries. Take your time."

Back home that night:

**You:** 다녀왔습니다. — *danyeowatseumnida.* — "I'm back."
**Host mom:** 왔어요? 오늘 어땠어요? — *wasseoyo? oneul eottaesseoyo?* — "You're home? How was today?"
**You:** 좋았어요. 그럼 안녕히 주무세요. — *joasseoyo. geureom annyeonghi jumuseyo.* — "It was good. Good night, then."

## Mistakes learners make with everyday Korean

- **Saying 괜찮아요 when you mean yes.** If the clerk offers a bag, a receipt, or a straw, 괜찮아요 is a no. Say **네, 주세요** to accept.
- **Using 수고하세요 upward.** Fine for staff and equals, awkward toward your boss or an elderly host. Swap in **감사합니다**.
- **Answering 밥 먹었어요? with your menu.** It is closer to "how are you" than a real question. **네, 먹었어요** and a question back — **밥 먹었어요?** — is enough.
- **Dropping 요 too early.** **괜찮아**, **알겠어**, and **잘 자** are for close friends and people younger than you. With anyone new, keep the 요. [Korean small talk phrases](/korean-small-talk-phrases) covers how that shift usually happens.
- **Overusing 진짜요?** It is a great reaction, but **아, 그래요?** is calmer and fits more situations. The difference is in [how to ask "really" in Korean](/how-to-ask-really-in-korean).

## Say them until they come out on their own

Everyday phrases only help if they come out fast. Nobody waits while you build **잠깐만요** in your head in the middle of a subway crowd.
