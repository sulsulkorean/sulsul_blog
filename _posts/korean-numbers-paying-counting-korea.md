Korean numbers for paying and counting use two systems. Sino-Korean **일, 이, 삼** (*il, i, sam*) handle money, phone digits, and minutes. Native Korean **하나, 둘, 셋** (*hana, dul, set*) count items and hours. At a cafe counter, **커피 두 잔 주세요** (*keopi du jan juseyo*) orders two coffees; at checkout, **이천 원이에요** (*icheon wonieyo*) means two thousand won.

> - Count items (cups, people, bags): Native Korean — **두 잔** (*du jan*), "two cups," not **이 잔**.
> - Say prices and large amounts: Sino-Korean — **천 원** (*cheon won*), **만 원** (*man won*).
> - Tell the hour: Native — **세 시** (*se si*), "three o'clock."
> - Tell the minutes: Sino — **삼십 분** (*samsip bun*), "thirty minutes."
> - Ask the total: **얼마예요?** (*eolmayeyo?*), "How much is it?"

## When do you use Sino-Korean numbers?

Sino-Korean numbers work like math blocks. **십** (*sip*, ten) plus **일** (*il*, one) makes **십일** (*sibil*, eleven). You hear them for money, dates, phone numbers, and minutes.

You: **이거 얼마예요?** — *igeo eolmayeyo?* — "How much is this?"  
Them: **삼천 원이에요.** — *samcheon wonieyo.* — "It's three thousand won."  
You: **카드 되요?** — *kadeu dwaeyo?* — "Do you accept card?"

| Situation | Say this |
|---|---|
| Ask the price | 얼마예요? (*eolmayeyo?*) |
| Three thousand won | 삼천 원 (*samcheon won*) |
| Ten thousand won | 만 원 (*man won*) |
| 3:30 on the clock | 세 시 삼십 분 (*se si samsip bun*) |

**천** (*cheon*) is one thousand and **만** (*man*) is ten thousand — the two units you hear most at markets and convenience stores.

## When do you use Native Korean numbers?

Native Korean numbers count things in front of you: cups, people, age, and hours. **하나** becomes **한**, **둘** becomes **두**, **셋** becomes **세**, and **넷** becomes **네** when a counter word follows.

**아메리카노 두 잔 주세요** (*amerikano du jan juseyo*) — "Two Americanos, please."  
**사람 세 명** (*saram se myeong*) — "Three people."

## What usually goes wrong at the register?

The classic mix-up is using Sino-Korean to count items — saying **이 개** (*i gae*) instead of **두 개** (*du gae*). Cashiers still understand you, but it marks you as a beginner instantly.

What helps: memorize one ordering line with a counter (**두 잔**, **한 개**) and one price line in Sino-Korean (**오천 원** (*ocheon won*), **만 이천 원** (*man icheon won*)). For teller-side numbers, see [how to exchange currency at a bank in Korea](/exchange-currency-bank-korea).

## How do hours and minutes combine both systems?

Hours use Native Korean; minutes use Sino-Korean. **다섯 시** (*daseot si*) is five o'clock; add **십오 분** (*sibo bun*) for 5:15.

You: **지금 몇 시예요?** — *jigeum myeot siyeyo?* — "What time is it now?"  
Them: **두 시 삼십 분이에요.** — *du si samsip bunieyo.* — "It's 2:30."  
You: **아, 감사합니다.** — *a, gamsahamnida.* — "Oh, thank you."
