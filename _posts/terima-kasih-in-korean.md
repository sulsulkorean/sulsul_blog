**Terima kasih** in Korean is **감사합니다** (*gam-sa-ham-ni-da*) when you speak politely, and **고마워** (*go-ma-wo*) when you talk to a close friend or someone younger. In between sits **고마워요** (*go-ma-wo-yo*), the soft polite version you hear most in K-dramas. All three mean the same thing — the only question is who you are talking to.

> - **감사합니다** — terima kasih (banyak) — to strangers, staff, elders
> - **고마워요** — terima kasih — polite but warm
> - **고마워** — makasih — to friends, younger siblings
> - **아니에요** — sama-sama — "it's nothing"

If you already speak Bahasa Indonesia, you have a head start on more than thank you. Below: how to pick the right thank you, how to answer one, the family words that match **kakak** and **adik**, the reactions you hear in every episode, and the one place Indonesian grammar will not help you at all.

## Which thank you to say: three levels, one meaning

Indonesian already switches register. You say **terima kasih, Bu** to a teacher and **makasih ya** to a friend. Korean does the same, except the change happens at the end of the word, not in the words around it.

| Korean | Romanized | Indonesian feel | Say it to |
|---|---|---|---|
| **감사합니다** | gam-sa-ham-ni-da | Terima kasih banyak, Pak | a shop clerk, a taxi driver, your boss, anyone older you don't know |
| **고마워요** | go-ma-wo-yo | Terima kasih | a coworker, a friendly neighbor, someone your age you just met |
| **고마워** | go-ma-wo | Makasih ya | a best friend, a younger sibling, a partner |

When in doubt, use **감사합니다**. Nobody in Korea is offended by too much politeness. The opposite is the problem: saying **고마워** to the grandmother who sold you tteokbokki sounds like you are talking down to her.

To make it bigger, add **정말** (*jeong-mal*, "really"): **정말 감사합니다** is your **terima kasih banyak sekali**. In dramas you also hear **진짜 고마워** (*jin-jja go-ma-wo*) between friends — "makasih banget."

## How to answer: the Korean sama-sama

This is where Indonesian speakers often get stuck. **Sama-sama** is automatic in Indonesian. Korean has a textbook word for "you're welcome," **천만에요** (*cheon-ma-ne-yo*), but people almost never say it out loud. It sounds stiff, like something from a language tape.

What Koreans actually say:

- **아니에요** (*a-ni-e-yo*) — "Oh, it's nothing." The most common reply by far.
- **별말씀을요** (*byeol-mal-sseu-meul-lyo*) — "Don't mention it." A little more polite.
- **뭘요** (*mwol-lyo*) — "What for?" Casual and warm.
- **아니야** (*a-ni-ya*) — the casual version, between friends.

So the drama scene goes like this:

> **A:** 오늘 도와줘서 정말 고마워요. (*Thank you so much for helping today.*)
> **B:** 아니에요. 다음에 또 불러요. (*It's nothing. Call me again next time.*)

Notice that B does not say "you're welcome." She waves the thanks away — the same feeling as **ah, nggak apa-apa** in Indonesian.

## Kakak, adik, Pak, Bu: the family words you already use

Indonesian uses **kakak** for any older sibling and **adik** for any younger one, then stretches them to people who are not family at all. Korean does the same stretching, but the older-sibling word depends on **who is speaking**.

| Korean | Who says it | Indonesian | English |
|---|---|---|---|
| **언니** (*eon-ni*) | a girl, to an older woman | kakak (perempuan), mbak | older sister |
| **누나** (*nu-na*) | a boy, to an older woman | kakak (perempuan), mbak | older sister |
| **오빠** (*o-ppa*) | a girl, to an older man | kakak (laki-laki), abang, mas | older brother |
| **형** (*hyeong*) | a boy, to an older man | kakak (laki-laki), abang, mas | older brother |
| **동생** (*dong-saeng*) | anyone, about someone younger | adik | younger sibling |

So when the heroine calls the lead **오빠**, think of an Indonesian girl calling her boyfriend **abang** or **mas** — affectionate, a little sweet, and only from her side.

For **Pak** and **Bu**, Korean adds **님** (*nim*) to a title. **선생님** (*seon-saeng-nim*) is a teacher or doctor, **사장님** (*sa-jang-nim*) is a boss or shop owner. A K-drama character who walks into a small restaurant and says **사장님, 여기 하나 더요!** is doing exactly what you do when you call **Bu, tambah satu ya!**

## Udah makan? The lines that sound like home

Some K-drama lines translate into Indonesian almost word for word. These are the ones you hear in nearly every episode:

- **밥 먹었어?** (*bap meo-geo-sseo?*) — **Udah makan belum?** In both cultures, this is less a question about food and more "I care about you."
- **진짜?** (*jin-jja?*) — **Masa sih?** / **Serius?**
- **아이고** (*a-i-go*) — **Aduh** / **Ya ampun.** The sigh a drama mom lets out before a lecture.
- **세상에** (*se-sang-e*) — **Ya Tuhan** / **Astaga.** Pure shock.
- **괜찮아?** (*gwaen-cha-na?*) — **Kamu nggak apa-apa?**
- **화이팅!** (*hwa-i-ting!*) — **Semangat!** The cheer you shout before an exam or a match.
- **미안해** (*mi-an-hae*) — **Maaf ya.** The polite form is **죄송합니다** (*joe-song-ham-ni-da*), "mohon maaf."
- **잘 먹겠습니다** (*jal meok-ge-sseum-ni-da*) — said before eating, to thank whoever cooked or paid. Indonesian has no exact match, but it plays the role **makasih, ya** does when someone treats you.

And the romance lines: **사랑해** (*sa-rang-hae*) is **aku sayang kamu** / **aku cinta kamu**, and **자기야** (*ja-gi-ya*) is the couple's **sayang**.

## Where Indonesian won't help: the verb goes last

Here is the honest catch. Indonesian puts the verb in the middle, like English: **Saya makan nasi.** Korean puts it at the very end.

> **저는 밥을 먹어요.**
> I — rice — eat.

That is why **감사합니다** feels like one long word: the "thank" and the polite ending are glued together at the end of the sentence. Small markers such as **은/는** and **을/를** tell you who is doing what, so you can stop translating word by word and wait for the last word to know what is happening.

The other catch is politeness. Indonesian makes politeness with word choice — **Anda** or **kamu**, **saya** or **aku**. Korean does it with endings: **-요** and **-습니다** turn any casual line polite. Learn to hear the ending, and you will know instantly whether two drama characters are close or strangers.

## Practice your thank yous out loud

Knowing that **고마워** means **makasih** takes a second. Saying **감사합니다** smoothly to a real person — without freezing on *ham-ni-da* — takes a few days of saying it out loud.
