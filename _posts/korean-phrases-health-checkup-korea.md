A **건강검진** (*geonganggeomjin*, health checkup) is not something you go looking for in Korea — it arrives. If you are on national health insurance or working at a Korean company, a notice turns up telling you it is your year, and a morning later you are moving down a corridor from one room to the next, being handed to a new person every few minutes. Nobody explains the order, because everyone else already knows it. Two patterns from the SULSUL workbook are what let you ask. Pattern 37 — **-기 전에** — covers everything you need to know beforehand: **검사하기 전에 물 마셔도 돼요?** (*geomsahagi jeone mul masyeodo dwaeyo?*), "Can I drink water before the test?" Pattern 38 — **-ㄴ/은 후에 / -고 나서** — covers everything that comes after: **검사 끝나고 나서 밥 먹어도 돼요?** (*geomsa kkeunnago naseo bap meogeodo dwaeyo?*), "Can I eat after the tests are done?"

> - Say why you came: **건강검진 받으러 왔어요** (*geonganggeomjin badeureo wasseoyo*), "I came for a health checkup."
> - Ask what is off limits: **검사하기 전에 뭐 하면 안 돼요?** (*geomsahagi jeone mwo hamyeon an dwaeyo?*), "What am I not allowed to do before the test?"
> - Ask about water: **검사하기 전에 물 마셔도 돼요?** (*geomsahagi jeone mul masyeodo dwaeyo?*), "Can I drink water before the test?"
> - Ask about eating: **검사 끝나고 나서 밥 먹어도 돼요?** (*geomsa kkeunnago naseo bap meogeodo dwaeyo?*), "Can I eat after the tests are done?"
> - Ask about results: **검사한 후에 결과 언제 나와요?** (*geomsahan hue gyeolgwa eonje nawayo?*), "When do the results come out after the test?"

## What do you say at the reception desk?

The morning starts at **접수** (*jeopsu*), reception, and it is quick: your **신분증** (*sinbunjeung*, ID) goes across the counter and a **문진표** (*munjinpyo*) comes back — the questionnaire about how much you drink, smoke, sleep and move, written in Korean only. Fill it in before the queue behind you builds.

Them: **어떤 검진 받으세요?** — *eotteon geomjin badeuseyo?* — "Which checkup are you here for?"  
You: **건강검진 받으러 왔어요.** — *geonganggeomjin badeureo wasseoyo.* — "I came for a health checkup."  
Them: **네, 신분증 주시겠어요? 문진표 먼저 작성해 주세요.** — *ne, sinbunjeung jusigesseoyo? munjinpyo meonjeo jakseonghae juseyo.* — "Sure, could I have your ID? Please fill in the questionnaire first."  
You: **검사하기 전에 뭐 하면 안 돼요?** — *geomsahagi jeone mwo hamyeon an dwaeyo?* — "What am I not allowed to do before the test?"

Pattern 37 is the easiest shape in the whole workbook, because there is nothing to conjugate: take any verb stem and add **-기 전에**, batchim or not. **먹기 전에** (before eating), **가기 전에** (before going), **자기 전에** (before sleeping). After a noun you drop the **-기** and use plain **전에** — **검사 전에** (*geomsa jeone*), **식사 전에** (*siksa jeone*). The one you will need before you even leave the house is **검진 받기 전에 예약해야 해요?** (*geomjin batgi jeone yeyakhaeya haeyo?*), "Do I have to book before getting the checkup?" — at most centres the answer is yes, and the busy months fill up early.

## How do you ask what happens after each test?

A checkup is a sequence, and you are the only person in the building who does not know it. **채혈** (*chaehyeol*) is the blood draw, **소변 검사** (*sobyeon geomsa*) the urine test, **흉부 엑스레이** (*hyungbu ekseurei*) the chest X-ray, and **위내시경** (*winaesigyeong*) the gastroscopy. Pattern 38 is how you ask what comes next without guessing.

You: **채혈하고 나서 어디로 가요?** — *chaehyeolhago naseo eodiro gayo?* — "Where do I go after the blood draw?"  
Them: **그럼 채혈부터 하고 나서 엑스레이 찍을게요.** — *geureom chaehyeolbuteo hago naseo ekseurei jjigeulgeyo.* — "We'll do the blood draw first and then the X-ray."  
You: **내시경 한 후에 운전해도 돼요?** — *naesigyeong han hue unjeonhaedo dwaeyo?* — "Can I drive after the endoscopy?"  
Them: **수면 내시경 하시면 오늘 운전하시면 안 돼요.** — *sumyeon naesigyeong hasimyeon oneul unjeonhasimyeon an dwaeyo.* — "If you have the sedated endoscopy, you must not drive today."

There are two ways to say "after," and they mean the same thing. **-ㄴ/은 후에** attaches to the verb stem — **-ㄴ 후에** with no batchim, **-은 후에** with one (**검사한 후에**, **먹은 후에**) — and after a noun it needs nothing at all: **퇴근 후에** (*toegeun hue*), "after work." **-고 나서** goes on any stem with no rules whatsoever, which makes it the safer one to reach for when you are being rushed: **끝나고 나서**, **하고 나서**, **먹고 나서**. Swap in **-은 다음에** or **-은 뒤에** anywhere and nothing changes but the sound of it. The sentence people most regret not asking is the sedation one — a **수면 내시경** leaves you groggy for hours, so a **보호자** (*bohoja*), someone to take you home, is often asked for at booking.

## What usually goes wrong at a Korean health checkup?

The first thing is the fast. **금식** (*geumsik*) starts the night before, not the morning of, and it is stricter than most people expect — no food, no coffee, no snack on the way. Water is usually the one exception in small amounts, but that is a question to ask rather than assume: **물도 마시면 안 돼요?** (*muldo masimyeon an dwaeyo?*), "Not even water?" When they ask how long you have gone without eating, **어제저녁부터 금식했어요** (*eojejeonyeokbuteo geumsikhaesseoyo*), "I've been fasting since last night," is the whole answer. Turning up having eaten does not usually mean a smaller checkup; it means a second trip.

The second is the questionnaire. The **문진표** is dense, medical and rarely offered in English, and the honest move is to stop rather than tick things blindly — **이거 무슨 뜻이에요?** (*igeo museun tteusieyo?*), "What does this mean?" is a normal thing to say to the nurse standing right there. The same goes for anything said to you at speed between rooms: **천천히 말해 주세요** (*cheoncheonhi malhae juseyo*), "Please speak slowly," and the rest of that toolkit is in [what to say when Korean is too fast](/korean-phrases-when-korean-is-too-fast) and [how to politely ask for help in Korean](/politely-ask-for-help-in-korean).

The third is what happens weeks later. The **결과지** (*gyeolgwaji*), the results sheet, is posted to your registered address rather than handed to you on the day, so an old address means results you never see — the counter that fixes that is in [Korean phrases for the community service center in Korea](/korean-phrases-community-service-center-korea). If something on it is flagged, the word is **재검사** (*jaegeomsa*), a re-test, and that is a normal follow-up rather than bad news by itself. Taking it to a doctor to have it explained is [how to see a doctor in Korea](/see-doctor-korea-korean-phrases), anything prescribed afterwards is picked up with [Korean phrases at a pharmacy in Korea](/korean-phrases-pharmacy-korea), and the teeth are a separate appointment altogether at [Korean phrases at the dentist in Korea](/korean-phrases-dentist-korea).
