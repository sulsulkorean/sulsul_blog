The **응급실** (*eunggeupsil*), the emergency room, is the one place in Korea where nobody will wait while you look up a word. A nurse meets you at the door with **어떻게 오셨어요?** (*eotteoke osyeosseoyo?*), "What brings you in?", and the next thirty seconds decide how quickly you are seen. Two patterns from the SULSUL workbook carry that answer. Pattern 86 — **-는 바람에** — is how you say the thing that went wrong: **넘어지는 바람에 발목을 다쳤어요** (*neomeojineun barame balmogeul dachyeosseoyo*), "I fell and hurt my ankle." Pattern 27 — **부터 … 까지** — is how you give the span of time, which is the second thing every nurse asks: **어젯밤부터 지금까지 열이 안 내려요** (*eojetbambuteo jigeumkkaji yeori an naeryeoyo*), "The fever hasn't come down since last night."

> - Say what happened: **넘어지는 바람에 다쳤어요** (*neomeojineun barame dachyeosseoyo*), "I fell and got hurt."
> - Say since when: **어젯밤부터 아팠어요** (*eojetbambuteo apasseoyo*), "It's hurt since last night."
> - Point at it: **여기가 제일 아파요** (*yeogiga jeil apayo*), "This is where it hurts most."
> - Say what you can't do: **숨쉬기 힘들어요** (*sumswigi himdeureoyo*), "I'm having trouble breathing."
> - Ask the wait: **얼마나 기다려야 해요?** (*eolmana gidaryeoya haeyo?*), "How long do I have to wait?"

## How do you say what happened?

**어떻게 오셨어요?** is not a polite greeting, it is the triage question, and the answer that gets you through it fastest is one cause and one result. That is exactly what Pattern 86 does: verb stem plus **-는 바람에**, then the thing it caused.

Them: **어떻게 오셨어요?** — *eotteoke osyeosseoyo?* — "What brings you in?"  
You: **계단에서 넘어지는 바람에 발목을 다쳤어요.** — *gyedaneseo neomeojineun barame balmogeul dachyeosseoyo.* — "I fell on the stairs and hurt my ankle."  
Them: **걸을 수 있으세요?** — *georeul su isseuseyo?* — "Can you walk?"  
You: **거의 못 걸어요. 많이 부었어요.** — *geoui mot georeoyo. mani bueosseoyo.* — "Barely. It's very swollen."

The form never changes — **-는 바람에** goes on any verb stem regardless of the batchim, so **넘어지다** becomes **넘어지는 바람에**, **먹다** becomes **먹는 바람에**. What does change is the meaning it carries: this pattern is only for results you did not want. **상한 걸 먹는 바람에 계속 토해요** (*sanghan geol meongneun barame gyesok tohaeyo*), "I ate something off and I keep throwing up," is natural. The same pattern on a good outcome sounds wrong to a Korean ear — for those you need **덕분에** (*deokbune*), "thanks to." In an emergency room that limitation is a feature, because everything you have to report is something that went wrong.

| Situation | Say this |
|---|---|
| You fell | 넘어지는 바람에 다쳤어요 (*neomeojineun barame dachyeosseoyo*) |
| Something broke or slipped | 미끄러지는 바람에 다쳤어요 (*mikkeureojineun barame dachyeosseoyo*) |
| Bad food | 상한 걸 먹는 바람에 토했어요 (*sanghan geol meongneun barame tohaesseoyo*) |
| You can't breathe well | 숨쉬기 힘들어요 (*sumswigi himdeureoyo*) |
| You're bleeding | 피가 계속 나요 (*piga gyesok nayo*) |

## How do you answer "since when"?

The second question is always **언제부터 아프셨어요?** (*eonjebuteo apeusyeosseoyo?*), "Since when has it hurt?" — and it is asked because the answer changes the treatment. Pattern 27 is two particles doing one job: **부터** marks the start, **까지** marks the end, and either one can stand alone. **어제부터 아팠어요** gives the start. **어제부터 오늘까지 아무것도 못 먹었어요** (*eojebuteo oneulkkaji amugeotdo mot meogeosseoyo*), "I haven't been able to eat anything since yesterday," gives both.

Them: **언제부터 아프셨어요?** — *eonjebuteo apeusyeosseoyo?* — "Since when has it hurt?"  
You: **어젯밤부터요. 열이 안 내려요.** — *eojetbambuteoyo. yeori an naeryeoyo.* — "Since last night. The fever won't come down."  
Them: **약은 드셨어요?** — *yageun deusyeosseoyo?* — "Have you taken anything for it?"  
You: **아침에 해열제 먹었는데 두 시간밖에 안 갔어요.** — *achime haeyeolje meogeonneunde du siganbakke an gasseoyo.* — "I took a fever reducer this morning, but it only lasted two hours."

**부터** is a stretch of time; **에** is a point on the clock. **세 시에 아팠어요** means it hurt at three o'clock and says nothing about now, while **세 시부터 아팠어요** means it started at three and has not stopped — a different sentence for a doctor. The same two particles do places as well, which is why **여기부터 여기까지 아파요** (*yeogibuteo yeogikkaji apayo*), "It hurts from here to here," works when you run a finger along your own side and the word for the body part will not come. Two more you will be asked: **알레르기 있으세요?** (*allereugi isseuseyo?*), "Any allergies?" and **지금 드시는 약 있으세요?** (*jigeum deusineun yak isseuseyo?*), "Are you on any medication?" Both are safest answered from a note on your phone rather than from memory, and the phrases for slowing the whole exchange down are in [Korean phrases for when Korean is too fast](/korean-phrases-when-korean-is-too-fast).

## What usually goes wrong at a Korean emergency room?

The first thing is choosing the wrong door. Korean emergency rooms sort everyone on arrival into five urgency levels, and a sprained ankle sits behind every chest pain that walks in after you — a wait of several hours is normal, not a mistake. For anything that can hold until morning, the better answer is a clinic with **야간진료** (*yaganjillyo*), evening hours, and for a child there is the **달빛어린이병원** (*dalbit eorini byeongwon*) network that stays open late specifically for kids. Not every hospital runs a full emergency department either, so **응급실 있어요?** (*eunggeupsil isseoyo?*) is worth asking before you set off. If it is serious, **119** is the ambulance number and the operator can bring an interpreter onto the line; if you are going under your own steam, [how to ask for a taxi in Korean](/ask-for-taxi-in-korean) is the faster read.

The second is the paperwork at **접수** (*jeopsu*), reception. Bring your **외국인등록증** (*oegugin deungnokjeung*), the residence card, and your health insurance details — national coverage is handled through the local office covered in [Korean phrases for a community service center in Korea](/korean-phrases-community-service-center-korea). An emergency room visit carries an extra emergency-care charge that a daytime clinic does not, and it is not waived just because the case turns out to be minor. The sentence to say before you leave is **진단서 받을 수 있어요?** (*jindanseo badeul su isseoyo?*), "Can I get a medical certificate?" — a **진단서** (*jindanseo*) is what travel insurance, a school, or an employer will ask for later, and going back for one another day is its own errand.

The third is what happens after. You leave with a **처방전** (*cheobangjeon*), a prescription, which is filled at a pharmacy rather than the hospital, and at two in the morning that means a **심야약국** (*simyayakguk*), a late-night pharmacy — the counter language is in [Korean phrases for a pharmacy in Korea](/korean-phrases-pharmacy-korea). An emergency room stabilises you and then hands you back: **내일 병원 다시 가야 해요?** (*naeil byeongwon dasi gaya haeyo?*), "Do I need to come back tomorrow?" is the question that tells you whether the next stop is a regular clinic, and [how to see a doctor in Korea when you feel sick](/see-doctor-korea-korean-phrases) covers that morning.
