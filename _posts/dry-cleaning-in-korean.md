Dry cleaning in Korean is **드라이클리닝** (*deu-ra-i-keul-li-ning*), borrowed straight from English. The shop that does it is a **세탁소** (*se-tak-so*), and that's the word you'll actually type into a map app or say to a neighbor: **세탁소 어디 있어요?** (*se-tak-so eo-di i-sseo-yo?*), "Where's the dry cleaner?" At the counter you rarely need the long word at all. Hand over the coat and say **드라이 해 주세요** (*deu-ra-i hae ju-se-yo*), "Dry-clean this, please." Koreans shorten 드라이클리닝 to **드라이** (*deu-ra-i*) almost every time.

> **Dry cleaning words in Korean, the short version**
> - Dry cleaning: **드라이클리닝** — *deu-ra-i-keul-li-ning* (spoken: **드라이**, *deu-ra-i*)
> - Dry cleaner (the shop): **세탁소** — *se-tak-so*
> - Laundry, washing: **세탁** — *se-tak* (casual: **빨래**, *ppal-lae*)
> - Washing in water: **물빨래** — *mul-ppal-lae*
> - Self-service laundromat: **빨래방** — *ppal-lae-bang*
> - "Dry-clean this, please": **드라이 해 주세요** — *deu-ra-i hae ju-se-yo*

## 세탁, 빨래, 드라이: which word means what

English has "laundry," "washing," and "dry cleaning." Korean has a few more, and each one points to a different method or place. Mix them up and the person at the counter will ask a follow-up question.

| Korean | Say it | Meaning | Where you meet it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 세탁 | *se-tak* | Washing, laundering (neutral) | Signs, machines, 세탁기 (washing machine) |
| 빨래 | *ppal-lae* | Laundry, doing the wash (everyday) | Talk at home, "I have laundry to do" |
| 드라이클리닝 / 드라이 | *deu-ra-i-keul-li-ning* / *deu-ra-i* | Dry cleaning | The dry cleaner counter |
| 물빨래 | *mul-ppal-lae* | Washing in water | Care labels, "can this go in water?" |
| 손빨래 | *son-ppal-lae* | Hand wash | Knits, delicates |
| 세탁소 | *se-tak-so* | Dry cleaner / laundry shop | Street signs, map searches |
| 빨래방 | *ppal-lae-bang* | Coin laundromat | Self-service, open late |
| 다림질 | *da-rim-jil* | Ironing | "Can you just press it?" |

The trap here is **세탁소** versus **빨래방**. A 세탁소 is staffed: you hand clothes over and come back for them. A 빨래방 is a room of machines where you do it yourself. If you need a suit cleaned, a 빨래방 can't help. If you just need a week of T-shirts washed tonight, the 빨래방 is faster and cheaper. For the do-it-yourself version, the post on [Korean phrases for a laundromat in Korea](/korean-phrases-laundromat-korea) covers the machines step by step.

Most neighborhood 세탁소 also press, hem, and fix zippers. That's why many signs read **세탁 · 수선** (*se-tak · su-seon*), "cleaning and repairs."

## Reading a Korean care label

Clothes bought in Korea come with Korean care labels, and the words are short enough to learn in a minute. These are the ones that decide whether the coat goes to the 세탁소 or into your machine:

- **드라이클리닝** — dry clean only
- **물세탁 금지** (*mul-se-tak geum-ji*) — do not wash in water
- **손세탁** (*son-se-tak*) — hand wash
- **30℃ 중성세제** (*jung-seong-se-je*) — wash at 30°C with mild detergent
- **표백 금지** (*pyo-baek geum-ji*) — no bleach
- **다림질 금지** (*da-rim-jil geum-ji*) — do not iron
- **건조기 사용 금지** (*geon-jo-gi sa-yong geum-ji*) — no tumble dryer

**금지** (*geum-ji*) means "prohibited," and it's the word to watch for. You'll see it far beyond laundry, from **주차 금지** (no parking) to **촬영 금지** (no photos).

When the label isn't clear, ask the expert. Pattern 44 from the SULSUL workbook, **(V)아/어도 돼요?** (*a/eo-do dwae-yo?*), "Is it okay to…?", is built for this:

- **이거 물빨래해도 돼요?** — *i-geo mul-ppal-lae-hae-do dwae-yo?* — "Can I wash this in water?"
- **세탁기에 돌려도 돼요?** — *se-tak-gi-e dol-lyeo-do dwae-yo?* — "Can I put it in the washing machine?"
- **건조기에 넣어도 돼요?** — *geon-jo-gi-e neo-eo-do dwae-yo?* — "Can it go in the dryer?"

If the answer is **안 돼요** (*an dwae-yo*) or **드라이 하셔야 돼요** (*deu-ra-i ha-syeo-ya dwae-yo*), "You need to dry-clean it," leave it with them.

## Explaining a stain without the vocabulary panic

A stain is **얼룩** (*eol-luk*), and the verb you want is **빼다** (*ppae-da*), "to take out." Pattern 23, **(V)아/어 주세요** (*a/eo ju-se-yo*), turns any service into a polite request:

- **얼룩 좀 빼 주세요.** — *eol-luk jom ppae ju-se-yo.* — "Please get this stain out."
- **다려 주세요.** — *da-ryeo ju-se-yo.* — "Please press it."
- **단추 좀 달아 주세요.** — *dan-chu jom da-ra ju-se-yo.* — "Please sew a button on."

The cleaner will want to know what the stain is, because coffee and oil come out differently. Name it and add **-이에요/예요**:

- **커피예요.** — *keo-pi-ye-yo.* — "It's coffee."
- **기름이에요.** — *gi-reu-mi-e-yo.* — "It's oil."
- **와인이에요.** — *wa-i-ni-e-yo.* — "It's wine."

To tell the whole story in one line, Pattern 86, **(V)는 바람에** (*neun ba-ra-me*), explains an unexpected bad result: **커피를 쏟는 바람에 얼룩이 생겼어요** (*keo-pi-reul sson-neun ba-ra-me eol-lu-gi saeng-gyeo-sseo-yo*), "I spilled coffee, so it got stained." It sounds natural and tells the cleaner exactly what happened.

## A two-minute drop-off

Here's how a drop-off usually goes. The staff do most of the talking, and the key question is the last one.

Staff: **어떻게 해 드릴까요?** — *eo-tteo-ke hae deu-ril-kka-yo?* — "What can I do for you?"  
You: **이 코트 드라이 해 주세요.** — *i ko-teu deu-ra-i hae ju-se-yo.* — "Please dry-clean this coat."  
Staff: **얼룩 있어요?** — *eol-luk i-sseo-yo?* — "Any stains?"  
You: **네, 여기요. 커피예요.** — *ne, yeo-gi-yo. keo-pi-ye-yo.* — "Yes, here. It's coffee."  
Staff: **전화번호 주세요.** — *jeon-hwa-beon-ho ju-se-yo.* — "Your phone number, please."  
You: **금요일까지 돼요?** — *geu-myo-il-kka-ji dwae-yo?* — "Can it be ready by Friday?"

That last line uses Pattern 27, **(N)까지** (*kka-ji*), "by / until." Swap in **내일까지** (*nae-il-kka-ji*, by tomorrow) or **오늘까지** (*o-neul-kka-ji*, by today) if you're leaving town soon. Many 세탁소 don't give paper tickets anymore. They write down your phone number and text you when it's ready, so keep your Korean number handy. ## Mistakes people make with these words

**Searching 드라이클리닝 on a map.** You'll get product ads and blog posts. Search **세탁소** and every shop on your street appears.

**Asking a 빨래방 for dry cleaning.** It's self-service, so there's no one to take your coat. Some larger laundromats have a drop-off counter, but most don't. Look for the word 세탁소 on the sign.

**Saying "laundry" when you mean dry cleaning.** **빨래 해 주세요** sounds like you want it washed in water, which can ruin wool. If it's a suit, a coat, or silk, say **드라이**.

**Forgetting to point out the stain.** Cleaners in Korea are careful, but they process a lot of clothes. If you don't say **얼룩 있어요**, a small mark can come back exactly as it left.

**Using 빼 주세요 for a hem.** 빼다 means "take out," which works for stains. For shortening pants, it's **줄여 주세요** (*ju-ryeo ju-se-yo*), "please shorten it." The post on [Korean phrases at a tailor in Korea](/korean-phrases-tailor-korea) covers alterations in more detail.

## Say it before you're at the counter

**드라이** sounds easy, but Korean pronunciation adds a vowel after each consonant: *deu-ra-i*, three beats, not the English "dry." The same goes for **얼룩 좀 빼 주세요**, where the tense **ㅃ** in *ppae* is what makes it clear.
