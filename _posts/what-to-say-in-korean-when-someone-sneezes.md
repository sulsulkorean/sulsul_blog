Korean has no equivalent of "bless you." If someone sneezes next to you on the subway, in a café, or across a dinner table, the normal, polite, completely unremarkable thing to do is **say nothing**. Nobody is being cold — a sneeze is treated as a private bodily event, and acknowledging it out loud draws attention the sneezer did not ask for. So if you have been standing there panicking for a phrase, the first answer is: you do not need one. The second answer is what Koreans *do* say, and it comes a beat later, aimed at the cold rather than the sneeze: **괜찮아요?** (*gwaenchanayo?*), "are you okay?", or **감기 조심하세요** (*gamgi josimhaseyo*), "be careful of catching a cold." And if *you* are the one who sneezed, the line you want is **죄송합니다** (*joesonghamnida*) — you apologise for the noise.

> - Are you okay? — **괜찮아요?** (*gwaenchanayo?*)
> - Are you okay? (to someone older) — **괜찮으세요?** (*gwaenchaneuseyo?*)
> - Do you have a cold? — **감기 걸렸어요?** (*gamgi geollyeosseoyo?*)
> - You must be catching a cold — **감기 걸렸나 봐요** (*gamgi geollyeonna bwayo*)
> - Be careful of catching a cold — **감기 조심하세요** (*gamgi josimhaseyo*)
> - Do you want a tissue? — **휴지 드릴까요?** (*hyuji deurilkkayo?*)
> - Sorry (after sneezing) — **죄송합니다** (*joesonghamnida*)
> - Excuse me (after sneezing) — **실례합니다** (*sillyehamnida*)
> - Achoo — **에취** (*echwi*)
> - Get well soon — **몸조리 잘하세요** (*momjori jalhaseyo*)

## Why silence is the correct answer

English speakers reach for "bless you" automatically, which makes the Korean silence feel like something is missing. It is not. The habit never existed here, there is no secular replacement for it, and no generation of Koreans grew up expecting a response.

A few younger Koreans who have lived abroad will say **블레스 유** (*beulleseu yu*) as a joke, in English, with a grin — it lands as a bit, not a blessing. You can smile at it. What you should not do is deliver it earnestly to a stranger, because you will be the only person in the conversation who thinks a line was required.

The word for a sneeze itself is **재채기** (*jaechaegi*), and the verb is **재채기하다** (*jaechaegihada*). The sound effect — the Korean "achoo" — is **에취**, which you will see in comics and hear in dramas.

| English habit | Korean reality |
|---|---|
| Bless you | nothing; silence is polite |
| Gesundheit | no equivalent exists |
| You okay? | **괜찮아요?** — yes, but after the *third* sneeze |
| Get well soon | **몸조리 잘하세요** — for someone actually sick |

## 괜찮아요? — the line that does exist

One sneeze gets nothing. Three or four in a row, or one that clearly shook the person, and the room softens into **괜찮아요?** — the same all-purpose "are you okay?" you would use if someone tripped.

Register matters here more than vocabulary. **괜찮아?** (*gwaenchana?*) is for a friend or someone younger. **괜찮아요?** is the safe polite default for a coworker, a classmate, a shop clerk. **괜찮으세요?** is what you say to anyone noticeably older than you, and it is the version a hotel clerk or a taxi driver should hear from you. The same ladder runs through every sentence you will build in Korean, and it is mapped out in [how to greet someone in different Korean politeness levels](/greet-korean-politeness-levels).

The answer you will get back is almost always **괜찮아요** — "I'm fine" — because that is the reflex, whether or not it is true. Two other replies are common from the sneezer: **알레르기예요** (*allereugieyo*), "it's an allergy," and **미세먼지 때문에요** (*misemeonji ttaemuneyo*), "it's the fine dust." That second one is a real, daily Korean sentence. On bad air days **미세먼지** explains half the sneezing in the country, and saying it signals *I am not sick, do not back away from me*.

## 감기 걸렸나 봐요 — guessing out loud

When the sneezing has clearly become a cold, Korean has a tidy way to say so without diagnosing anyone. Attach **나 봐요** to a verb and you get "seems like" — a conclusion drawn from what you can see:

**감기 걸렸나 봐요** — *gamgi geollyeonna bwayo* — "looks like you've caught a cold."
**많이 아픈가 봐요** — *mani apeunga bwayo* — "you must be feeling pretty rough."
**환절기라서 그런가 봐요** — *hwanjeolgiraseo geureonga bwayo* — "it must be the change of seasons."

That last one is worth memorising on its own. **환절기** (*hwanjeolgi*) is the turn between seasons, and Koreans blame it for everything — sore throats, headaches, sneezing fits. "환절기라서요" is a complete, natural excuse for feeling off in March or October, and it is standard small talk, close kin to the weather openers in [Korean weather phrases for small talk in Korea](/korean-weather-phrases-small-talk).

The softer version of the same guess is a plain question: **감기 걸렸어요?** (*gamgi geollyeosseoyo?*), "do you have a cold?" Use that with a friend; use **나 봐요** when you want to sound like you are observing rather than interrogating.

## What to say when you are the one sneezing

This is the half most guides skip, and it is the half you will actually need. A sneeze in Korea is something you cover and then apologise for.

**죄송합니다** — *joesonghamnida* — the formal apology. Correct in an office, a meeting, a quiet restaurant.
**실례합니다** — *sillyehamnida* — "excuse me," literally "I am being impolite." Slightly lighter than 죄송합니다 and a very natural fit here.
**죄송해요** — *joesonghaeyo* — the everyday polite version among people you know.
**감기 걸려서요** — *gamgi geollyeoseoyo* — "it's because I have a cold," offered as an explanation so nobody wonders.

If the difference between 죄송합니다 and 미안해요 is still fuzzy, it is unpacked in [how to say sorry in Korean](/how-to-say-sorry-in-korean).

Two pieces of etiquette carry more weight than any of these phrases. First, **do not blow your nose at the table.** Sniffing is tolerated; the full honk into a tissue is genuinely unpleasant to Korean ears, and people step away to a restroom or a hallway to do it. Second, **wear a mask if you are sick.** A **마스크** (*maseukeu*) on a coughing, sneezing person reads as consideration, not as alarm, and you can buy one at any convenience store. That single habit will do more for how you are perceived than a perfect apology.

## Pushing it one step further: advice

Once the cold is established, Korean conversation moves to care, and two patterns do most of the work. **(V)는 게 어때요?** suggests gently; **(V)아/어야 해요** says what should happen.

**약 먹는 게 어때요?** — *yak meongneun ge eottaeyo?* — "how about taking some medicine?"
**일찍 들어가는 게 어때요?** — *iljjik deureoganeun ge eottaeyo?* — "how about heading home early?"
**푹 쉬어야 해요** — *puk swieoya haeyo* — "you need to rest properly."
**병원 가 봐야 해요** — *byeongwon ga bwaya haeyo* — "you should get it looked at."

And the send-offs: **몸조리 잘하세요** (*momjori jalhaseyo*), "take care of yourself," the standard thing to say to someone who is unwell, and **빨리 나으세요** (*ppalli naeuseyo*), "get better soon." If the cold wins and you end up at a counter asking for medicine yourself, the phrases for that are in [Korean phrases for the pharmacy in Korea](/korean-phrases-pharmacy-korea).

## How it actually runs

A coworker sneezes twice, then a third time, at the next desk:

You: **괜찮으세요? 감기 걸렸나 봐요.** — *gwaenchaneuseyo? gamgi geollyeonna bwayo.* — "Are you okay? Looks like you've caught a cold."
Them: **네, 환절기라서요. 죄송합니다.** — *ne, hwanjeolgiraseoyo. joesonghamnida.* — "Yeah, it's the season change. Sorry."
You: **휴지 드릴까요?** — *hyuji deurilkkayo?* — "Want a tissue?"
Them: **감사합니다.** — *gamsahamnida.* — "Thank you."
You: **약 먹는 게 어때요? 몸조리 잘하세요.** — *yak meongneun ge eottaeyo? momjori jalhaseyo.* — "How about some medicine? Take care of yourself."

Now the reverse — you sneeze in a quiet café and the person beside you glances over:

You: **아, 죄송합니다. 미세먼지 때문에요.** — *a, joesonghamnida. misemeonji ttaemuneyo.* — "Oh, sorry. It's the fine dust."
Them: **괜찮아요. 감기 조심하세요.** — *gwaenchanayo. gamgi josimhaseyo.* — "No problem. Take care you don't get sick."

## What goes wrong

**Saying "bless you" in English to a stranger.** It is not understood as a courtesy, just as an unexpected English sentence from someone you were not talking to. Silence costs nothing.

**Translating "bless you" into Korean.** **축복합니다** (*chukbokhamnida*) is a real word and it means a religious blessing. Said after a sneeze, it is bizarre.

**Reacting to every single sneeze.** One sneeze is weather. Commenting on it turns a non-event into a conversation about someone's body.

**Using 괜찮아? with someone older.** The casual ending undercuts the kindness. **괜찮으세요?** is the form that matches the intent.

**Confusing 감기 조심하세요 with 몸조리 잘하세요.** The first is preventive — "don't catch a cold" — and fits someone who sneezed once. The second is for someone who is already sick. Swapping them is harmless but noticeable.

**Blowing your nose at the table.** No phrase recovers from this one. Step out.
