If you are a Tamil speaker watching K-dramas, some Korean words will sound like your own house. **엄마** (*eom-ma*) means **அம்மா** — "mom." **아빠** (*a-ppa*) means **அப்பா** — "dad." **나** (*na*) means **நான்** — "I." **너** (*neo*, often said *ni*) means **நீ** — "you." **와** (*wa*) means **வா** — "come." And when a Korean grandmother sighs **아이고** (*a-i-go*), she is saying exactly what your grandmother means by **ஐயோ**.

> - **엄마** (*eom-ma*) — அம்மா — "mom"
> - **아빠** (*a-ppa*) — அப்பா — "dad"
> - **나 / 너** (*na / neo*) — நான் / நீ — "I / you"
> - **이리 와** (*i-ri wa*) — இங்கே வா — "come here"
> - **아이고** (*a-i-go*) — ஐயோ — "oh no, oh my"

Below are the words that sound alike, the family words that match **அக்கா** and **அண்ணா**, the reactions and care lines you hear in every episode, and the places where the Tamil match leads you wrong.

## Five Korean words that already sound Tamil

Say these out loud and you will hear it:

- **엄마** (*eom-ma*) — **அம்மா** — mom. The first word of every crying scene.
- **아빠** (*a-ppa*) — **அப்பா** — dad. Same sound, same meaning.
- **나** (*na*) — **நான்** — I, me. *나 괜찮아* — "I'm okay."
- **너** (*neo*) — **நீ** — you, to a friend or someone younger. In casual speech many Koreans say **니** (*ni*), which is almost **நீ**.
- **와** (*wa*) — **வா** — come. **이리 와** (*i-ri wa*) — **இங்கே வா** — "come here," the line every drama hero says with an open arm.

Are Korean and Tamil related? In the early 1900s the missionary Homer Hulbert wrote a whole book comparing Korean with Dravidian languages, and the idea still goes viral on social media. Most linguists today treat these matches as coincidence. For a learner it does not matter much: a coincidence you can hear is still a free word you will never forget.

## அக்கா and அண்ணா in Korean: four words, not two

Tamil does not call an older friend by name alone — you say **அக்கா** or **அண்ணா**. Korean does the same. The twist is that in Korean, **the word changes with who is speaking.**

| Korean | Who says it | Tamil | English |
|---|---|---|---|
| **언니** (*eon-ni*) | a girl, to an older woman | அக்கா | older sister |
| **누나** (*nu-na*) | a boy, to an older woman | அக்கா | older sister |
| **오빠** (*o-ppa*) | a girl, to an older man | அண்ணா | older brother |
| **형** (*hyeong*) | a boy, to an older man | அண்ணா | older brother |
| **동생** (*dong-saeng*) | anyone, about someone younger | தம்பி / தங்கச்சி | younger sibling |

So a girl's **오빠** and a boy's **형** are both **அண்ணா**. And like **அண்ணா** for the man at the tea stall, these words reach beyond family: a senior in college, a close friend's older brother, a friendly neighbour.

One big difference. A Tamil girl would never call her boyfriend **அண்ணா**. A Korean girl calls her boyfriend **오빠** all the time — that is why the word sounds so sweet in a romance and so strange the first time you hear it.

## Same word order: Korean sentences feel Tamil

English says "I ate rice." Tamil and Korean both put the verb at the end:

> **나는 밥을 먹었어.** (*na-neun ba-beul meo-geo-sseo.*)
> **நான் சோறு சாப்பிட்டேன்.** (*naan soru saappitten.*)
> I — rice — ate.

Both languages also stick little endings onto words instead of using separate words like "to" or "at." Korean **학교에** (*hak-gyo-e*) is "to school," just like Tamil **பள்ளிக்கு** (*pallikku*). If you think in Tamil, you can build a Korean sentence in the same order your brain already uses. English speakers have to flip everything around; you do not.

## நீ and நீங்க: Korean politeness, the Tamil way

You already switch between **வா** and **வாங்க**, between **நீ** and **நீங்க**, without thinking. Korean has the same switch, and it lives at the end of the sentence:

- **와** (*wa*) — **வா** — come (to a friend)
- **오세요** (*o-se-yo*) — **வாங்க** — please come (polite)
- **고마워** (*go-ma-wo*) — **தேங்க்ஸ்** — thanks (to a friend)
- **감사합니다** (*gam-sa-ham-ni-da*) — **நன்றிங்க** — thank you (polite)
- **미안해** (*mi-an-hae*) — **சாரி டா / சாரி டி** — sorry (to a friend)
- **죄송합니다** (*joe-song-ham-ni-da*) — **மன்னிக்கணும்** — I apologize (formal)

Think of Korean **-요** as Tamil **-ங்க**. Add it, and you are speaking to an elder or a stranger. Leave it off, and you are speaking like close friends in a drama.

## K-drama reactions you already shout in Tamil

These are the sounds of every episode:

- **아이고** (*a-i-go*) — **ஐயோ** — the perfect match. Pain, surprise, tiredness, a big bill — one word does it all.
- **대박** (*dae-bak*) — **செம! / வேற லெவல்!** — "Amazing!"
- **헐** (*heol*) — **என்னது?!** — "Wait, what?"
- **진짜?** (*jin-jja?*) — **உண்மையா?** — "Really?"
- **어떡해** (*eo-tteo-ke*) — **இப்போ என்ன பண்றது?** — "What do I do now?"
- **괜찮아** (*gwaen-chan-a*) — **பரவாயில்லை** — "It's okay."
- **화이팅** (*hwa-i-ting*) — **ஆல் தி பெஸ்ட்!** — "You've got this!" It comes from English "fighting," but nobody is fighting.

## சாப்பிட்டியா? Care lines in Korean

A Tamil mother asks **சாப்பிட்டியா?** before anything else. A Korean mother does too.

![Korean home-cooked dishes on a dining table](/assets/blog/scenes/home-cooking.jpg)

- **밥 먹었어?** (*bap meo-geo-sseo?*) — **சாப்பிட்டியா?** — "Did you eat?" It really means "I care about you."
- **밥 먹었어요?** (*bap meo-geo-sseo-yo?*) — **சாப்பிட்டீங்களா?** — the polite version.
- **잘 자** (*jal ja*) — **நல்லா தூங்கு** — "Sleep well."
- **조심해** (*jo-sim-hae*) — **பார்த்து போ** — "Be careful."
- **보고 싶어** (*bo-go si-peo*) — **உன்னைப் பார்க்கணும்** — "I miss you," literally "I want to see you" — the same picture as the Tamil.

A short exchange between friends:

> **A:** 밥 먹었어? (*bap meo-geo-sseo?*) — சாப்பிட்டியா?
> **B:** 아직. 너는? (*a-jik. neo-neun?*) — இன்னும் இல்லை. நீ?
> **A:** 나도. 같이 먹자! (*na-do. ga-chi meok-ja!*) — நானும் இல்லை. சேர்ந்து சாப்பிடலாம்!

## Where the Tamil match breaks

**Calling a stranger 오빠 like அண்ணா.** In Tamil, any shopkeeper can be **அண்ணா**. In Korean, a girl saying **오빠** to a stranger sounds flirty. Use **사장님** (*sa-jang-nim*, "boss") for a shop owner, or **저기요** (*jeo-gi-yo*, "excuse me") to get attention.

**Using 너 with elders.** **너** is **நீ**, never **நீங்க**. Saying it to a Korean friend's mother is like saying **நீ** to your own aunt. Use her title or skip "you" altogether — Korean drops it easily.

**Mixing up 엄마 and 어머니.** **엄마** is the warm **அம்மா** at home. **어머니** (*eo-meo-ni*) is closer to **அம்மா** said formally, and it is what you use for a friend's mother.

**Pushing too much air.** Tamil speakers have an advantage here. Korean ㅂ, ㄷ, ㄱ are soft, unaspirated sounds — closer to Tamil ப, த, க than to English "p, t, k." And just as Tamil ப can sound like "b" between vowels, Korean ㅂ becomes "b" in **아버지** (*a-beo-ji*). Trust your Tamil ear.
