**누나** (*nuna*) means "older sister" — but only out of a man's mouth. A male speaker says 누나 to his own older sister and to any woman a little older than him that he is close to: a classmate two years ahead, a neighbour he grew up next to, a colleague he eats lunch with. A woman in exactly the same situation would say **언니** (*eonni*) instead. That is the part subtitles never explain: 누나 tells you the speaker is male before it tells you anything about the woman. It also needs closeness, not just age. A stranger who happens to be older is not your 누나, and calling her one is the kind of mistake that makes a Korean flinch on your behalf.

> - The word: **누나** (*nuna*), "older sister," said by a male speaker.
> - The female-speaker version: **언니** (*eonni*), same meaning, different mouth.
> - The respectful form: **누님** (*nunim*), for a woman much older or when you want distance.
> - Your actual sister: **친누나** (*chinnuna*), "blood older sister."
> - The question that decides it: **몇 년생이세요?** (*myeot nyeonsaengiseyo?*), "What year were you born?"

## Who says 누나, and who says 언니

Korean has four of these words, and they split twice — once by the speaker's gender, once by the listener's.

| You are | Older man | Older woman |
|---|---|---|
| Male | 형 (*hyeong*) | 누나 (*nuna*) |
| Female | 오빠 (*oppa*) | 언니 (*eonni*) |

So one woman gets called two different things on the same evening depending on who is talking to her, and nothing about her has changed. Her younger brother says 누나. Her younger female cousin says 언니. Both are correct at the same table.

This is why hearing 누나 in a drama is a piece of information, not just a name. When a male character uses it, he is announcing that she is older and that they are close enough for him to skip her name entirely. The whole grid of these titles, including the in-law ones that trip people up, is laid out in [Korean family titles: how to address people in Korea](/korean-family-titles-how-to-address-people).

## 누나 works as a name — that is the strange part

In English, "sis" is a nickname you add on top of a name. 누나 replaces the name. You say it directly to her, over and over, in ordinary sentences, and using her bare name instead would sound cold or oddly formal.

You: **누나, 이거 먹어도 돼요?** — *nuna, igeo meogeodo dwaeyo?* — "Noona, can I eat this?"  
Her: **먹어, 먹어. 배고프지?** — *meogeo, meogeo. baegopeuji?* — "Eat, eat. You're hungry, right?"  
You: **고마워요, 누나.** — *gomawoyo, nuna.* — "Thanks, noona."

Notice she answers in **반말** (*banmal*), the casual form, while he stays a step politer. That gap is normal between an older and younger friend and nobody reads it as cold — the older one speaks down casually, the younger one speaks up politely. If the two of them are properly close, he will drop to 반말 too, but she offers that first. Which ending belongs where is unpacked in [how to greet someone in different Korean politeness levels](/greet-korean-politeness-levels).

## 누나 or 누님? When the 님 goes on

**누님** (*nunim*) is 누나 with the honorific **님** (*nim*) fused on, and it is not simply "polite 누나." It adds distance along with the respect. You use it for a woman clearly much older than you, for a friend's mother's generation, or in a formal setting where plain 누나 would sound too chummy.

Between men there is also a half-joking use: calling a woman **누님** when she has just done something impressively tough. It flatters and teases at once, and you will hear it in variety shows more than in real offices.

At work, neither word is the safe default. Korean offices run on job titles — **대리님** (*daerinim*), **과장님** (*gwajangnim*) — and a man calling a female colleague 누나 in front of a manager sounds like he is dragging the friendship into the wrong room. The word for "the senior who started before me" is a different one entirely, explained in [what does sunbae mean in Korean](/what-does-sunbae-mean-in-korean).

And in a restaurant, the woman serving you is not your 누나. The warm word there is **이모님** (*imonim*), literally "auntie," or the neutral **저기요** (*jeogiyo*), "excuse me."

## 누나팬 and the 누나 romance trope

K-pop fans meet this word from a second direction. A **누나팬** (*nunapaen*) is a female fan older than the idol she supports, and idols address that part of the crowd directly:

**누나들, 보고 싶었어요!** — *nunadeul, bogo sipeosseoyo!* — "Noonas, I missed you!"

The **들** (*deul*) on the end makes it plural. It is an affectionate greeting, not a comment on anyone's age, and the equivalent aimed at older male fans is **형들** (*hyeongdeul*).

Then there is the drama version. A couple where the woman is older is a **연상연하 커플** (*yeonsang-yeonha keopeul*), and the story almost always turns on the same small linguistic moment: he has been calling her 누나 for ten episodes, and then he stops. Sometimes he switches to her name, sometimes to **자기야** (*jagiya*) — the pet name broken down in [what does jagiya mean in Korean](/what-does-jagiya-mean-in-korean). Either way, Korean viewers register the change instantly, because dropping 누나 means he is refusing the little-brother position the word puts him in.

Fans sometimes ask whether couples keep using 누나 after they start dating. Plenty do, out of habit and affection. But it stays a slightly loaded choice, which is exactly why writers keep reaching for it.

## How you find out whether she is your 누나

You cannot use the word until you know who is older, so the question comes first. Asking a flat **몇 살이에요?** (*myeot sarieyo?*) is fine among peers, but the smoother version asks for a birth year instead of a number:

You: **누나, 혹시 몇 년생이세요?** — *nuna, hoksi myeot nyeonsaengiseyo?* — "Can I ask what year you were born?"  
Her: **저 98년생이에요.** — *jeo gupal-nyeonsaengieyo.* — "I was born in '98."  
You: **아, 그럼 누나라고 불러도 돼요?** — *a, geureom nunarago bulleodo dwaeyo?* — "Ah, then can I call you noona?"  
Her: **당연하지! 편하게 불러.** — *dangyeonhaji! pyeonhage bulleo.* — "Of course! Call me whatever's comfortable."

**~라고 불러도 돼요?** (*~rago bulleodo dwaeyo?*), "may I call you ~?", is the sentence to memorise. It turns a guess into an invitation, and it works for 누나, 언니, 형 and 오빠 alike. More ways to handle the age conversation are in [how to ask someone's age in Korean](/how-to-ask-age-in-korean).

## Mistakes K-drama fans make with 누나

**Saying it as a woman.** The single most common error. If you are female, the word you want is 언니 — always, even about the same person.

**Using it on a stranger.** Age alone does not make someone your 누나. Closeness does. To a woman you have never met, use 저기요.

**Treating it as a compliment or a flirt.** On its own it is plain family vocabulary, as neutral as "older sister." The romance in your favourite scene came from the actor's face, not the noun.

**Skipping the permission step.** Guessing wrong and calling a younger woman 누나 is a small disaster. Ask the birth year, then ask if you may use the title.

**Reading the spelling as English.** Fans write *noona* because of subtitles, but it is two light syllables, *nu-na*, with the vowel of "food" cut short — never "new-na," never a heavy stress on the second half. The same fix applies to *oppa*, which is **오빠** with a tightened first consonant, not "oh-PAH."

**Learning only this one.** 누나 is one corner of a four-word system, and you will use all four within a week of arriving. The everyday drama vocabulary that sits next to it is collected in [common K-drama phrases to use in conversation](/common-k-drama-phrases-conversation).
