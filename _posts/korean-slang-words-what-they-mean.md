Korean slang is mostly built two ways: **short reactions** you shout at a screen (**대박**, **헐**, **진짜?**), and **chopped-up compounds** that squeeze a whole sentence into two syllables (**노잼** = "no fun," **심쿵** = "my heart just stopped"). Once you know which machine made a word, you can usually guess a new one on sight — which matters, because Korean slang turns over fast and no list stays complete. Below are the ones that have outlasted the trend cycle, grouped by how they work, with a note on who you can actually say them to.

> - **대박** (*daebak*) — "whoa, huge." Good news or bad news, both.
> - **헐** (*heol*) — "wait, what." The sound of being blindsided.
> - **짱** (*jjang*) — the best, the top.
> - **완전** (*wanjeon*) — "totally," dropped in front of anything.
> - **사이다** (*saida*) — a scene that finally gives you satisfaction.
> - **고구마** (*goguma*) — a scene that makes you want to scream.
> - **노잼** (*nojaem*) / **꿀잼** (*kkuljaem*) — boring / hilarious.
> - **인정** (*injeong*) — "agreed," "facts."
> - **최애** (*choeae*) — your absolute favorite, your bias.

## 대박, 헐, 짱, 완전: the four you hear in episode one

**대박** (*daebak*) originally meant a jackpot — a huge win. As slang it just means "that's enormous," and Koreans use it for a lottery win and a car crash with the same face-first energy. Length carries the meaning: a short **대박** is "nice," a stretched **대애애박** is "I cannot believe this."

**헐** (*heol*) is not a word so much as an exhale. It covers the half-second before you have processed news. English "what?!" is close, but 헐 is more passive — you are not asking a question, you are reacting.

**짱** (*jjang*) means the best or the top, and it also works as a suffix: **얼짱** (*eoljjang*) is "best face," **몸짱** (*momjjang*) is "best body." It has been around since the 90s, so it now reads slightly warm and dated to Koreans in their twenties — charming from a foreigner, not cutting-edge.

**완전** (*wanjeon*) technically means "completely," and in slang it bolts onto anything to crank it up. **완전 맛있어** (*wanjeon masisseo*) — "this is so good." **완전 내 스타일** (*wanjeon nae seutail*) — "completely my type." If you learn one intensifier, learn this one, because unlike the rougher options further down it is safe in almost any company.

Them: **이거 봐, 대박이지?** — *igeo bwa, daebagiji?* — "Look at this, insane right?"  
You: **헐, 완전 대박.** — *heol, wanjeon daebak.* — "Whoa, that's wild."  
Them: **그치? 짱이야.** — *geuchi? jjangiya.* — "Right? It's the best."

## 사이다 and 고구마: the two words a K-drama fan needs most

These two get their own section because no dictionary will explain them and every Korean comment section runs on them.

**고구마** (*goguma*) is a sweet potato. Eat one dry, with nothing to drink, and it lodges in your chest. That feeling — stuck, stifled, unable to breathe — is what Koreans call a **고구마 전개** (*goguma jeongae*), a "sweet potato plot": the heroine says nothing while she is being wronged, the misunderstanding drags for four more episodes, and you are yelling at your screen.

**사이다** (*saida*) is the lemon-lime soda you would have wanted with that sweet potato. A **사이다 전개** is the release — the moment the quiet character finally speaks, the villain gets caught, the resignation letter lands on the desk. Fans describe an actor as **사이다 같은 캐릭터** (*saida gateun kaerikteo*), "a character like soda."

You: **이 드라마 너무 고구마야.** — *i deurama neomu gogumaya.* — "This drama is so frustrating."  
Them: **8회 봐, 완전 사이다.** — *8hoe bwa, wanjeon saida.* — "Watch episode 8, it's pure payoff."

Two more that live in the same conversation. **케미** (*kemi*) is clipped English "chemistry," used for any pairing: **케미 좋아** (*kemi joa*), "they have great chemistry." And **썸** (*sseom*), from English "something," is the talking stage — not dating yet, definitely not nothing. **썸 타는 중** (*sseom taneun jung*) means "we're in that stage." ## The chopping machine: how new slang gets built

Most modern Korean slang is an abbreviation. Koreans take the first syllable of each word in a phrase and staple them together, and the result is unguessable until someone shows you the seam. Once you see the seam, the whole category opens up.

| Slang | Romanization | Built from | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| 노잼 | *nojaem* | no + 재미 (fun) | boring, not funny |
| 꿀잼 | *kkuljaem* | 꿀 (honey) + 재미 | hilarious, great fun |
| 심쿵 | *simkung* | 심장 (heart) + 쿵 (thud) | heart-stopping cute |
| 최애 | *choeae* | 최고 (best) + 애정 (affection) | absolute favorite, bias |
| 입덕 | *ipdeok* | 입 (enter) + 덕후 (fan) | falling into a fandom |
| 갑분싸 | *gapbunssa* | 갑자기 분위기 싸해짐 | the mood suddenly froze |
| 얼죽아 | *eoljuga* | 얼어 죽어도 아이스 아메리카노 | iced americano even in winter |
| 답정너 | *dapjeongneo* | 답은 정해져 있고 너는 대답만 | you already decided my answer |

Look at **갑분싸**. It is four words — *갑자기 분위기 싸해짐*, "suddenly the atmosphere went cold" — reduced to three syllables, and it is the single most useful word for describing a joke that did not land. **얼죽아** is even better as proof of the pattern: a full sentence about ordering iced coffee in freezing weather, folded into three syllables, now a personality type people claim about themselves.

**최애** is the one you will use most as a fan. It means your number-one — your bias — and it has a sibling, **차애** (*chaae*), for second place. **입덕** is the moment you fell in, **탈덕** (*taldeok*) is the moment you left, and **덕후** (*deokhu*) — a Korean respelling of Japanese *otaku* — is the fan themselves. Calling yourself a 덕후 is affectionate; calling someone else one is a gamble. The concert-floor versions of these are in [Korean fan phrases to know at a K-pop concert](/korean-phrases-kpop-concert).

## Korean slang in texting: the consonant shorthand

Korean typing slang strips words down to their initial consonants, so a message can look like it has no vowels at all. It is not a code — it is exactly how the letters sit on a Korean keyboard.

| Typed | Full word | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| ㅋㅋㅋ | 크크크 | laughing; more ㅋ = harder laugh |
| ㅎㅎ | 하하 | softer, friendlier laugh |
| ㅠㅠ / ㅜㅜ | — | crying eyes |
| ㅇㅇ | 응응 | "yeah, yeah" |
| ㄱㅅ | 감사 | thanks |
| ㅈㅅ | 죄송 | sorry |
| ㅇㅈ | 인정 | agreed, facts |
| ㄹㅇ | 리얼 | for real |
| ㅊㅋ | 축하 | congrats |

One live rule worth knowing: **ㅋㅋ counts**. A single **ㅋ** can read as cold or sarcastic, **ㅋㅋ** is neutral, **ㅋㅋㅋㅋㅋ** is genuinely amused. And **인정** written out loud, not abbreviated, is a full conversational move — someone makes a point, you say **인정** and that settles it. The longer messaging conventions, including who you can send ㅇㅇ to, are in [Korean phrases for KakaoTalk texting](/korean-phrases-kakaotalk-texting).

## 개- and 존-: stronger than the subtitles suggest

Korean has intensifiers that mean "extremely" and carry baggage. **개** (*gae*) is literally "dog," and prefixed to an adjective it means "super": **개맛있어** (*gaemasisseo*), "insanely tasty." Young Koreans use it constantly with friends. It is still crude. Said in front of a boss, a professor, or anyone's parents, it lands like swearing.

**존맛** (*jonmat*), often typed as **JMT**, means "unbelievably delicious," and its first syllable comes from a vulgar root most Koreans no longer think about. Same rule applies: everywhere online, never at a company dinner.

The safe swap is the one from earlier — **완전**. **완전 맛있어** gets you the same enthusiasm with none of the risk, and **진짜** (*jinjja*), "really," works identically. When you do not know the room, use those two. ## Where slang goes wrong for learners

**Using slang up the age ladder.** Almost every word on this page is **반말** territory — casual speech for friends and people younger than you. Slang plus a **-요** ending does not fix it; **대박이에요** to a client still sounds like a teenager. Fix the relationship first: [Korean family titles: how to address people in Korea](/korean-family-titles-how-to-address-people) covers who you are allowed to be casual with.

**Reaching for the newest word.** Slang from this year, used slightly wrong, marks you faster than slang from ten years ago used correctly. 대박 and 완전 never sound bad. A trend word you picked up from one clip often does.

**Saying 헐 as "hull."** It is one short syllable, closer to *huhl*, cut off fast. Stretched out, it stops being a reaction and becomes a performance.

**Translating 사이다 as "cider."** Korean 사이다 is clear lemon-lime soda, not apple cider. Order 사이다 in a restaurant and you get Sprite.

**Collecting instead of speaking.** Reading forty slang words is an afternoon. Producing **완전 고구마야** at the right beat in a real conversation, without a two-second delay, is a different skill — and it is the one that makes a Korean friend answer you in Korean instead of switching to English.
