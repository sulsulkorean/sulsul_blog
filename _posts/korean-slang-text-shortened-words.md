Most Korean text slang is not new vocabulary at all. It is ordinary words squeezed into fewer syllables, spelled the way people actually say them fast. **걍** (*gyang*) is **그냥** (*geunyang*, "just"). **넘** (*neom*) is **너무** (*neomu*, "so / too"). **낼** (*nael*) is **내일** (*naeil*, "tomorrow"). **담에** (*dame*) is **다음에** (*daeume*, "next time"), so **담에 봐요** is simply "see you next time." Once you can hear the long word inside the short one, a Korean group chat stops looking like a code and starts looking like normal speech typed in a hurry.

> - **걍** ← 그냥 — just, for no reason
> - **넘** ← 너무 — so, too much
> - **낼** ← 내일 — tomorrow
> - **담에** ← 다음에 — next time
> - **젤** ← 제일 — the most
> - **첨** ← 처음 — the first time
> - **근데** ← 그런데 — but, by the way
> - **어케** ← 어떻게 — how

## How a two-syllable word becomes one

Korean syllables are blocks, and when two blocks sit next to each other with a weak sound in between, spoken Korean tends to melt them together. Texting just writes that melted version down.

Look at **그냥**. Said quickly, the 으 vowel almost disappears, and 그 + 냥 slides into **걍**. **다음에** loses its middle 으 the same way: 다 + 음 becomes **담**, and you get **담에**. **제일** collapses into **젤**, **처음** into **첨**, **마음** into **맘**.

So the rule for reading these is simple: when a one-syllable word looks unfamiliar, try stretching it back into two. **낼** has to be 내일. **젤** has to be 제일. You will guess right far more often than you expect, because nobody invented these spellings. They are just the sound.

This is different from the consonant shortcuts like ㅋㅋ and ㅇㅇ, which keep only the first letter of each syllable. Those are covered in [what ㅋㅋ means in Korean texting](/what-does-kk-mean-in-korean-texting). The words on this page are still full syllables you can read aloud.

## Plans and time: 낼, 담에, 이따, 첨

Making plans is where you will see these the most, because plans get typed quickly between other things.

**낼 시간 돼?** (*nael sigan dwae?*) — "You free tomorrow?" The full version, 내일 시간 돼?, is exactly the same sentence.

**담에 보자** (*dame boja*) — "Let's meet next time." Friends end almost every chat that doesn't end in a plan with this. The polite version is **담에 봐요** or, written out in full, **다음에 봐요** (*daeume bwayo*), which is fine to say to almost anyone.

**이따 연락할게** (*itta yeollakhalge*) — "I'll message you later." 이따 is already the short form of 이따가, and in texts it is the default.

**첨 가 봐** (*cheom ga bwa*) — "It's my first time going." 첨 is 처음, and you will see it in reviews too: **첨 먹어 봤는데 넘 맛있어** — "first time trying it, so good."

## Feelings and emphasis: 넘, 젤, 걍, 맘

These are the words that carry the mood of a message.

**넘** is probably the single most common shortened word in Korean texting. **넘 귀여워** (*neom gwiyeowo*) — "so cute." **넘 피곤해** (*neom pigonhae*) — "so tired." Fans type it under every idol photo, often doubled: **넘넘 좋아**.

**젤** turns anything into a top pick. **이 노래 젤 좋아** (*i norae jel joa*) — "I like this song the most." Ask a friend **누가 젤 좋아?** and you are asking who their bias is.

**걍** is a shrug in one syllable. **걍 집에 있을래** (*gyang jibe isseullae*) — "I'll just stay home." It also answers "why?" on its own: **왜?** — **걍.** "Why?" "No reason." Said in a flat tone, that one-word answer can sound a little closed off, so add ㅎㅎ if you want it to feel friendly.

**맘** is 마음, heart or mind, and it shows up in soft messages. **맘에 들어?** (*mame deureo?*) — "Do you like it?" **맘 편히 쉬어** — "Rest easy."

## The little linking words: 근데, 암튼, 땜에, 어케, 왤케

These glue sentences together, and they are so common in speech that many Koreans hardly notice they are shortened.

**근데** (*geunde*) is 그런데, "but" or "by the way." It is also how people change the subject: **근데 그 드라마 봤어?** — "By the way, did you watch that drama?"

**암튼** (*amteun*) is 아무튼, "anyway." It closes a long rant and moves on.

**땜에** (*ttaeme*) is 때문에, "because of." **너 땜에 웃었어** — "I laughed because of you."

**어케** (*eoke*) is 어떻게, "how." **어케 알았어?** — "How did you know?" In a K-drama text scene, a character staring at their phone in shock is very often reading 어케.

**왤케** (*waelke*) is 왜 이렇게, "why so." **왤케 늦어?** — "Why are you so late?" It squeezes three syllables into two and sounds playful rather than angry.

## People and things: 남친, 여친, 쌤, 생파, 겜

Nouns get cut too, usually by keeping the first syllable of each part.

**남친** (*namchin*) and **여친** (*yeochin*) are 남자친구 and 여자친구, boyfriend and girlfriend. These are so standard that people say them out loud, and K-drama characters use them in ordinary dialogue.

**쌤** (*ssaem*) is 선생님, teacher. Students call their teachers 쌤 to their faces in a friendly way, but it is still casual, so stick with 선생님 until you hear them use it first.

**생파** (*saengpa*) is 생일 파티, birthday party. **겜** (*gem*) is 게임, a game. **카톡** (*katok*) is KakaoTalk itself, and **카톡해** means "message me."

## Cute spellings: 넹, 웅, 고마웡

Some texters add a soft ㅇ to the end of a word to make it sound cuter, the written version of a little 애교 voice. **네** becomes **넹**, **응** becomes **웅**, **고마워** becomes **고마웡**, and **알겠어** becomes **알겠어용**.

Between close friends or couples this reads as sweet. With a coworker it can read as too much, and from someone you just met it can feel odd. If you want the spoken version of this tone, [aegyo lines in Korean](/aegyo-lines-in-korean) covers how it sounds out loud.

## A real chat, decoded

Here is a short chat between two friends:

A: **낼 뭐해?** — *nael mwohae?* — "What are you doing tomorrow?"
B: **걍 집에 있을 듯 ㅋㅋ 왜?** — *gyang jibe isseul deut* — "Probably just staying home lol, why?"
A: **생파 할 건데 올래? 첨 가는 데라 넘 기대돼** — *saengpa hal geonde ollae? cheom ganeun dera neom gidaedwae* — "I'm having a birthday party, wanna come? It's a place I've never been, I'm so excited"
B: **헐 갈게! 근데 어케 가?** — *heol galge! geunde eoke ga?* — "Oh, I'll come! But how do I get there?"
A: **이따 카톡으로 보낼게** — *itta katogeuro bonaelge* — "I'll send it on KakaoTalk later"
B: **웅 고마웡 담에 밥 살게** — *ung gomawong dame bap salge* — "Okay, thanks! I'll buy you a meal next time"

Count the shortened words: 낼, 걍, 생파, 첨, 넘, 근데, 어케, 이따, 카톡, 웅, 고마웡, 담에. Twelve in six lines, and every one of them is an everyday word you already know in its full form.

## When the short spelling sounds wrong

**Using them with someone older or senior.** 낼, 걍, and 넘 are 반말 territory. To a boss, a teacher, or a friend's parent, write the full word with a polite ending: **내일 시간 괜찮으세요?** instead of 낼 시간 돼?. The shortened spelling on its own already signals "we are close."

**Writing them in anything formal.** Emails, job applications, and messages to a landlord use the full spelling. 담에 in a formal note looks like a typo.

**Saying 걍 to end a conversation.** A one-word **걍** in reply to a real question can feel like you are brushing someone off. Give a reason, or soften it: **걍 피곤해서 ㅎㅎ** — "just tired, hehe."

**Pronouncing them as written letters.** Many of these, like 근데, 넘, 담에, 남친, and 생파, are said out loud exactly as typed. Others, like 어케 and 왤케, sound natural in a fast casual voice but odd if you say them slowly and carefully. When in doubt, speak the full word.

**Guessing the wrong long form.** 넘 is almost always 너무, not 넘다 (to cross). 맘 is 마음, not "mom," although some people do use 맘 for mothers in words like 워킹맘. Context sorts it out quickly.
