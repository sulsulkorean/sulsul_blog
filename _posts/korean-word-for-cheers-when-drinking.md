The Korean word for "cheers" is **건배** (*geon-bae*), but at most tables you'll hear **짠** (*jjan*) instead. 건배 is the proper word, the one a manager says standing up at a company dinner. 짠 is the sound of two glasses touching, and it's what friends say when the soju arrives. If you only learn one, learn 짠: it works with friends, with your Korean host family, and at any BBQ place where everyone is your age. Switch to 건배 when the table is formal or someone older is leading the toast.

> - **짠!** (*jjan*) — "Cheers!" Casual, the everyday one.
> - **건배!** (*geon-bae*) — "Cheers!" Formal and group toasts.
> - **원샷!** (*won-syat*) — "Bottoms up!" Finish the glass.
> - **위하여!** (*wi-ha-yeo*) — "To…!" The answer to a toast.
> - **한 잔 해요.** (*han jan hae-yo*) — "Let's have a drink."
> - **조금만 주세요.** (*jo-geum-man ju-se-yo*) — "Just a little, please."

## 짠 or 건배: which one to say

Both words mean "cheers." The difference is the room you're in.

**짠** is an onomatopoeia, like "clink." It's light and friendly, and you can say it with almost anyone once the drinks are on the table. Friends say it, couples say it, and a group of coworkers on the second round says it too. You can even say it to a parent or an older friend. The word itself isn't rude; it's just relaxed.

**건배** comes from Chinese characters meaning "dry cup," the same root as Japanese *kanpai* and Chinese *ganbei*. In Korea it signals that this is a real toast. You'll hear it at weddings, year-end parties, team dinners, and anywhere one person stands up and speaks before everyone drinks.

A quick rule of thumb:

| Situation | What to say |
|---|---|
| Friends, chimaek, a night out | **짠!** |
| Host family dinner, first glass | **건배!** or **짠!** (follow whoever goes first) |
| Company dinner (회식), boss raises a glass | **건배!** |
| Someone gives a speech and shouts a toast | Repeat their last word, usually **위하여!** |

When in doubt, wait one second. Someone at the table will say it first, and you echo it.

## The first glass, 원샷, and what "one shot" really means

The first drink of the night is a small ceremony. Glasses get filled, someone says **자, 짠!** ("Okay, cheers!") or **건배!**, and many groups drink that first glass in one go.

That's **원샷**, Konglish for "one shot." It means "finish the glass now," and it's usually said right after the toast: **짠! 원샷!** You'll also hear **첫 잔은 원샷** ("The first glass is one shot"), which is a common table custom, not a law. A soju glass is small, so one shot is a few sips. A beer glass is not, so don't feel trapped by it.

If you don't want to finish it, these lines are normal and polite:

- **반만 마실게요.** (*ban-man ma-sil-ge-yo*) — "I'll drink half."
- **천천히 마실게요.** (*cheon-cheon-hi ma-sil-ge-yo*) — "I'll drink slowly."
- **저는 술을 잘 못 마셔요.** (*jeo-neun su-reul jal mot ma-syeo-yo*) — "I can't drink much."

You can still lift your glass and say 짠 with water, juice, or cola. Nobody will think twice. The clink is about joining the moment, not about what's inside the glass.

## 위하여 and other toasts you'll hear

At bigger gatherings, one person makes a short toast called a **건배사**. They say a line, then shout a cue, and everyone answers. The most common pattern:

> **Leader:** 우리 팀의 건강을 위하여!
> *(To our team's health!)*
>
> **Everyone:** 위하여!
> *(To it!)*

**위하여** means "for the sake of." You'll hear it after almost any toast: **여러분의 행복을 위하여!** (To everyone's happiness!) or **우리의 우정을 위하여!** (To our friendship!). All you have to do is shout **위하여!** back with your glass up.

Some offices use playful acronym toasts. The leader says the first part and the table answers the second. You won't need to make these yourself as a visitor, but it helps to know that a strange word before the clink is almost always a toast, and the safe move is to repeat what everyone else shouts.

## Clinking with someone older

Korean drinking manners mostly come down to age. These small moves matter more than the word you choose:

- **Hold your glass lower.** When you clink with someone older, let your glass touch below the rim of theirs. It's a quiet sign of respect.
- **Use two hands.** Hold the glass with one hand and support it with the other, or touch your wrist or chest with your free hand.
- **Turn your head slightly.** When you drink in front of someone much older, turn a little to the side while you sip. Young Koreans do this with bosses and grandparents.
- **Don't pour your own glass.** Pour for others and let them pour for you. When someone pours for you, lift your glass with two hands.

When an older person pours for you, a simple **감사합니다** is enough. When you pour for them, say **제가 따라 드릴게요** (*je-ga tta-ra deu-ril-ge-yo*), "Let me pour for you."

## Lines for the rest of the night

Once the toast is done, the glasses keep moving. These are the lines you'll actually use:

- **한 잔 더 하실래요?** (*han jan deo ha-sil-lae-yo*) — "Would you like one more?"
- **잔 비었어요.** (*jan bi-eo-sseo-yo*) — "Your glass is empty." Usually said while picking up the bottle.
- **소주 한 병 더 주세요.** (*so-ju han byeong deo ju-se-yo*) — "One more bottle of soju, please." Say it to the server.
- **소맥 말아 줄게요.** (*so-maek ma-ra jul-ge-yo*) — "I'll mix you a somaek." Soju plus beer.
- **이제 그만 마실게요.** (*i-je geu-man ma-sil-ge-yo*) — "I'll stop drinking now."
- **취했어요.** (*chwi-hae-sseo-yo*) — "I'm drunk."

A useful trick: a full glass means "I'm done for now." Koreans usually refill empty glasses, so if you leave yours with a little left in it, people will stop topping you up.

## Mistakes visitors make with 건배 and 짠

- **Saying "kampai."** Koreans understand it, but it marks you as thinking of Japan. Say 건배.
- **Pronouncing 짠 like "jan."** The ㅉ is tense. Squeeze the sound: *jjan*, short and sharp.
- **Clinking and then putting the glass down.** After 짠, at least take a sip before setting it down. Clinking without drinking feels like leaving a high-five hanging.
- **Drinking on the street with a cup from the bar.** Rules depend on the place. If you plan to keep going outside, read [can you drink and walk in Korea](/can-you-drink-and-walk-in-korea) first.
- **Forcing 원샷 on others.** You can invite it, but if a friend says 천천히 마실게요, let it go. Pushing drinks is seen as old-fashioned today.

## A short drinking conversation

> **A:** 다 따랐어요? 자, 짠!
> *(Everyone's glass full? Okay, cheers!)*
>
> **B:** 짠! 첫 잔은 원샷이죠?
> *(Cheers! The first one's bottoms up, right?)*
>
> **You:** 저는 반만 마실게요. 술을 잘 못 마셔요.
> *(I'll just drink half. I can't drink much.)*
>
> **A:** 괜찮아요, 천천히 드세요. 우리의 첫 여행을 위하여!
> *(That's fine, take it slow. To our first trip!)*
>
> **Everyone:** 위하여!

## Practice the toast out loud

Toasts happen fast. Someone says 짠 and the glasses are already moving, so the word needs to come out of you without a pause. Say these three lines out loud until they feel easy: **자, 짠!**, **건배!**, and **위하여!** Then add the polite exits, **조금만 주세요** and **천천히 마실게요**, because those are the ones you'll be glad you know at 11 p.m.
