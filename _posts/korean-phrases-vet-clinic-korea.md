A **동물병원** (*dongmulbyeongwon*, animal hospital) is the one appointment where you have to describe symptoms for a patient who cannot describe them — and you have to do it in Korean, at a counter, with a nervous animal in your arms. Two patterns from the SULSUL workbook carry the whole visit. Pattern 08 — the past tense **-았/었어요** — is how you report what already happened: **어제부터 밥을 안 먹었어요** (*eojebuteo babeul an meogeosseoyo*), "It hasn't eaten since yesterday." Pattern 52 — **-ㄴ/은/는 것 같아요** — is how you offer a guess without claiming to be the vet: **어디가 아픈 것 같아요** (*eodiga apeun geot gatayo*), "Something seems to hurt."

> - Check in: **예약했는데요** (*yeyakhaenneundeyo*), "I have an appointment."
> - Say what happened: **어제부터 밥을 안 먹었어요** (*eojebuteo babeul an meogeosseoyo*), "It hasn't eaten since yesterday."
> - Offer a guess: **어디가 아픈 것 같아요** (*eodiga apeun geot gatayo*), "Something seems to hurt."
> - Ask about the medicine: **약은 어떻게 먹여요?** (*yageun eotteoke meogyeoyo?*), "How do I give the medicine?"
> - Ask about a follow-up: **언제 다시 와야 해요?** (*eonje dasi waya haeyo?*), "When do I come back?"

## How do you check in at a Korean vet clinic?

The front desk asks for three things: whether you booked, what animal, and what is wrong in one line. Answer all three before they finish asking and the rest of the visit is calm.

Them: **어떻게 오셨어요?** — *eotteoke osyeosseoyo?* — "What brings you in?"  
You: **예약했는데요. 강아지가 밥을 안 먹어요.** — *yeyakhaenneundeyo. gangajiga babeul an meogeoyo.* — "I have an appointment. My dog isn't eating."  
Them: **네, 잠시만 기다려 주세요.** — *ne, jamsiman gidaryeo juseyo.* — "Okay, one moment please."

**강아지** (*gangaji*) is a dog, **고양이** (*goyangi*) is a cat, and cats are expected to arrive in an **이동장** (*idongjang*, carrier) rather than in your arms. If you have no booking, **예약 안 했는데 진료 돼요?** (*yeyak an haenneunde jillyo dwaeyo?*), "I didn't book — can you still see us?" is the walk-in line. Bring the **접종 수첩** (*jeopjong sucheop*), the vaccination booklet, if the clinic has given you one; **접종 수첩 여기 있어요** (*jeopjong sucheop yeogi isseoyo*) saves a whole conversation.

## How do you describe what is wrong?

Vets ask two questions in every language: what is happening, and since when. Pattern 08 answers both at once, because Korean puts the "since when" in front and the past tense at the end.

Them: **언제부터 그랬어요?** — *eonjebuteo geuraesseoyo?* — "Since when has it been like this?"  
You: **어제 저녁부터 그랬어요. 두 번 토했어요.** — *eoje jeonyeokbuteo geuraesseoyo. du beon tohaesseoyo.* — "Since last night. It vomited twice."  
Them: **설사도 했어요?** — *seolsado haesseoyo?* — "Any diarrhoea as well?"  
You: **네, 오늘 아침에 했어요.** — *ne, oneul achime haesseoyo.* — "Yes, this morning."

The handful of symptom verbs is short enough to memorise in one sitting: **토했어요** (*tohaesseoyo*), "vomited"; **설사해요** (*seolsahaeyo*), "has diarrhoea"; **기운이 없어요** (*giuni eopseoyo*), "has no energy"; **다리를 절어요** (*darireul jeoreoyo*), "is limping"; **자꾸 긁어요** (*jakku geulgeoyo*), "keeps scratching." Then Pattern 52 lets you add what you suspect without overstating it — **뭘 잘못 먹은 것 같아요** (*mwol jalmot meogeun geot gatayo*), "I think it ate something it shouldn't have," or **좀 아픈 것 같아요** (*jom apeun geot gatayo*), "it seems a bit unwell." That softness is the point: **아파요** is a diagnosis, **아픈 것 같아요** is an observation, and the second is what a vet actually wants from you. The same two patterns run your own doctor's appointment, which [how to see a doctor in Korea](/see-doctor-korea-korean-phrases) covers end to end.

## What usually goes wrong at the 동물병원?

The first thing is walking out without understanding the instructions. Medicine for animals comes with a dose and a schedule, and the sentence to ask is **약은 어떻게 먹여요?** (*yageun eotteoke meogyeoyo?*) — note **먹여요** (*meogyeoyo*), "feed it," not **먹어요**, which would mean you are taking it. Follow with **하루에 몇 번이에요?** (*harue myeot beonieyo?*), "How many times a day?" If the answer moves too fast, **다시 말해 주세요** (*dasi malhae juseyo*) is always allowed, and [what to say when Korean is too fast](/korean-phrases-when-korean-is-too-fast) has the rest of that toolkit. Anything you then pick up at a human pharmacy runs on the phrasing in [Korean phrases for the pharmacy in Korea](/korean-phrases-pharmacy-korea).

The second is missing the follow-up. Vaccinations and treatments in Korea are usually scheduled in rounds, so end every visit with **언제 다시 와야 해요?** (*eonje dasi waya haeyo?*) and write the date down at the counter. **예방접종 하러 왔어요** (*yebangjeopjong hareo wasseoyo*), "I came for a vaccination," is the opening line for those return trips.

The third is paperwork you did not know about. Dogs in Korea are registered with a microchip or tag under **동물등록** (*dongmul deungnok*), and any clinic will tell you where you stand if you ask **동물등록 여기서 돼요?** (*dongmul deungnok yeogiseo dwaeyo?*), "Can I do the pet registration here?" Registration details are tied to your address, so it belongs on the same list as the errands in [Korean phrases for the community service center in Korea](/korean-phrases-community-service-center-korea) and [Korean phrases at the apartment management office in Korea](/korean-phrases-apartment-management-office-korea). When you are unsure of any of it, the openers in [how to politely ask for help in Korean](/politely-ask-for-help-in-korean) get a staff member to slow down and start over with you.
