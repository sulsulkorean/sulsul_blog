"Love story" in Korean is **러브 스토리** (*reobeu seutori*) or, in plain Korean, **사랑 이야기** (*sarang iyagi*). But when Korean fans talk about a K-drama love story, they rarely stop there. They name the setup: a **재벌** heir, a **첫사랑** who comes back, a **계약 결혼** that turns real, a **삼각관계** nobody wins. Most romances mix two or three of these, so once you know the words you can follow a Korean plot summary and explain your favorite couple in one sentence.

> - **재벌** (*jaebeol*) — a family that owns a giant business group, or someone born into one.
> - **첫사랑** (*cheotsarang*) — first love.
> - **계약 결혼** (*gyeyak gyeolhon*) — contract marriage.
> - **삼각관계** (*samgakgwangye*) — love triangle.
> - **기억상실** (*gieoksangsil*) — memory loss, amnesia.
> - **운명** (*unmyeong*) — fate, destiny.
> - **클리셰** (*keullisye*) — cliché. Fans say it with affection.

## The rich one: 재벌 and the office titles that go with it

**재벌** is the word English borrowed as "chaebol." In a love story, the male lead is often **재벌 3세** (*jaebeol samse*), the "third generation": the grandson of the founder. Opposite the heir is a lead with bills to pay, and the gap between them is **신분 차이** (*sinbun chai*), a difference in social standing.

You will also hear the heir called by a job title instead of a name. These three come up most:

- **실장님** (*siljangnim*) — the head of an office or team. The classic romantic-lead title of older dramas.
- **본부장님** (*bonbujangnim*) — the head of a division.
- **대표님** (*daepyonim*) — the head of the company.

The moment a lead stops saying **본부장님** and uses the first name, the relationship has changed. Then comes **집안 반대** (*jiban bandae*), opposition from the family, with the line every fan can finish: **우리 아들하고 헤어져요** (*uri adeulhago heeojeoyo*), "Break up with my son."

## The one from the past: 첫사랑, 운명, and 재회

**첫사랑** is **첫** (first) plus **사랑** (love). It can mean the feeling or the person, so **내 첫사랑이었어** (*nae cheotsarangieosseo*) is "You were my first love" or "That was my first love," depending on the scene.

Koreans also have a saying about it: **첫사랑은 안 이루어진대** (*cheotsarangeun an irueojindae*), "They say first love never works out." When a character says this in episode one, the writer is usually about to prove it wrong.

The engine of these stories is **재회** (*jaehoe*), a reunion after years apart. Around it you get:

- **우리 전에 만난 적 있어요?** (*uri jeone mannan jeok isseoyo?*) — "Have we met before?"
- **이건 운명이야.** (*igeon unmyeongiya*) — "This is fate."
- **하필 왜 너야?** (*hapil wae neoya?*) — "Why did it have to be you, of all people?"

**하필** is a useful little word. It means "of all the possible options," and it always carries a sigh.

## The deal: 계약 결혼 and 정략결혼 are not the same

Both get translated as marriages without love, but the Korean separates who decided.

**계약 결혼** is a contract marriage. The two leads agree to it themselves, usually with rules and an end date: **딱 일 년만이에요** (*ttak il nyeonmanieyo*), "Just one year, exactly." The contract scene nearly always includes **감정은 빼고** (*gamjeongeun ppaego*), "leaving feelings out of it." You know how that goes.

**정략결혼** (*jeongnyakgyeolhon*) is a marriage arranged by two families for money or power. The couple may not have been asked. If you see this word in a plot summary, expect company mergers and cold dinners.

Fans have a label for the way these stories unfold: **선결혼 후연애** (*seongyeolhon huyeonae*), "marriage first, romance after." **선** means before and **후** means after, and you will see the same pair in other Korean expressions.

## The obstacles: 삼각관계, 혐관, and 기억상실

**삼각관계** is literally a "triangle relationship." The leads are **남주** (*namju*) and **여주** (*yeoju*), short for male lead and female lead, and the kind man who does not get the girl is the **서브남주** (*seobeunamju*), the second male lead. Falling for him as a viewer is **서브 앓이** (*seobeu ari*): **앓이** comes from the verb for being ill, so you are lovesick for the sub. The scene you are waiting for has this line: **나야, 걔야?** (*naya, gyaeya?*), "Me or him?" (**걔** can also mean "her.")

**혐관** (*hyeomgwan*) is newer fan slang for an enemies-to-lovers setup. It shortens **혐오 관계**, a relationship of dislike. You will meet it online far more than in spoken dialogue.

**기억상실** is the amnesia plot. One lead wakes up in hospital and says **누구세요?** (*nuguseyo?*), "Who are you?" The other answers **나 기억 안 나?** (*na gieok an na?*), "You don't remember me?" Notice the gap: the first line is polite because it is spoken to a stranger, the second is casual because it is spoken to a lover. The speech level alone tells you who has lost what.

## Describing a love story in one sentence

The pattern is short: **[what happens] + -는 이야기예요** (*-neun iyagiyeyo*), "It's a story about …"

- **재벌이랑 계약 결혼하는 이야기예요.** (*jaebeorirang gyeyak gyeolhonhaneun iyagiyeyo*) — "It's about a contract marriage with a chaebol."
- **첫사랑을 다시 만나는 이야기예요.** (*cheotsarangeul dasi mannaneun iyagiyeyo*) — "It's about meeting a first love again."

If you have a noun instead of an action, put it straight in front: **재벌 이야기예요**, "It's a chaebol story." To ask, say **무슨 이야기예요?** (*museun iyagiyeyo?*), "What's it about?"

And to say what you like: **저는 계약 결혼 나오는 드라마 좋아해요** (*jeoneun gyeyak gyeolhon naoneun deurama joahaeyo*), "I like dramas with a contract marriage in them." **나오는** means "that appears," so you can swap in **재벌 나오는**, **첫사랑 나오는**, or any word from this page.

## Two friends and one very predictable drama

> **A:** 그 드라마 어때?
> *geu deurama eottae?*
> "How's that drama?"
>
> **B:** 재벌에 계약 결혼에 첫사랑까지 다 나와.
> *jaebeore gyeyak gyeolhone cheotsarangkkaji da nawa.*
> "It's got a chaebol, a contract marriage, even a first love. Everything."
>
> **A:** 완전 클리셰네. 근데 왜 봐?
> *wanjeon keullisyene. geunde wae bwa?*
> "Total cliché. So why are you watching it?"
>
> **B:** 뻔한데 설레니까.
> *ppeonhande seollenikka.*
> "Because it's predictable, but it makes my heart flutter."
>
> **A:** 결말은?
> *gyeolmareun?*
> "And the ending?"
>
> **B:** 말 안 해. 직접 봐!
> *mal an hae. jikjeop bwa!*
> "Not telling. Watch it yourself!"

Two words here are worth keeping. **뻔하다** (*ppeonhada*) is "to be obvious, predictable," and **설레다** (*seolleda*) is that fluttery feeling before something good. On endings, there are three: **해피엔딩** (*haepiending*), **새드엔딩** (*saedeuending*), and **열린 결말** (*yeollin gyeolmal*), the "open ending" that starts arguments.

## Where fans trip over these words

**Calling someone 재벌 for having a nice car.** **재벌** means business-dynasty money. For an ordinary well-off person, say **부자** (*buja*).

**Using 첫사랑 for a first date or first partner.** Your **첫사랑** is the first person you truly loved. Many Koreans mean someone they never dated at all. A first partner is **첫 남자친구** or **첫 여자친구**.

**Mixing up 남주 and 남자친구.** **남주** is a role in a story. Your own partner is a **남친** or **여친**, and the words for real relationships are in [K-drama romance words, from 썸 to breakup](/k-drama-romance-korean-words).

**Keeping drama speech with strangers.** **하필 왜 너야?** and **나 기억 안 나?** are casual lines between people who are close. With someone new, stay with **-요**, as in **무슨 이야기예요?**
