To say thank you in Korean, say **감사합니다** (*gamsahamnida*) to anyone you do not know well — shop staff, a stranger who helped you, a taxi driver, someone older than you. To someone you are close to, say **고마워요** (*gomawoyo*), or **고마워** (*gomawo*) if you are the same age or they are younger. That is the whole decision, and **감사합니다** alone will carry you through an entire trip without ever sounding wrong. The part that confuses most learners is that **감사합니다** and **고맙습니다** (*gomapseumnida*) are *equally* polite — they are not two rungs on a ladder. They are two different words that landed on the same formal ending, and Koreans pick between them by feel, not by rank.

> - Thank you (to anyone) — **감사합니다** (*gamsahamnida*)
> - Thank you (same formality, warmer) — **고맙습니다** (*gomapseumnida*)
> - Thanks (polite but closer) — **고마워요** (*gomawoyo*)
> - Thanks (to friends) — **고마워** (*gomawo*)
> - Thank you so much — **정말 감사합니다** (*jeongmal gamsahamnida*)
> - You're welcome — **아니에요** (*anieyo*)

## 감사합니다, 고맙습니다, 고마워요 — which one and when

Start with the ending, because that is where the politeness actually lives. **-습니다 / -합니다** is the formal polite ending. **-어요 / -아요** is the everyday polite ending. **-어 / -아** with nothing after it is **반말**, casual speech. Gratitude follows the same ladder as every other Korean verb.

| Say this | To whom | Feel |
|---|---|---|
| **감사합니다** (*gamsahamnida*) | strangers, staff, elders, anyone | safe, correct everywhere |
| **고맙습니다** (*gomapseumnida*) | same as above | equally formal, a touch warmer |
| **고마워요** (*gomawoyo*) | a friendly coworker, a neighbour, someone slightly older you know | polite but close |
| **고마워** (*gomawo*) | friends, younger people, family | casual |
| **감사드립니다** (*gamsadeurimnida*) | emails, speeches, announcements | ceremonial |

Now the root difference, which is the real answer to "why are there two?" **감사** comes from Sino-Korean characters meaning gratitude, so **감사합니다** is literally "I do gratitude." **고맙다** is a native Korean word for being grateful, so **고맙습니다** is the home-grown version. Sino-Korean vocabulary carries a faintly official tone in Korean — the same way "gratitude" feels more formal than "thanks" in English even though both are polite. That is the entire gap between them. Use either one in a shop and nobody will blink.

One asymmetry worth knowing: **고마워요** is extremely common, but **감사해요** is not. It exists grammatically, yet it sounds slightly off to most ears, so when you want to step down from formal to friendly, step down the **고맙다** side of the family and say **고마워요**.

## How 감사합니다 is actually pronounced

The spelling lies a little. **합니다** is written with a **ㅂ**, but a **ㅂ** sitting in front of a **ㄴ** turns into **ㅁ** in speech. So **감사합니다** comes out as *gam-sa-**ham**-ni-da*, never *gam-sa-hap-ni-da*. The same rule is why **고맙습니다** lands as *go-map-**sseum**-ni-da*, with the **습** tightened.

Two more things that make it sound native rather than recited. The stress is flat — Korean does not punch one syllable the way English does, so resist the urge to hit *GAM-sa*. And in fast real speech the first syllable softens toward *kam*, which is why you will hear "kamsahamnida" in dramas. Aim for even, unhurried syllables and it reads as respectful.

## The bow is part of the phrase

In Korea, gratitude comes out of the body as much as the mouth, and getting this wrong is more visible than a pronunciation slip. A quick nod of the head — chin down, back straight, about one second — is what goes with **감사합니다** to a cashier, a barista, or a stranger who pointed you to the right exit. Anything deeper looks theatrical in a convenience store.

For someone clearly older, a teacher, or a person who went out of their way for you, bend from the waist maybe fifteen to thirty degrees and hold it a beat longer. The deep ninety-degree bow you see in period dramas belongs to apologies, ceremonies, and idols thanking a stadium — not to daily life.

Two practical notes. If your hands are full, the nod alone is enough; nobody expects a performance. And when a shop worker says **감사합니다** to *you* on the way out, you are not obliged to match it — a nod, a **네~** (*ne*), or **수고하세요** (*sugohaseyo*, roughly "keep up the good work") is the normal exchange. ## Thanking someone *for* something: 주셔서 감사합니다

Bare **감사합니다** is complete on its own, but naming what you are grateful for is what turns a reflex into a sentence. The frame is simple: take a verb, attach **-아/어 주셔서**, and finish with **감사합니다**. The **주셔서** part means "because you did it for me," so the whole thing reads "thank you for doing that for me."

- **도와주셔서 감사합니다** — *dowajusyeoseo gamsahamnida* — "Thank you for helping me."
- **알려 주셔서 감사합니다** — *allyeo jusyeoseo gamsahamnida* — "Thank you for letting me know."
- **기다려 주셔서 감사합니다** — *gidaryeo jusyeoseo gamsahamnida* — "Thank you for waiting."
- **찍어 주셔서 감사합니다** — *jjigeo jusyeoseo gamsahamnida* — "Thank you for taking the photo."
- **와 주셔서 감사합니다** — *wa jusyeoseo gamsahamnida* — "Thank you for coming."

Among friends, the same frame relaxes to **-아/어 줘서 고마워**: **도와줘서 고마워** (*dowajwoseo gomawo*), "thanks for helping." Notice that **주셔서** drops to **줘서** — the honorific **시** disappears with it.

There is one more phrase that native speakers reach for constantly and textbooks rarely teach: **덕분에** (*deokbune*), "thanks to you." **덕분에 잘 찾았어요** (*deokbune jal chajasseoyo*) means "I found it easily thanks to you," and plain **덕분이에요** (*deokbunieyo*), "it's thanks to you," is a graceful thing to say when someone brushes off their own help. Use it for good outcomes only — it credits a person for something that went well.

You: **도와주셔서 감사합니다.** — *dowajusyeoseo gamsahamnida.* — "Thank you for helping me."
Them: **아니에요, 별말씀을요.** — *anieyo, byeolmalsseumeuryo.* — "Not at all, don't mention it."
You: **덕분에 잘 찾았어요.** — *deokbune jal chajasseoyo.* — "I found it easily thanks to you."

## What to say back: 아니에요, not 천만에요

When someone thanks *you*, the everyday reply is **아니에요** (*anieyo*) — literally "it's not," in the sense of "it was nothing." It is Pattern 18 in the SULSUL workbook, the **맞아요 / 아니에요** pair that also handles agreeing, declining, and waving off a compliment. Stretch the vowel a little and warm it up: **아니에요~**.

| Reply | Romanization | Use it when |
|---|---|---|
| **아니에요** | *anieyo* | the default "you're welcome" |
| **아닙니다** | *animnida* | same thing, formal register |
| **괜찮아요** | *gwaenchanayo* | "it's fine, no trouble" |
| **별말씀을요** | *byeolmalsseumeuryo* | "don't mention it," polite and gracious |
| **네~** | *ne* | a nod plus this is what most people actually do |

And the one to retire: **천만에요** (*cheonmaneyo*). Nearly every phrasebook lists it as "you're welcome," and almost nobody says it out loud. Using it is not an error, but it marks you instantly as someone reciting a book. Say **아니에요** instead.

## Where you will actually need it in a day

Gratitude is the highest-frequency phrase in a traveller's day, and it clusters in predictable places: the moment change lands in your hand, the moment a stranger points at the right exit, the moment a stranger hands your phone back after taking your photo, the moment food arrives at the table.

- Paying and getting change back — **감사합니다** as you take the card or the bag
- After someone gives directions — **감사합니다** with a nod, then walk
- After a meal, to the staff — **잘 먹었습니다** (*jal meogeosseumnida*), "I ate well," covered in [what to say in Korean before and after eating](/what-to-say-in-korean-before-and-after-eating)
- When someone speaks slowly on purpose for you — **천천히 말해 주셔서 감사합니다**
- When a stranger goes out of their way — **정말 감사합니다** with a slightly deeper bow

## What usually goes wrong

**Saying 감사 by itself.** Clipping the ending off leaves you with a bare noun. It reads as curt in person even though people type it casually online. Keep the whole word.

**Adding 요 to 감사합니다.** **감사합니다요** is not more polite, it is ungrammatical. **-습니다** is already the formal ending; nothing stacks on top of it.

**Using 감사합니다 with close friends, every time.** It is never rude, but repeated at a friend's dinner table it lands like saying "I am much obliged" to your roommate. Switch to **고마워**.

**Saying 수고하세요 to someone senior.** It means "keep working hard," which is fine downward or sideways to staff, and awkward upward to a boss or an elder — you would be assessing their effort. To someone senior, **감사합니다** is the safe close.

**Reaching for 천만에요.** Textbook-correct, conversationally dead. **아니에요** is the living reply.

**Skipping the nod.** A perfectly pronounced **감사합니다** delivered with a motionless head feels strangely cold. The small bow is not decoration — it is half the phrase.

**Dropping the 시 in 주셔서.** **도와줘서 감사합니다** mixes casual and formal in one breath. Either **도와주셔서 감사합니다** or **도와줘서 고마워** — pick a lane and keep it.
