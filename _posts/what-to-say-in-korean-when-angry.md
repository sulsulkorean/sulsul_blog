If something has gone wrong and you need the Korean for it right now, the sentence you want is almost never the angriest one. **기분 나빠요** (*gibun nappayo*) — "this has upset me" — said calmly, in polite endings, does more work in Korea than any raised voice. Below that sits **짜증나요** (*jjajeungnayo*), which is irritation rather than anger. Above it sits **화났어요** (*hwanasseoyo*), "I am angry," which is a statement about you, not an attack on them. And the practical fix-this sentences — **이거 잘못 나온 것 같은데요** (*igeo jalmot nawon geot gatteundeyo*), "I think this came out wrong" — stay polite on purpose, because in Korea the person who keeps their endings intact is the person who wins the exchange.

> - Irritated: **짜증나요** (*jjajeungnayo*) — "this is annoying."
> - Upset by what someone did: **기분 나빠요** (*gibun nappayo*).
> - Genuinely angry: **화났어요** (*hwanasseoyo*).
> - "That's absurd": **어이가 없네요** (*eoiga eomneyo*).
> - Make it stop: **하지 마세요** (*haji maseyo*), "please don't."
> - Enough: **그만하세요** (*geumanhaseyo*).
> - Never to a stranger: **야** (*ya*), **미쳤어요?** (*michyeosseoyo?*).

## 짜증나 and 화나다 are not the same feeling

English leans on one word — *angry* — and stretches it over everything from a slow lift to a betrayal. Korean splits that range, and picking the wrong half is how learners end up sounding either dramatic or oddly cold.

**짜증나다** (*jjajeungnada*) is irritation. Friction. The subway card that will not tap, the third delivery that is late, the rain on the one free afternoon. It is aimed at a situation far more often than at a person, and that is why Koreans say it constantly without it being a big deal.

- **짜증나** (*jjajeungna*) — casual, to yourself or a close friend.
- **짜증나요** (*jjajeungnayo*) — polite, safe to say in front of almost anyone.
- **진짜 짜증나** (*jinjja jjajeungna*) — "this is so annoying."

**화나다** (*hwanada*) is anger proper. It is heavier, it points at something specific, and announcing it changes the temperature of the room.

- **화났어요** (*hwanasseoyo*) — "I'm angry."
- **화 안 났어요** (*hwa an nasseoyo*) — "I'm not angry," the thing you say when your face says otherwise.
- **화내지 마세요** (*hwanaeji maseyo*) — "please don't get angry," said to someone else.

Notice the grammar: 화나다 is "anger arises," not "I am angry at you." The feeling shows up; it does not get thrown. Keeping that framing is most of what makes a Korean complaint sound reasonable rather than hostile.

Then there is **열받다** (*yeolbatda*), literally "to receive heat" — the one that matches English "pissed off." **열받아** (*yeolbada*) is fine among friends, **열받았어요** keeps it polite, and neither belongs in a shop. The crude step past it is **빡치다** (*ppakchida*); you will hear it in clips and comment sections, and like the rest of the rough vocabulary in [Korean slang words and what they actually mean](/korean-slang-words-what-they-mean), it is worth recognising and leaving unsaid.

## The sentences that actually fix something

Here is the part that matters when you are standing at a counter. Almost every fixable problem in Korea is solved with a flat, polite observation — not an accusation. The grammar trick is the ending **-ㄴ/은 것 같은데요** (*-n geot gatteundeyo*), "it seems that…," which lets you state a fact while leaving the other person room to be the one who fixes it.

| Situation | Say this | Reads as |
|---|---|---|
| Wrong dish arrived | 이거 제가 시킨 거 아닌데요 | "This isn't what I ordered." |
| Something is off | 이거 잘못 나온 것 같은데요 | "I think this came out wrong." |
| The bill looks wrong | 계산이 좀 이상한데요 | "The bill seems off." |
| You want it checked | 다시 확인해 주시겠어요? | "Could you check again?" |
| You want it redone | 바꿔 주실 수 있어요? | "Could you swap it?" |
| You want a refund | 환불해 주세요 | "Please refund this." |
| You have hit the limit | 책임자분 계세요? | "Is the manager in?" |

The small word doing the heavy lifting in several of those is **좀** (*jom*). It means "a bit," and it is the single most useful softener in the language — **좀 이상한데요** is "it's a bit strange," which is how a Korean adult says "this is wrong" without declaring war. The same particle turns a demand into a request: **좀 도와주세요** (*jom dowajuseyo*). More of that register is in [how to politely ask for help in Korean](/politely-ask-for-help-in-korean), and the money-specific version lives in [Korean phrases for returns and exchanges in Korea](/korean-phrases-return-exchange-korea).

And when you need someone to stop doing something — a vendor who will not let go of your sleeve, a stranger standing too close, a photo being taken of you:

- **하지 마세요** (*haji maseyo*) — "please don't."
- **만지지 마세요** (*manjiji maseyo*) — "don't touch."
- **그만하세요** (*geumanhaseyo*) — "stop that."
- **괜찮아요, 필요 없어요** (*gwaenchanayo, piryo eopseoyo*) — "I'm fine, I don't need it."
- **저리 가세요** (*jeori gaseyo*) — "go away." Strong. Keep it for when you mean it.

## 어이없다, 됐어요, and the sounds of a Korean losing patience

You will hear anger long before you have to produce it, and Korean irritation has a very recognisable soundtrack.

**어이가 없네** (*eoiga eomne*) is the famous one — "this is absurd," "I have no words." It is less a complaint than a verdict, delivered with a small laugh of disbelief. The polite form **어이가 없네요** exists and is cutting precisely because it stays polite.

**됐어요** (*dwaesseoyo*) literally means "it's become," as in "that will do." In a shop it is a harmless "no thanks." Said flatly in an argument it means "forget it, we're done," and **됐어** without the 요 is a door closing. Tone carries the whole meaning, which is also true of 진짜요 and the other reaction words sorted out in [how to ask really in Korean](/how-to-ask-really-in-korean).

A few more you should be able to catch:

- **아 진짜** (*a jinjja*) — "oh, come on." The most common sigh in Korea.
- **참나** (*chamna*) — a scoff, roughly "unbelievable."
- **말이 안 되잖아요** (*mari an doejanayo*) — "that doesn't make sense."
- **진정하세요** (*jinjeonghaseyo*) — "please calm down."
- **왜 그러세요?** (*wae geureoseyo?*) — "what's the matter with you?" Sharp, depending on delivery.

There is one escalation signal that has no English equivalent, and it catches learners completely off guard: **dropping the 요**. A shopkeeper who has been saying 있어요 all afternoon and suddenly says 없어 has not got casual with you. Going the other direction works too — a Korean who switches from warm, chatty speech into crisp, flawless 존댓말 is not being nicer, they are putting a wall up. ## At the table when the order is wrong

The food is not what you ordered and the restaurant is busy. Here is the whole exchange, start to finish.

You: **저기요~** — *jeogiyo* — "Excuse me."
Server: **네, 손님.** — *ne, sonnim* — "Yes, sir/ma'am."
You: **이거 제가 시킨 거 아닌 것 같은데요.** — *igeo jega sikin geo anin geot gatteundeyo* — "I don't think this is what I ordered."
Server: **아, 잠시만요. 확인해 볼게요.** — *a, jamsimanyo. hwaginhae bolgeyo* — "Oh, one moment. Let me check."
You: **김치찌개 시켰어요.** — *gimchijjigae sikyeosseoyo* — "I ordered kimchi stew."
Server: **죄송합니다, 바로 바꿔 드릴게요.** — *joesonghamnida, baro bakkwo deurilgeyo* — "Sorry, I'll swap it right away."
You: **네, 감사합니다.** — *ne, gamsahamnida* — "Thank you."

Nothing in that conversation sounds angry, and the problem is fixed in twenty seconds. That is the Korean service script working as designed. If it does not work — if you are told the order is correct when it plainly is not — the next line is **메뉴판 좀 다시 볼 수 있을까요?** (*menyupan jom dasi bol su isseulkkayo?*), "could I see the menu again?", which settles it with evidence instead of volume. ## When you want to say it was not okay

Sometimes nothing needs fixing — you just need the other person to know they crossed a line. This is where 짜증나요 is too small and 화났어요 is too big, and where most learners have no sentence at all.

- **기분 나빠요** (*gibun nappayo*) — "that upset me." Direct, adult, not aggressive.
- **기분 나빴어요** (*gibun nappasseoyo*) — past tense, for something that already happened.
- **좀 불편했어요** (*jom bulpyeonhaesseoyo*) — "I was uncomfortable."
- **그건 좀 아닌 것 같아요** (*geugeon jom anin geot gatayo*) — "I don't think that was right." The most Korean sentence on this page: a complete rejection wrapped in "I think" and "a bit."
- **다음부터는 미리 말씀해 주세요** (*daeumbuteoneun miri malsseumhae juseyo*) — "next time, please tell me in advance."

Said in 존댓말, at normal volume, these land hard. Koreans read the content, not the decibels, and a calm 기분 나빴어요 from someone who has been pleasant all day registers as serious in a way shouting never would. ## Mistakes learners make when they get angry in Korean

**Starting with 야.** 야 (*ya*) means "hey," and it is for close friends your own age and younger siblings. Aimed at a stranger, a server or anyone older, it is close to a slap. The word you want to get attention is **저기요** (*jeogiyo*) — every single time.

**Asking 미쳤어요?** "Are you crazy?" is a line K-drama characters shout at people they have known for sixteen episodes. From a stranger it is an invitation to a fight, and the 요 on the end does not soften it.

**Dropping the 요 to sound firm.** It does the opposite of firm. Polite endings are the armour; take them off and you have just told the room you lost your composure, and the complaint goes with it.

**Getting louder.** Volume reads as loss of control in Korean public space, and the moment you raise your voice the conversation becomes about your behaviour rather than the problem.

**Saying 짜증나 about a person to their face.** It is fine about traffic and fine about your own day. Pointed at someone standing in front of you, it is contempt.

**Translating "I'm angry at you" literally.** 당신한테 화났어요 sounds stiff and faintly threatening — 당신 is not a friendly "you." Name the thing instead: **이게 좀 기분 나빠요**, "this part upset me."

**Using the rough words you learned from subtitles.** 빡쳐, 미친, and the harder prefixes carry their full weight when a non-native speaker uses them, because you lose the benefit of the doubt about tone. Understanding them is useful. Saying them is not — the same rule that applies to the drama vocabulary in [common K-drama phrases to use in conversation](/common-k-drama-phrases-conversation).
