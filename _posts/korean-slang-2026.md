The Korean slang you will hear most in 2026 is not brand new — it is the handful of words from the last few years that survived. **킹받네** (*king-banne*, "that's so annoying"), **갓생** (*gatsaeng*, a disciplined, productive life), **오운완** (*o-un-wan*, "workout done today"), **중꺾마** (*jung-kkeok-ma*, "what matters is not giving up"), **럭키비키** (*reokki-biki*, "Lucky Vicky," turning bad luck into good), and the MBTI jab **너 T야?** (*neo ti-ya?*, "are you a T?") are the ones still alive in group chats, variety show captions, and comment sections. Most of them are chopped-down sentences, so once you see the full sentence behind each one, they stop looking like code.

> - **킹받네** (*king-banne*) — "ugh, so annoying," half-joking.
> - **갓생** (*gatsaeng*) — a "god-tier life": up early, gym, study, no wasted day.
> - **오운완** (*o-un-wan*) — 오늘 운동 완료, "today's workout: done."
> - **스불재** (*seu-bul-jae*) — a disaster you caused yourself.
> - **너 T야?** (*neo ti-ya?*) — "are you a T?" when someone answers feelings with logic.
> - **폼 미쳤다** (*pom michyeotda*) — someone is in crazy-good form.
> - **중꺾마** (*jung-kkeok-ma*) — "what matters is a heart that doesn't break."
> - **럭키비키** (*reokki-biki*) — "Lucky Vicky," seeing the upside of anything.
> - **국룰** (*gungnul*) — "national rule," the thing everyone just does.
> - **알잘딱깔센** (*al-jal-ttak-kkal-sen*) — do it right, neatly, with good sense, without being told.

## Words about your own life: 갓생, 오운완, 스불재

A big chunk of current slang is people narrating their own day.

**갓생** (*gatsaeng*) is English "god" + 생 (*saeng*, life). It means the productive, self-improving life: wake up at six, read, work out, cook instead of ordering delivery. **갓생 살고 싶다** (*gatsaeng salgo sipda*) — "I want to live a 갓생" — is usually said at 1 a.m. by someone who is not doing that.

**오운완** (*o-un-wan*) is **오**늘 **운**동 **완**료, "today's workout complete." It started as a hashtag under gym selfies and now gets said out loud: **오운완!** as you walk in the door sweaty.

**스불재** (*seu-bul-jae*) is **스**스로 **불**러온 **재**앙, "a disaster I summoned myself." You stayed up watching four episodes and have a 9 a.m. meeting? 스불재. It is a way of complaining while admitting it is your own fault, which makes it surprisingly polite.

## Words for reacting to people: 킹받네, 너 T야?, 폼 미쳤다

**킹받네** (*king-banne*) is 열받네 (*yeol-banne*, "I'm getting heated") with the English "king" swapped in as an intensifier. It is lighter than real anger — the word for a friend's smug face, a cliffhanger, a typo you sent to your boss. Pronounce it *king-ban-ne*; the ㄷ in 받 turns into an n sound before ㄴ.

**너 T야?** (*neo ti-ya?*) comes from MBTI, which Koreans use as everyday small talk. T is "thinking," F is "feeling." If you tell a friend "I'm sad, I failed my test" and they answer "how many points did you miss by?", the reply is **너 T야?** — "wow, are you a T?" It is teasing, not an insult, but it is 반말, so keep it among friends.

**폼 미쳤다** (*pom michyeotda*) is "the form is insane," borrowed from sports commentary. Use it for anyone performing at their peak: a singer hitting a live note, a friend's outfit, your mom's kimchi this year. **오늘 폼 미쳤다** — "you're on fire today."

## Attitude words: 중꺾마, 럭키비키, 오히려 좋아

Some slang is less a word than a mindset with a name.

**중꺾마** (*jung-kkeok-ma*) is short for **중**요한 건 **꺾**이지 않는 **마**음, "what matters is a heart that doesn't break." It spread through esports and the 2022 World Cup and turned into the Korean shorthand for grit. You will see it on banners, in fan comments under an idol's comeback, and in exam-season messages.

**럭키비키** (*reokki-biki*, "Lucky Vicky") comes from IVE's Jang Wonyoung, whose relentlessly positive way of reframing things fans nicknamed **원영적 사고** (*wonyeongjeok sago*, "Wonyoung-style thinking"). The bread you wanted sold out, so now you get to try a new one? 완전 럭키비키잖아. It is said with a grin and works best when the situation is mildly bad.

**오히려 좋아** (*ohiryeo joa*) — "if anything, that's better" — does the same job without the celebrity reference. Rain cancelled the picnic? 오히려 좋아, we stay in and watch a drama.

## Sentences squeezed into four syllables: 국룰, 알잘딱깔센, 점메추

Korean loves taking a whole sentence and keeping only the first syllable of each word. Fans even made a word about it: **별다줄** (*byeol-da-jul*), **별**걸 **다 줄**인다, "you abbreviate everything."

**국룰** (*gungnul*) is 국민 (*gungmin*, the nation) + rule. It means the unwritten standard everyone follows. **치킨엔 맥주가 국룰이지** — "chicken with beer is the national rule."

**알잘딱깔센** (*al-jal-ttak-kkal-sen*) is **알**아서 **잘 딱 깔**끔하고 **센**스 있게: "figure it out yourself, do it right, clean and with good sense." It is what you hope a new teammate is.

**점메추** (*jeom-me-chu*) is **점**심 **메**뉴 **추**천, "lunch menu recommendation, please" — posted at 11:50 every weekday. Swap 점 for 저 (저녁, dinner) and you get **저메추**.

**찐** (*jjin*), from 진짜, means "the real one." **찐친** (*jjinchin*) is a real close friend, and **찐텐** (*jjinten*) is genuine excitement, the opposite of **억텐** (*eokten*), forced, put-on energy.

## Slang that already sounds dated in 2026

Slang ages fast, and using last cycle's words is the quickest way to sound like a textbook.

**어쩔티비** (*eojjeol-tibi*, "so what, go watch TV") was everywhere among schoolkids a few years ago. Now adults use it mostly ironically. **갑분싸** (*gap-bun-ssa*, "the mood suddenly went cold") is still understood but reads a little older. **핵인싸** (*haek-inssa*, "super insider") has softened back into plain **인싸** (*inssa*, the social one) and **아싸** (*assa*, the loner).

The words in the earlier sections are the safer bet. And the reaction words — **대박**, **헐**, **진짜?** — never go out of style. [Korean slang words and what they actually mean](/korean-slang-words-what-they-mean) covers those evergreen ones and the chopping machine behind them.

## A group chat in 2026 slang

Three friends planning a Saturday:

A: **나 오늘 오운완! 갓생 시작이다.** — *na oneul o-un-wan! gatsaeng sijagida.* — "Workout done today. 갓생 starts now."  
B: **나는 어제 새벽 3시까지 드라마 봤어. 스불재...** — *naneun eoje saebyeok sesikkaji deurama bwasseo. seu-bul-jae...* — "I watched dramas till 3 a.m. Self-inflicted disaster."  
C: **토요일 점심 뭐 먹어? 점메추 좀.** — *toyoil jeomsim mwo meogeo? jeom-me-chu jom.* — "What are we eating Saturday? Lunch ideas, please."  
A: **떡볶이가 국룰이지.** — *tteokbokkiga gungnuriji.* — "Tteokbokki is the national rule."  
B: **근데 그 집 토요일에 문 닫아.** — *geunde geu jip toyoire mun dada.* — "But that place is closed on Saturday."  
C: **너 T야? 그럼 다른 데 가면 되지. 오히려 좋아.** — *neo ti-ya? geureom dareun de gamyeon doeji. ohiryeo joa.* — "Are you a T? Then we go somewhere else. Even better."

Six lines, and a direct translation would lose almost all of it.

## Who you can say these to

**Slang is 반말 territory.** Nearly all of these live in casual speech with friends your age or younger. Saying **킹받네** to your teacher or a shop owner lands badly. With strangers, the safe versions are plain: **진짜 좋네요** instead of 폼 미쳤다, **제가 실수했어요** instead of 스불재.

**Know the age gap.** Many Koreans over forty know these words from TV but do not use them. 갓생 and 오운완 are widely understood; 알잘딱깔센 may get a blank look from your friend's dad.

**Do not over-stack.** A sentence with three slang words in it sounds like a meme account. One per message is how people actually text — see [what ㅋㅋ means in Korean texting](/what-does-kk-mean-in-korean-texting) for the rest of the chat shorthand.

**Watch the vulgar cousins.** Some popular slang hides a swear word in its abbreviation — **존버**, for example, starts with 존나. Learn to recognize it, but do not say it until you know who you are with.
