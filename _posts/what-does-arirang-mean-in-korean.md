**아리랑** (*arirang*) has no translation, and that is the honest answer. It is the refrain of Korea's best-known folk song, and the word itself is not a noun with a dictionary entry the way 사랑 is "love" or 고개 is "hill." Korean linguists have argued over its origin for more than a century without settling it. In practice, 아리랑 functions the way "la la la" or "hey nonny no" functions in English songs — a sung syllable chain — except that Koreans hear sorrow, longing, and endurance in it, which is why it ended up on the UNESCO list of intangible cultural heritage in 2012. If someone asks you what it means, the correct answer is not a word. It is "it's the sound of missing someone."

> - **아리랑** (*arirang*) — the refrain of Korea's most famous folk song; untranslatable by design.
> - **아라리요** (*arariyo*) — the second half of the refrain. Also a vocal sound, not a sentence.
> - **고개** (*gogae*) — a hill, or a mountain pass. 아리랑 고개 is the pass in the song.
> - **님** (*nim*) — a beloved person, in old poetic Korean.
> - **정** (*jeong*) — the deep attachment between people that Korean songs are built on.
> - **민요** (*minyo*) — folk song.
> - **한** (*han*) — accumulated sorrow carried without resolution.

## The four lines, and what they actually say

The song is short. Most Koreans know the standard Seoul version — **본조아리랑** (*bonjo arirang*) — by heart, and it is four lines long.

**아리랑, 아리랑, 아라리요** — *arirang, arirang, arariyo* — the refrain. Untranslated in every serious edition.

**아리랑 고개로 넘어간다** — *arirang gogaero neomeoganda* — "Over the Arirang pass I go." **넘어가다** (*neomeogada*) is to cross over something.

**나를 버리고 가시는 님은** — *nareul beorigo gasineun nimeun* — "The beloved who abandons me and leaves."

**십리도 못 가서 발병난다** — *simnido mot gaseo balbyeongnanda* — "Won't make ten li before their feet give out."

That last line is the one that surprises people. It is not a blessing for safe travels. **발병나다** (*balbyeongnada*) means to come down with sore, blistered feet, and **십리** (*simni*) is about four kilometers. The singer is telling the person walking away that they will not get far before their feet fail them — half curse, half wish, entirely the voice of someone who does not want to be left. Translate it literally and it sounds petty. Hear it sung and it sounds like grief.

That gap between the literal words and the felt meaning is the whole reason 아리랑 survives. The grammar is plain enough for a first-year learner to parse. The emotion underneath it is the thing Koreans mean when they say **한** (*han*) — sorrow that stays.

## Why nobody can agree on the origin

There are three theories you will meet, and no consensus among them.

**아리 + 랑, "beautiful beloved."** In old Korean, **아리** carried a sense of *lovely* or *bright*, and **랑** (郎) was a suffix for a young man — the same 랑 inside **화랑** (*hwarang*), the elite youth corps of the Silla kingdom. Read this way, 아리랑 is an address: *my beautiful love*. This is the version Korean teachers like, because it fits the song's subject.

**아리다 + 랑, "the ache."** **아리다** (*arida*) means to sting or smart — the word for a sore tooth or a raw heart. Readers of this theory hear 아리랑 as the sound of pain, which fits the fourth line far better than "beautiful beloved" does.

**아리랑 고개 as a real place.** Several regions claim a physical pass by that name, and the song may simply be named after it. The trouble is that each region's pass points to a different legend, and the legends contradict each other.

The reason none of this resolves: 아리랑 is older than the records of it. It travelled by voice, farm to farm, for generations before anyone wrote it down, and each valley rewrote the verses. So the word reached us intact while its meaning did not. Korean dictionaries now list it as the song's title and the refrain — and stop there, which is the only defensible position.

## One song, many versions

If a Korean friend says 아리랑 and you picture one melody, you are picturing one of dozens. The three regional versions you are most likely to encounter:

| Version | Romanization | Where it's from |
|---|---|---|
| 정선아리랑 | *Jeongseon Arirang* | Gangwon mountains; slow, heavy, the oldest-sounding |
| 진도아리랑 | *Jindo Arirang* | Jindo island in the southwest; faster, playful, call-and-response |
| 밀양아리랑 | *Miryang Arirang* | Miryang in the southeast; bright and brisk, the one that feels like a march |

Each has its own verses, and singers improvise new ones. That is normal for **민요** (*minyo*) — folk song — where the refrain is fixed and the verses belong to whoever is singing.

## Where K-content fans actually run into it

**Arirang TV.** The English-language Korean broadcaster that carries K-pop and culture programming. Named after the song, and the first place many fans see the word in Latin letters — which is why plenty of people assume 아리랑 means "Korea." It does not.

**Ceremonies and stadiums.** Olympic teams, national events, and award shows pull 아리랑 out for the moment that needs to feel like all of Korea at once. When North and South Korean athletes have marched together, 아리랑 is what played — it belongs to neither state and both.

**Idol and crossover arrangements.** Traditional-fusion stages, orchestral covers, gugak-pop collaborations. If an idol group does a heritage stage, 아리랑 is the safest and most common choice.

**Sageuk soundtracks.** Historical dramas use the melody as shorthand for homeland and loss. You may have heard it under a scene without the word ever appearing in the subtitles.

Here is how it comes up in conversation:

Them: **아리랑 들어봤어요?** — *arirang deureobwasseoyo?* — "Have you heard Arirang?"  
You: **네, 들어봤어요. 무슨 뜻이에요?** — *ne, deureobwasseoyo. museun tteusieyo?* — "Yes, I have. What does it mean?"  
Them: **음… 뜻이 없어요. 그냥 아리랑이에요.** — *eum… tteusi eopseoyo. geunyang arirangieyo.* — "Um… it has no meaning. It's just Arirang."

That exchange is not a failure of their English. It is the real answer, and if you can reply **아, 느낌이 중요한 거네요** (*a, neukkimi jungyohan geonneyo*) — "ah, so the feeling is what matters" — you have understood the song better than a translation would have let you.

## Five things to get right

**It is not the national anthem.** That is **애국가** (*aegukga*), a separate 20th-century composition. Calling 아리랑 the anthem is the single most common mistake, and Koreans will correct it.

**The 랑 in 아리랑 is not the particle 랑.** Learners who know **-랑** as the casual "with" — **친구랑 갔어요** (*chingurang gasseoyo*), "I went with a friend" — try to split the word that way. 아리랑 is one unit. Do not parse it.

**Even beats, no stress.** *A-ri-rang*, three light syllables of equal weight. English speakers push the first or last one and it stops sounding like the word.

**Don't ask a Korean person to translate it.** Ask what it *feels* like instead. You will get a real answer, and a better conversation.

**It is not a pop song you perform casually.** It sits closer to a hymn — sung at ceremonies, memorials, and national moments.
