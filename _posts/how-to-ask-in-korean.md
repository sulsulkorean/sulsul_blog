To ask in Korean whether you can do something, take the verb and add **아도 돼요?** or **어도 돼요?** — literally "even if I do it, is it okay?" So **여기 앉아도 돼요?** (*yeogi anjado dwaeyo?*) is "Can I sit here?" and **사진 찍어도 돼요?** (*sajin jjigeodo dwaeyo?*) is "Can I take a picture?" One ending covers almost every "Can I…?" and "May I…?" you will need on a trip. The yes usually comes back as **네, 괜찮아요** (*ne, gwaenchanayo*), "Yes, it's fine," and the no as **안 돼요** (*an dwaeyo*), "You can't."

> - The ending: verb + **아도/어도 돼요?** (*-ado/-eodo dwaeyo?*), "Can I…?"
> - The short form: noun + **돼요?** (*dwaeyo?*), "Is ___ possible?" — **카드 돼요?** (*kadeu dwaeyo?*), "Can I pay by card?"
> - The yes you will hear: **네, 괜찮아요** (*ne, gwaenchanayo*) or **네, 돼요** (*ne, dwaeyo*).
> - The no: **안 돼요** (*an dwaeyo*), and the rule version, verb + **(으)면 안 돼요**, "You shouldn't…"

## Why "Can I" in Korean is not a question word

English builds "Can I…?" from a helper verb. Korean has no word you can drop in front of a sentence to make it a request for permission. Instead, the permission lives at the end of the verb. **돼요** (*dwaeyo*) means "it works" or "it's allowed," and **-아도/-어도** means "even if." Put them together and you are asking: "If I do this, does it still work?"

That is why the same word, **돼요**, shows up everywhere. A cashier says **돼요** when your card goes through. A shop clerk says **안 돼요** when the fitting room is closed. Once you hear it as "allowed / not allowed," most of the Korean around rules and requests stops being a mystery.

## Building 아도 돼요 and 어도 돼요 from any verb

The choice between 아도 and 어도 follows the same vowel rule as the past tense. Look at the last vowel of the verb stem:

- **ㅏ or ㅗ → 아도.** 가다 → **가도 돼요?** (*gado dwaeyo?*), "Can I go?" 앉다 → **앉아도 돼요?** (*anjado dwaeyo?*), "Can I sit?" 보다 → **봐도 돼요?** (*bwado dwaeyo?*), "Can I look?"
- **Any other vowel → 어도.** 먹다 → **먹어도 돼요?** (*meogeodo dwaeyo?*), "Can I eat this?" 찍다 → **찍어도 돼요?** (*jjigeodo dwaeyo?*), "Can I take (a photo)?" 열다 → **열어도 돼요?** (*yeoreodo dwaeyo?*), "Can I open it?"
- **하다 → 해도.** **해도 돼요?** (*haedo dwaeyo?*), "Can I do it?" 계산하다 → **계산해도 돼요?** (*gyesanhaedo dwaeyo?*), "Can I pay now?"
- **ㅡ drops out.** 쓰다 → **써도 돼요?** (*sseodo dwaeyo?*), "Can I use it?"

If you already know **먹었어요** (*meogeosseoyo*), "I ate," you already know **먹어도**. Swap **-었어요** for **-어도 돼요?** and you are asking permission instead of reporting the past.

## The eight "Can I…?" lines that cover a trip

These are the moments where travelers actually need permission, in the order they tend to come up:

1. **여기 앉아도 돼요?** (*yeogi anjado dwaeyo?*) — "Can I sit here?" For a cafe seat with a bag on it, or a shared table at a food court.
2. **사진 찍어도 돼요?** (*sajin jjigeodo dwaeyo?*) — "Can I take a picture?" Ask before photographing a market stall, a dish in a small restaurant, or a person.
3. **입어 봐도 돼요?** (*ibeo bwado dwaeyo?*) — "Can I try it on?" 봐도 after a verb means "try doing it."
4. **화장실 써도 돼요?** (*hwajangsil sseodo dwaeyo?*) — "Can I use the restroom?" Useful in a cafe where you have not ordered yet.
5. **이거 만져 봐도 돼요?** (*igeo manjyeo bwado dwaeyo?*) — "Can I touch this?" For fabrics, ceramics, anything on display.
6. **들어가도 돼요?** (*deureogado dwaeyo?*) — "Can I go in?" At a temple hall, a gallery room, or a shop that looks closed.
7. **창문 열어도 돼요?** (*changmun yeoreodo dwaeyo?*) — "Can I open the window?" In a taxi or a shared room.
8. **카드 돼요?** (*kadeu dwaeyo?*) — "Can I pay by card?" With a noun, you can skip the verb and attach **돼요?** directly. **포장 돼요?** (*pojang dwaeyo?*), "Can I get it to go?" works the same way.

To soften any of them, start with **혹시** (*hoksi*), "by any chance": **혹시 사진 찍어도 돼요?** sounds careful and polite without adding any grammar.

## Hearing the answer: 괜찮아요, 돼요, and 안 돼요

Many learners memorize the question and freeze at the reply. The textbook yes is **네, 해도 돼요** (*ne, haedo dwaeyo*), but Koreans rarely repeat the verb. What you will mostly hear:

- **네, 괜찮아요** (*ne, gwaenchanayo*) — "Yes, it's fine." The most common yes.
- **네, 돼요** (*ne, dwaeyo*) — "Yes, you can."
- **그럼요** (*geureomyo*) — "Of course."
- **안 돼요** (*an dwaeyo*) — "You can't." Short and not rude; it is simply the rule.
- **죄송한데 안 돼요** (*joesonghande an dwaeyo*) — "Sorry, but you can't." What staff say to a customer.

Often the answer is a hand gesture: an open palm toward the seat means yes; crossed forearms in an X means no. Reply with **감사합니다** (*gamsahamnida*) either way.

## A cafe exchange, start to finish

> **You:** 저기요, 여기 앉아도 돼요? (*Jeogiyo, yeogi anjado dwaeyo?*) — "Excuse me, can I sit here?"
> **Stranger:** 네, 괜찮아요. (*Ne, gwaenchanayo.*) — "Yes, it's fine."
> **You:** 감사합니다. 혹시 콘센트 써도 돼요? (*Gamsahamnida. Hoksi konsenteu sseodo dwaeyo?*) — "Thank you. By any chance, can I use the outlet?"
> **Staff:** 죄송한데 거기는 안 돼요. 저쪽은 돼요. (*Joesonghande geogineun an dwaeyo. Jeojjogeun dwaeyo.*) — "Sorry, not that one. That side is fine."

**콘센트** (*konsenteu*) is the Korean word for a wall socket — say "outlet" and you will get a blank look.

## The flip side: 면 안 돼요 on signs and from staff

The same 돼요 runs rules in the other direction. Verb + **(으)면 안 돼요** means "If you do this, it's not okay" — "You shouldn't…" Use **으면** after a final consonant and **면** after a vowel:

- **여기서 사진 찍으면 안 돼요.** (*Yeogiseo sajin jjigeumyeon an dwaeyo.*) — "You can't take photos here."
- **만지면 안 돼요.** (*Manjimyeon an dwaeyo.*) — "Please don't touch."
- **여기서 먹으면 안 돼요.** (*Yeogiseo meogeumyeon an dwaeyo.*) — "No eating here."

When a museum guard says one of these, a quick **아, 죄송합니다** (*a, joesonghamnida*), "Oh, sorry," closes it.

## Mistakes that make the question sound off

- **Writing 되요.** The spoken sound is the same, but the correct spelling is **돼요** (되 + 어요 = 돼요). Koreans notice it in texts.
- **Picking the wrong vowel.** *먹아도* and *가어도* sound broken. Check the last vowel of the stem first.
- **Using 할 수 있어요? for permission.** **할 수 있어요?** (*hal su isseoyo?*) asks whether someone is able to do something, not whether it is allowed. "Can I sit here?" as 앉을 수 있어요? sounds like you are asking if your knees work.
- **Skipping the 저기요.** A bare question at a stranger's back is abrupt. **저기요** (*jeogiyo*), "excuse me," gets their attention first.
- **Going too formal.** **해도 될까요?** (*haedo doelkkayo?*), "Would it be all right if…?" is lovely in an office, but 돼요? is the everyday register for shops and cafes.
