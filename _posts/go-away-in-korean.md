To say "go away" in Korean, use **저리 가** (*jeo-ri ga*) with friends or **저리 가세요** (*jeo-ri ga-se-yo*) with anyone else. Literally it means "go over there." K-dramas also have much harsher options. **꺼져** (*kkeo-jyeo*) means "get lost," **나가** (*na-ga*) means "get out," and **내버려 둬** (*nae-beo-ryeo dwo*) means "leave me alone." Subtitles can turn several of these into the same two English words, even though a real Korean listener would hear a big difference between them.

> - **저리 좀 가.** — Go away, would you? (playful or annoyed, friends)
> - **저리 가세요.** — Go away. (polite grammar, firm message)
> - **비켜.** — Move. / Out of my way. (casual, rude to strangers)
> - **내버려 둬.** — Leave me alone. (casual, hurt or tired)
> - **나가.** — Get out. (casual, angry)
> - **가 버려.** — Just go. / Go away. (casual, emotional)
> - **꺼져.** — Get lost. (a fight word)

## 저리 가: the plain "go away"

**저리** means "over that way," and **가** is the casual command form of 가다, "to go." So the phrase points the person away from you. That makes it the most neutral option on the list, and your voice decides most of what it means.

With a friend who keeps poking you, **아, 저리 좀 가~** is pure teasing. The little word **좀** softens it, and the stretched ending tells everyone you're not really upset. From someone with a flat voice and no eye contact, plain **저리 가** is cold.

The polite form, **저리 가세요**, is a strange creature. The grammar is respectful, but the message still isn't, so it sounds like a firm warning. Use it only when you really want a person gone.

## 꺼져: the one that starts fights

**꺼져** comes from **꺼지다**, the verb for a light or a flame going out. Saying it to a person means "vanish." The usual English match is "get lost," though in a heated scene it can feel closer to something the subtitles aren't allowed to say.

Two longer versions turn up in the biggest confrontations:

- **꺼져 버려** (*kkeo-jyeo beo-ryeo*) adds 버리다, which gives a sense of "and be gone for good."
- **내 눈앞에서 꺼져** (*nae nun-a-pe-seo kkeo-jyeo*) means "get out of my sight." The softer word 사라져 (*sa-ra-jyeo*, "disappear") often replaces 꺼져, as in **내 눈앞에서 사라져**. Picture a chaebol parent saying that one across a very long dining table.

You can technically build a polite form, 꺼지세요, but it only sounds more sarcastic. For where this word sits among the others, see [Korean bad words in K-dramas](/korean-slang-bad-words). It often appears right next to [닥쳐, "shut up"](/what-does-dakchyeo-mean-in-korean).

## 나가 and 비켜: when the room matters

Two of these words depend on where everyone is standing.

**나가** (*na-ga*) means "get out," so someone has to be inside something first, like a room, an office, or a car. A parent in a drama points at the door and shouts **나가!** The polite request is **나가 주세요** ("please leave"), which a staff member might say to someone causing trouble.

**비켜** (*bi-kyeo*) means "move" or "out of my way." The person is physically blocking you, and you want a path. A character storming through a hospital corridor says **비켜!** without slowing down.

The learner trap is that 비켜 looks like the word for squeezing past someone on a crowded subway. It isn't. Say **잠시만요** (*jam-si-man-yo*, "excuse me a moment") or **좀 지나갈게요** ("I'm just going to get past"). Even **좀 비켜 주세요** can sound a little sharp, so keep those two softer lines ready.

## "Leave me alone" is a different feeling

Sometimes a character doesn't care where the other person goes, as long as the pressure stops. Korean has separate lines for that.

**내버려 둬** (*nae-beo-ryeo dwo*) is "leave me alone" or "let it be." You'll usually hear **나 좀 내버려 둬**, with 좀 sounding tired instead of soft. It's less an attack than a plea.

**혼자 있고 싶어** (*hon-ja it-go si-peo*) means "I want to be alone." It's the gentlest line in this post, and it works outside dramas too. With someone you need to be polite to, say **혼자 있고 싶어요**.

**귀찮게 하지 마** (*gwi-chan-ke ha-ji ma*) means "stop bothering me," and **따라오지 마** (*tta-ra-o-ji ma*) means "stop following me." The second one tends to come right after a character has walked out of a restaurant in tears, with the other lead three steps behind.

## One breakup scene, five ways to say it

A park bench above the city, late at night. Seo-yeon wants Min-ho gone, or at least she says so.

> **서연:** 왜 왔어? 가.
> *Why did you come? Go.*
>
> **민호:** 얘기 좀 하자. 5분만.
> *Let's talk. Just five minutes.*
>
> **서연:** 할 말 없어. 저리 가.
> *I have nothing to say. Go away.*
>
> **민호:** 서연아, 내 말 좀 들어 봐.
> *Seo-yeon, just hear me out.*
>
> **서연:** 나 좀 내버려 둬. 혼자 있고 싶어.
> *Leave me alone. I want to be alone.*
>
> **민호:** 알았어. 갈게. 근데 밥은 꼭 챙겨 먹어.
> *Okay. I'm going. But make sure you eat.*
>
> **서연:** 가라고 했잖아. 빨리 가.
> *I told you to go. Just go.*

Look at that last line. **가라고** is 가라 ("go") plus 고, a marker that reports what someone said, so **가라고 했잖아** comes out as "I already told you to go." On its own, **가라고!** means "I said go!" Korean characters repeat themselves with this ending when they're losing an argument, and the shorter 가라니까 ("I'm telling you, go") does the same job.

## Why "go away" so often means "don't go"

Drama writers love this gap. A character says **가** and looks at the floor. The other person takes two steps, and the camera stays on the one who's left behind. Three scenes later, you get the line they meant all along: **가지 마**, "don't go." We covered that moment in [K-drama lines every fan knows by heart](/k-drama-lines).

So a single **가** on its own isn't always what it seems. It can also mean other things entirely, and only tone tells you which:

- **가!** — Go! (a command)
- **가?** — You're leaving? (a question)
- **나 가.** — I'm heading out. (a plain statement)

You also may have heard a blunter form, **가라**. Its most quoted use comes from the 2001 Korean film *친구* (*Friend*). One gangster tells another to lie low in Hawaii, and gets back **니가 가라, 하와이**: "You go to Hawaii." Koreans still joke with that line today.

## What to say when you really need someone gone

Skip the drama lines in real life. They raise the temperature, and a foreign accent won't soften them. These work better:

**A pushy street seller or flyer person:** **아니요, 괜찮아요** ("no, I'm fine"), and keep walking. If they keep going, **안 사요** ("I'm not buying") is clear without being rude.

**Someone who won't take the hint:** **하지 마세요** ("please stop") and then **저리 가세요**. Say them loudly, since being heard by other people helps.

**Someone following you:** **따라오지 마세요** ("stop following me"). Walk into a shop or a convenience store. If you feel unsafe, the police number in Korea is 112, and our guide on [how to say help in Korean](/how-to-say-help-in-korean) has the words for a real emergency.

**Your own friend, in a bad mood:** **오늘은 좀 혼자 있고 싶어** ("I kind of want to be alone today"). Nobody gets hurt by that one.

Two easy mistakes are worth catching early:

**Using 저리 가세요 for directions.** If someone asks where the exit is, 저리 가세요 can feel like you're shooing them off. Say **저쪽으로 가세요** ("go that way") instead.

**Dropping 안녕히 from a goodbye.** **안녕히 가세요** is a warm farewell. A bare **가세요** in a flat voice can sound more like "you can leave now." Our guide on [how to say goodbye in Korean](/how-to-say-goodbye-in-korean) sorts this out.

## When "go away" isn't about a person

English also uses "go away" for pain, and for trips. Korean uses different verbs for those.

- **My headache went away:** 두통이 없어졌어요. 없어지다 means "to disappear" or "to be gone."
- **I'm going away for the weekend:** 주말에 여행 가요, literally "I'm going on a trip this weekend."
- **He went away and never came back:** 떠나다 is the verb, "to leave for somewhere far." 그는 떠났어요 is sad, and you'll find it in plenty of OST lyrics.

## Practice the ones you'd really say

You'll probably never need 꺼져, and that's fine, since you only have to recognize it. Your speaking practice should go to the lines you could actually use. Pick **잠시만요**, **아니요, 괜찮아요**, and **혼자 있고 싶어요**, then say each one out loud five times. Keep your voice calm and steady, because in Korean a lot of the politeness lives in your tone.
