If you want a basic Korean phrases PDF, the fastest one is this page: scroll down to the cheat sheet, then save it as a PDF (on iPhone: Share → Print → pinch the preview → Save to Files; on Android or a laptop: Print → Save as PDF). It works offline, so it still opens in the subway tunnel, on airplane mode, or when your roaming runs out. The sheet holds about thirty lines, and four endings — **주세요**, **있어요?**, **어디예요?**, **돼요?** — do most of the work.

> - **안녕하세요** — *an-nyeong-ha-se-yo* — hello
> - **감사합니다** — *gam-sa-ham-ni-da* — thank you
> - **이거 주세요** — *i-geo ju-se-yo* — this one, please
> - **화장실 어디예요?** — *hwa-jang-sil eo-di-ye-yo?* — where is the restroom?
> - **카드 돼요?** — *ka-deu dwae-yo?* — can I pay by card?

A phrase PDF is only useful if you can find the right line in three seconds while someone is waiting for you. So the sheet below is sorted by the moment you will be in, not by grammar, and every line is short enough to read off a screen.

## The cheat sheet

**Being polite (say these out loud)**

| Korean | Sounds like | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 안녕하세요 | an-nyeong-ha-se-yo | Hello |
| 감사합니다 | gam-sa-ham-ni-da | Thank you |
| 죄송합니다 | joe-song-ham-ni-da | I'm sorry |
| 저기요 | jeo-gi-yo | Excuse me (to get attention) |
| 네 / 아니요 | ne / a-ni-yo | Yes / No |
| 괜찮아요 | gwaen-cha-na-yo | It's okay / No, thanks |

**Asking for things**

| Korean | Sounds like | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 이거 주세요 | i-geo ju-se-yo | This one, please |
| 물 주세요 | mul ju-se-yo | Water, please |
| ___ 있어요? | i-sseo-yo? | Do you have ___? |
| 영수증 주세요 | yeong-su-jeung ju-se-yo | A receipt, please |
| 봉투 주세요 | bong-tu ju-se-yo | A bag, please |

|---|---|---|

Numbers worth adding to the top of your PDF: **119** (fire and ambulance), **112** (police), and **1330** (the Korea travel hotline, which answers in English and several other languages).

## Why four endings cover most of the sheet

Look back at the tables and you will see the same few endings over and over. That is not an accident. They are the first four patterns in the SULSUL textbook's *Survival Engine*, the part built for the first week in Korea:

1. **(Noun) 주세요** — "please give me ___." Pattern 01, the survival cheat code. Swap the noun and you have a new sentence: 물 주세요, 영수증 주세요, 봉투 주세요.
2. **(Noun) 있어요? / 없어요?** — "is there ___? / there isn't ___." Pattern 03. 유심 있어요? 큰 사이즈 있어요? 자리 있어요?
3. **(Noun) 어디예요? / 어디 있어요?** — "where is ___?" Pattern 04, the compass for lost foreigners. 화장실, 지하철역, 출구 — any place word goes in front.
4. **돼요?** — "is it okay / does it work?" 카드 돼요? 사진 찍어도 돼요? 포장 돼요?

![A traveler at a pharmacy counter in Korea, pointing to a phrase on a phone screen](/assets/blog/scenes/pharmacy.jpg)

Once you see the pattern, the PDF stops being a list to memorize and becomes a set of slots. If a word you need is not on the sheet, look it up, drop it into one of the four slots, and you have a correct, polite sentence. **진통제 있어요?** (do you have painkillers?) is not on the sheet, but 있어요? is, and that is enough.

## Show it or say it: how to use the sheet in the moment

Not every line on a phrase PDF should be spoken. Some work better as a screen you turn toward the other person.

**Say out loud:** greetings, thank you, sorry, 저기요, and 이거 주세요. These are short, they are expected, and saying them yourself changes the tone of the whole exchange. A clerk who hears 안녕하세요 from a tourist usually smiles and slows down.

**Show the screen:** addresses, allergy information, and anything in the emergency table. A taxi driver reads a Korean address faster than they can decode a pronunciation, and in an emergency you do not want to be practicing vowels.

**Do both:** for 어디예요? questions, say the line and hold up the screen. The person hears your attempt, sees the word, and almost always points. You only need to catch **저쪽이요** (*jeo-jjo-gi-yo*, "that way") or **쭉 가세요** (*jjuk ga-se-yo*, "go straight").

## A real exchange, using only the sheet

Here is a convenience store at night, run entirely from the tables above.

> **You:** 안녕하세요. 물 있어요?
> **Clerk:** 네, 저쪽에 있어요. *(points to the fridge)*
> **You:** *(brings the water)* 이거 주세요. 카드 돼요?
> **Clerk:** 네. 봉투 필요하세요?
> **You:** 아니요, 괜찮아요. 감사합니다!

The only line you did not have was **봉투 필요하세요?** ("do you need a bag?"). But 봉투 is on your sheet, and 괜찮아요 answers almost any offer politely. That is how a one-page sheet stretches further than its thirty lines.

## Three ways a phrase PDF goes wrong

**Saving a list with no pronunciation.** Hangul alone is hard to read at speed on day one, and romanization alone loses the Korean you need to show. Keep both columns, as above.

**Making it too long.** A forty-page phrasebook PDF is useless at a counter. If you cannot find a line in three seconds, it might as well not be there. One page, sorted by situation, is the right size.

**Only reading it.** A sheet you have never said out loud comes out as a whisper at the real counter. The words are on the page; the confidence comes from having said them before.
