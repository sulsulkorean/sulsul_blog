You can visit Korea without speaking Korean. Airports, subways, big hotels and chain cafes will carry you in English. The Korean for tourists that earns its place is for everywhere else: the local bus, the family restaurant with a handwritten menu, the neighborhood pharmacy, the taxi. For those, plan on about **ten spoken lines and ten written signs**. If you learn only one, make it **영어 메뉴 있어요?** (*yeongeo menyu isseoyo?*), "Do you have an English menu?", because you can swap the first two words for almost anything you need.

> - Do you have an English menu? **영어 메뉴 있어요?** (*yeongeo menyu isseoyo?*)
> - Please take me to this address. **이 주소로 가 주세요.** (*i jusoro ga juseyo*)
> - Does this bus go to the market? **이 버스 시장 가요?** (*i beoseu sijang gayo?*)
> - Is there one that isn't spicy? **안 매운 거 있어요?** (*an maeun geo isseoyo?*)
> - Please say it slowly. **천천히 말해 주세요.** (*cheoncheonhi malhae juseyo*)
> - Please write it here. **여기에 써 주세요.** (*yeogie sseo juseyo*)

## A map of your trip: where English works

Think of Korea in three zones. You'll move between them several times a day.

| Zone | Where you'll be | What to expect |
|---|---|---|
| English works | Airports, subway signs and announcements, KTX trains, large hotels, palaces and big museums | Signs and staff in English. Korean is a courtesy. |
| English is patchy | Chain cafes, department stores, Olive Young, kiosks, Myeongdong and Hongdae | Short English, plus a screen or a menu photo to point at. |
| English stops | City and town buses, taxis, markets, small restaurants, local clinics and pharmacies, anywhere rural | Korean, gestures and a translation app. |

Most Koreans studied English at school for years and read it better than they speak it. A typed or written question often gets a better answer than a spoken one. So does a Korean opening, which tells the other person they don't have to perform in English. **한국어 잘 못해요** (*hangugeo jal mothaeyo*), "I don't speak Korean well," is friendlier than launching straight into English, and people usually slow down when they hear it.

To check first, ask **영어 할 수 있어요?** (*yeongeo hal su isseoyo?*), "Can you speak English?" That's Pattern 12 in the SULSUL workbook, (V)ㄹ/을 수 있어요.

## On the bus, where nobody is at a window

The subway has English on every platform. A town bus has a driver watching traffic and a stop list you may not be able to read. Ask from the doorstep, before you tap:

- **이 버스 시장 가요?** (*i beoseu sijang gayo?*) — "Does this bus go to the market?" Swap **시장** for your stop.
- **어디에서 내려야 돼요?** (*eodieseo naeryeoya dwaeyo?*) — "Where do I have to get off?" (Pattern 41)
- **도착하면 말해 주시겠어요?** (*dochakhamyeon malhae jusigesseoyo?*) — "Could you tell me when we get there?" (Patterns 43 and 23)

Here's how it sounds at the front of a bus in a small city:

> **You:** 안녕하세요. 이 버스 시장 가요?
> *annyeonghaseyo. i beoseu sijang gayo?* — Hello. Does this bus go to the market?
>
> **Driver:** 네, 가요. 여섯 정거장 가세요.
> *ne, gayo. yeoseot jeonggeojang gaseyo.* — Yes, it does. Go six stops.
>
> **You:** 도착하면 말해 주시겠어요?
> *dochakhamyeon malhae jusigesseoyo?* — Could you tell me when we get there?
>
> **Driver:** 네, 말해 줄게요.
> *ne, malhae julgeyo.* — Sure, I'll tell you.
>
> **Driver (later):** 여기예요, 내리세요.
> *yeogiyeyo, naeriseyo.* — This is it, hop off.
>
> **You:** 감사합니다!
> *gamsahamnida!* — Thank you!

Count the stops as well as trusting the driver. On your way off, tap your card again if you plan to transfer. The rest of the ride is in [riding the bus in Korea](/korean-phrases-riding-bus).

## In a taxi, the address does the talking

Your pronunciation of a hotel name is the weak link. An English name may be something else entirely on the Korean map. Save the **Korean address** as a screenshot, hold it up, and say **이 주소로 가 주세요** (*i jusoro ga juseyo*).

Then you need two more lines: **여기서 내려 주세요** (*yeogiseo naeryeo juseyo*), "Please let me out here," and **카드 돼요?** (*kadeu dwaeyo?*), "Can I pay by card?" For luggage, add **트렁크 열어 주세요** (*teureongkeu yeoreo juseyo*), "Please open the trunk."

All three requests run on **-아/어 주세요**, Pattern 23. Once you hear it, you'll notice it in half the sentences you need.

## In a small restaurant with no photos on the wall

This is where most visitors freeze, and the fix is short. Sit down, hold up fingers for how many you are, and say **두 명이요** (*du myeongiyo*), "Two people." Then:

1. **영어 메뉴 있어요?** — If yes, you're done. If no, carry on.
2. **뭐가 들어가요?** (*mwoga deureogayo?*) — "What's in it?" Point at the line on the menu.
3. **안 매운 거 있어요?** (*an maeun geo isseoyo?*) — "Is there one that isn't spicy?"
4. **이거 하나 주세요.** (*igeo hana juseyo*) — "One of this, please."

A restaurant that only makes one or two dishes may bring food almost as soon as you sit. That's normal: the menu is the sign outside. To understand the questions coming back at you, keep [what Korean restaurant staff say](/korean-waiter-phrases-what-staff-say) open on your phone.

## At a neighborhood pharmacy

Look for the big **약** (*yak*, "medicine") on the sign. Point to where it hurts and name the part: **머리가 아파요** (*meoriga apayo*), "My head hurts." **배가 아파요** (*baega apayo*) is stomach, and **목이 아파요** (*mogi apayo*) is throat.

Before you leave, ask **하루에 몇 번 먹어요?** (*harue myeot beon meogeoyo?*), "How many times a day do I take it?" Korean uses **먹다**, "to eat," for pills. Hand over your phone or a pen with **여기에 써 주세요**, so the answer is on paper. A fuller list is in [phrases for the pharmacy](/korean-phrases-pharmacy-korea).

## The ten signs worth reading

A door can't repeat itself slowly, so signs matter more than most phrase lists admit. Learn these as shapes if you haven't studied Hangul yet.

| Sign | Romanization | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| 영업 중 | *yeongeop jung* | Open |
| 준비 중 | *junbi jung* | Getting ready; closed for now, back later |
| 오늘 휴무 | *oneul hyumu* | Closed today |
| 정기 휴무 | *jeonggi hyumu* | Regular closing day (a weekday follows) |
| 재료 소진 | *jaeryo sojin* | Ingredients ran out; finished for today |
| 당기세요 | *danggiseyo* | Pull |
| 미세요 | *miseyo* | Push |
| 출입 금지 | *churip geumji* | No entry |
| 고장 | *gojang* | Out of order |
| 셀프 | *selpeu* | Self-service (usually water and side dishes) |

**준비 중** fools the most people. It appears between lunch and dinner, when many kitchens take a **브레이크 타임** (*beureikeu taim*), "break time," often somewhere between three and five in the afternoon. Come back later and the same door is open. If you're unsure, ask **오늘 문 열어요?** (*oneul mun yeoreoyo?*), "Are you open today?"

## Questions you'll be asked, and the one-word answers

Speaking is half of it. These come at you quickly, and each takes one word to answer.

- **어디로 가세요?** (*eodiro gaseyo?*) — "Where are you going?" Show the address.
- **몇 분이세요?** (*myeot buniseyo?*) — "How many people?" **두 명이요.**
- **봉투 필요하세요?** (*bongtu piryohaseyo?*) — "Do you need a bag?" **네** (*ne*, yes) or **괜찮아요** (*gwaenchanayo*, no thanks).
- **영수증 드릴까요?** (*yeongsujeung deurilkkayo?*) — "Would you like the receipt?" The same two answers work.

When nothing lands, say **잘 모르겠어요** (*jal moreugesseoyo*), "I don't follow," then **천천히 말해 주세요**. Those are Patterns 21 and 22, and there's more help in [when someone speaks too fast](/korean-phrases-when-korean-is-too-fast).

## Can't I just use a translation app?

Use it, with a line of Korean in front. Pushing a phone toward someone without a word feels abrupt. Say **번역기 써도 돼요?** (*beonyeokgi sseodo dwaeyo?*), "May I use a translator?" (Pattern 44), then hold it out with **여기에 말해 주세요** (*yeogie malhae juseyo*), "Please speak here."

Apps do well with printed menus and typed sentences. They struggle with handwriting, noisy markets and dish names, which they translate literally. And save **1330**, the Korea Travel Helpline: it interprets in English over the phone. For emergencies, **112** is police and **119** is fire and ambulance.

## Mistakes that make a short trip harder

- **Learning long sentences.** A noun plus **있어요?** or **주세요** beats a paragraph you can't finish.
- **Reading 괜찮아요 as "yes."** When a bag or receipt is offered, it usually means "No, thank you."
- **Saying "you" to strangers.** Skip it. **기사님** (*gisanim*) is right for a driver, and **사장님** (*sajangnim*) for a shop owner.
- **Waiting for the perfect accent.** Said slowly and politely, **이거 하나 주세요** works in any accent.
