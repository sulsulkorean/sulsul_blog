The everyday way to say "I'm annoyed" in Korean is **짜증 나** (*jja-jeung na*) with friends, or **짜증 나요** (*jja-jeung na-yo*) with anyone else. 짜증 is the feeling of irritation, and 나다 means "to come up," so the phrase is literally "irritation is rising." It covers almost everything English means by "annoyed" or "this is annoying." But Korean doesn't stop there. If the annoyance is about effort, Koreans say **귀찮아** (*gwi-cha-na*). If it's the stuffy, nothing-is-moving kind, they say **답답해** (*dap-da-pae*). And if something small keeps catching your attention, it's **신경 쓰여** (*sin-gyeong sseu-yeo*). K-drama subtitles turn all four into "annoying," which is why fans often hear four different words and read the same one.

> - **짜증 나.** — I'm annoyed. / This is so annoying. (the all-purpose word)
> - **귀찮아.** — What a hassle. / I can't be bothered. (about effort)
> - **답답해.** — This is so frustrating. (stuck, slow, stifling)
> - **신경 쓰여.** — It's bugging me. (can't stop noticing it)
> - **성가셔.** — What a nuisance. (slightly old-fashioned, a bit formal)
> - **아, 진짜.** — Ugh, seriously. (the sound before any of the above)

## 짜증 나: the word you'll hear every episode

**짜증** is a noun, "irritation" or "annoyance." You can attach different verbs to it, and each one changes who the annoyance belongs to.

- **짜증 나** (*jja-jeung na*) — "I'm annoyed." The feeling comes up in you.
- **짜증 내** (*jja-jeung nae*) — "you're taking it out on someone." 내다 means "to put out," so **짜증 내지 마** is "don't snap at me."
- **짜증 나게 하다** (*jja-jeung na-ge ha-da*) — "to annoy someone." **왜 자꾸 짜증 나게 해?** is "Why do you keep annoying me?"

That middle one matters more than learners expect. In a drama, when one sibling barks at another, the reply is almost always **왜 나한테 짜증이야?** ("Why are you snapping at *me*?"). The fight is no longer about the original problem. It's about where the irritation landed.

For emphasis, Koreans stack words in front: **진짜 짜증 나**, **완전 짜증 나**, or the slangy **개짜증** (*gae-jja-jeung*), where 개 is an intensifier you should only use with close friends. Our post on [Korean slang words](/korean-slang-words-what-they-mean) explains why that little 개 has nothing to do with dogs.

One more spelling note: you'll see both **짜증 나** and **짜증나** online. The spaced version is the standard spelling, but texting drops the space all the time, so read them as the same word.

## 귀찮아: annoyed by effort, not by people

**귀찮다** (*gwi-chan-ta*) is the word English doesn't quite have. It means something is too much trouble, and you don't want to do it. It's the feeling of a Sunday morning when the laundry is still in the machine.

- **아, 귀찮아.** — Ugh, I can't be bothered.
- **씻기 귀찮아.** — I can't be bothered to wash up.
- **나가기 귀찮아.** — Going out is too much effort.

Notice the pattern: verb stem + **기 귀찮아**. That one shape covers a huge part of daily complaining. 요리하기 귀찮아 (cooking), 대답하기 귀찮아 (answering), 일어나기 귀찮아 (getting up).

When 귀찮다 points at a person, though, it becomes sharper. **귀찮게 하지 마** means "stop bothering me," and in a romance it's often the line the cold lead says right before falling in love with the person bothering them. Said to a friend's face, **너 귀찮아** ("you're a pain") stings more than 짜증 나 does, because it suggests the person is a burden.

## 답답해: when nothing moves

**답답하다** (*dap-dap-ha-da*) started as a physical feeling: a tight chest, a stuffy room, clothes that are too small. Koreans still use it that way. **여기 너무 답답해, 창문 좀 열자** means "It's so stuffy in here, let's open a window."

But in K-dramas you mostly hear it for frustration with a situation that won't move:

- **답답해 죽겠어.** — This frustration is killing me.
- **아, 답답해!** — Ugh, this is so frustrating! (watching someone fail to say what they feel)
- **너 진짜 답답하다.** — You're so frustrating. (to someone slow to act or slow to understand)

That last one is a classic best-friend line. The heroine refuses to confess, keeps saying she's "fine," and the friend sighs **답답하다, 답답해**. Fans watching at home say exactly the same thing to the screen. There's even a nickname for this: **고구마** (*go-gu-ma*), "sweet potato," because eating too much dry sweet potato leaves your chest feeling stuffed. A frustrating storyline is a 고구마 plot. The satisfying payoff, when someone finally speaks up, is **사이다** (*sa-i-da*), "soda," the drink that clears it all away.

## 신경 쓰여 and 거슬려: small things that won't let go

**신경 쓰이다** (*sin-gyeong sseu-i-da*) literally means "nerves get used." It's the word for something that keeps pulling your attention, like a ticking clock or a text you haven't answered.

It also has a double life. In romance dramas, **신경 쓰여** is half a confession. When the male lead says **너 자꾸 신경 쓰여** ("you keep bugging me"), every viewer knows he means "I can't stop thinking about you."

**거슬리다** (*geo-seul-li-da*) is the less friendly cousin. Something **거슬려** when it grates on you, like a noise, an attitude, or a tone of voice. **말투가 거슬려** means "your tone is getting on my nerves," and it is a fight word in any office drama.

## A scene: two roommates, five kinds of annoyed

Ji-woo comes home tired. Her roommate Da-eun is on the sofa.

> **지우:** 아, 진짜 짜증 나. 버스 또 놓쳤어.
> *Ugh, so annoying. I missed the bus again.*
>
> **다은:** 저녁 먹었어? 같이 라면 먹을래?
> *Did you eat? Want to have ramyeon together?*
>
> **지우:** 끓이기 귀찮아. 그냥 배달시키자.
> *Too much trouble to cook. Let's just order delivery.*
>
> **다은:** 근데 너 아까부터 왜 나한테 짜증이야?
> *But why have you been snapping at me since you got in?*
>
> **지우:** 미안. 팀장님 때문에 하루 종일 답답했어.
> *Sorry. My team leader had me frustrated all day.*
>
> **다은:** 그 사람 말투 아직도 거슬려?
> *His tone still getting to you?*
>
> **지우:** 응. 계속 신경 쓰여. 아, 몰라. 그냥 먹자.
> *Yeah. It keeps bugging me. Ugh, whatever. Let's just eat.*

Notice how each word does a different job. 짜증 나 is the general mood. 귀찮아 is about the effort of cooking. 짜증이야 is the roommate pointing out the irritation landed on her. 답답했어 is a whole day of feeling stuck. And 거슬려 and 신경 쓰여 are about one small thing that won't leave. **아, 몰라** ("ugh, I don't know") is how Koreans close the topic without solving anything.

## The sounds Koreans make before the words

A lot of annoyance in Korean comes out before any vocabulary does. These short sounds are everywhere in dramas:

- **아, 진짜.** — "Ugh, seriously." The most common one by far.
- **어휴** (*eo-hyu*) — a long sigh. Tired, fed up, often aimed at a child or a little brother.
- **에이** (*e-i*) — "Oh, come on." Mild disappointment.
- **왜 이래?** (*wae i-rae*) — "What's wrong with you?" or "Why are you like this?" Said when someone is acting up.
- **됐어.** (*dwae-sseo*) — "Forget it." The door closing on a conversation.

With strangers, keep these quiet or skip them. A loud **아, 진짜** at a shop counter reads as rude, even if you meant it about your own wallet.

## Mistakes learners make with "annoyed"

**Calling a person 짜증 나 to their face.** **너 짜증 나** is "you're annoying," and it lands harder than the English. Say it about the traffic, the weather, or your own luck instead.

**Using 귀찮아 when you mean "I'm tired."** If a friend invites you out and you say **귀찮아**, it sounds like they're the hassle. Try **오늘은 좀 피곤해** ("I'm a bit tired today") instead.

**Mixing up 답답해 and 짜증 나.** Slow Wi-Fi is 답답해 because nothing is moving. Wi-Fi that keeps disconnecting is 짜증 나 because it keeps irritating you. Both work, but the first sounds more like a native speaker.

**Forgetting the polite form.** At work or with someone older, add 요: **짜증 나요**, **귀찮아요**, **답답해요**. Even then, these describe your feeling, so keep them about situations, not about the listener. ## How to say "annoying" about a thing

English uses "annoying" as an adjective: an annoying song, an annoying person. Korean builds it from the same verbs:

- **짜증 나는 노래** — an annoying song
- **귀찮은 일** — a tedious chore
- **답답한 사람** — a frustrating person (slow to act)
- **거슬리는 소리** — a grating noise

That -는 and -은 ending turns any of them into a describing word, so you can now label almost anything in your day.
