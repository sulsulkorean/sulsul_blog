To ask the time in Korean, say **지금 몇 시예요?** (*jigeum myeot siyeyo?*), "What time is it now?" If you are stopping a stranger, put **실례지만** (*sillyejiman*, "excuse me, but") in front of it and you are done — that is the whole question, and it works at a bus stop, in a station, or in a shop where your phone has just died. The genuinely hard part is not asking. It is understanding the answer, because Korean counts hours with one set of numbers and minutes with a completely different set. **3:20** comes out as **세 시 이십 분** (*se si isip bun*) — native Korean **세** for the hour, Sino-Korean **이십** for the minutes. Mix them up and you will say something that sounds, to a Korean ear, roughly like "the third-ish o'clock and twenty-ish."

> - What time is it now? — **지금 몇 시예요?** (*jigeum myeot siyeyo?*)
> - Excuse me, what time is it? — **실례지만 지금 몇 시예요?** (*sillyejiman jigeum myeot siyeyo?*)
> - What time does it open? — **몇 시에 열어요?** (*myeot sie yeoreoyo?*)
> - What time does it close? — **몇 시에 닫아요?** (*myeot sie dadayo?*)
> - It's 3:20. — **세 시 이십 분이에요.** (*se si isip bunieyo.*)
> - Half past three. — **세 시 반이에요.** (*se si banieyo.*)

## 지금 몇 시예요? — the question itself

**몇** (*myeot*) is the question word for "how many / what number," and **시** (*si*) is the hour. Put them together and **몇 시** is literally "what hour." Add **예요** and you have a question — the same question-word-plus-예요 shape that gives you **뭐예요?** ("what is it?") and **어디예요?** ("where is it?"), Pattern 15 in the SULSUL workbook. **지금** (*jigeum*) means "now," and while you can drop it, keeping it makes the question unmistakable even if your pronunciation is still wobbly.

Speaking of pronunciation: **몇 시** is not pronounced the way it is spelled. The **ㅊ** at the end of **몇** flattens and the **시** tightens, so it comes out closer to *myeot-ssi* — almost "myuht-shi" with a hard little push on the second syllable. Aim for that and people will catch it immediately.

Two safer-than-you-think variations. To an older stranger or anyone in uniform, **실례지만 지금 몇 시예요?** is the courteous full package. Among friends in **반말**, the same question drops to **지금 몇 시야?** (*jigeum myeot siya?*). And if you want to hand someone your phone problem instead of asking outright, **혹시 시간 아세요?** (*hoksi sigan aseyo?*) — "Do you happen to know the time?" — is softer still.

You: **실례지만 지금 몇 시예요?** — *sillyejiman jigeum myeot siyeyo?* — "Excuse me, what time is it?"
Them: **지금 네 시 십 분이에요.** — *jigeum ne si sip bunieyo.* — "It's 4:10."
You: **감사합니다!** — *gamsahamnida!* — "Thank you!"

## Hours are native numbers, minutes are Sino-Korean

This is the one rule that decides whether you understand the reply. Korean has two number systems, and the clock uses both at once: **native Korean numbers for the hour, Sino-Korean numbers for the minute.** There is no logic to appeal to here — it is simply how the clock is read, and every Korean speaker does it automatically.

| Time | Korean | Romanization |
|---|---|---|
| 1:00 | **한 시** | *han si* |
| 2:00 | **두 시** | *du si* |
| 3:00 | **세 시** | *se si* |
| 4:00 | **네 시** | *ne si* |
| 5:00 | **다섯 시** | *daseot si* |
| 6:00 | **여섯 시** | *yeoseot si* |
| 7:00 | **일곱 시** | *ilgop si* |
| 8:00 | **여덟 시** | *yeodeol si* |
| 9:00 | **아홉 시** | *ahop si* |
| 10:00 | **열 시** | *yeol si* |
| 11:00 | **열한 시** | *yeolhan si* |
| 12:00 | **열두 시** | *yeoldu si* |

Notice what happens to the first four. The standalone native numbers are **하나, 둘, 셋, 넷**, but in front of a counter like **시** they shorten to **한, 두, 세, 네**. So it is **네 시**, never *넷 시*. This shortening is not special to telling time — it happens with every counter, which is why it is worth burning in now.

Minutes, meanwhile, are pure Sino-Korean: **오 분** (5), **십 분** (10), **십오 분** (15), **이십 분** (20), **삼십 분** (30), **사십오 분** (45), **오십 분** (50). Stack them and you get the real thing:

- **여섯 시 삼십 분** — *yeoseot si samsip bun* — 6:30
- **아홉 시 사십오 분** — *ahop si sasibo bun* — 9:45
- **열두 시 오 분** — *yeoldu si o bun* — 12:05

For 30 minutes, everyone says **반** (*ban*, "half") instead: **여섯 시 반** (*yeoseot si ban*), "half past six." And **전** (*jeon*, "before") handles the countdown: **세 시 십 분 전** (*se si sip bun jeon*) is "ten to three." If the two number sets still feel slippery, the full side-by-side breakdown — including which one you need when you hand over cash — is in [Korean numbers for paying and counting in Korea](/korean-numbers-paying-counting-korea).

## Morning or evening? 오전, 오후, and the 24-hour clock

Korean does not lean on AM and PM in speech the way English does. **오전** (*ojeon*) is AM and **오후** (*ohu*) is PM, and they go *before* the time, not after: **오전 열 시** (10 AM), **오후 일곱 시** (7 PM). In everyday conversation people are just as likely to use the part of day — **아침 여덟 시** (*achim yeodeol si*, "eight in the morning"), **저녁 여섯 시** (*jeonyeok yeoseot si*, "six in the evening"), **밤 열한 시** (*bam yeolhan si*, "eleven at night").

Printed schedules are a different world. Train boards, movie listings, and clinic signs all use the 24-hour clock, so you will read **19:00** on a KTX ticket — but the clerk will say it aloud as **오후 일곱 시**. Expect the mismatch and it stops being confusing. ## 몇 시에 — asking what time something happens

Once you can say **몇 시**, adding one letter turns it into the most useful travel question of all. Attach **에** and you are asking *at* what time something happens: **몇 시에** (*myeot sie*). This is Pattern 28 in the workbook, the time-appointment frame, and it carries you through opening hours, departures, and meeting times.

- **몇 시에 열어요?** — *myeot sie yeoreoyo?* — "What time do you open?"
- **몇 시에 닫아요?** — *myeot sie dadayo?* — "What time do you close?"
- **버스가 몇 시에 와요?** — *beoseuga myeot sie wayo?* — "What time does the bus come?"
- **몇 시에 출발해요?** — *myeot sie chulbalhaeyo?* — "What time does it leave?"
- **몇 시에 만날까요?** — *myeot sie mannalkkayo?* — "What time shall we meet?"

To ask for a span rather than a point, use **부터** ("from") and **까지** ("until"): **몇 시부터 몇 시까지 해요?** (*myeot sibuteo myeot sikkaji haeyo?*), "What hours are you open?" It is the single best thing to say at a restaurant door, a museum desk, or a **찜질방** counter, and the answer usually comes back in the same shape — **열 시부터 밤 열 시까지요** ("ten in the morning until ten at night").

And when you answer with a time yourself, the **에** rides along: **두 시에 만나요** (*du sie mannayo*), "Let's meet at two." One trap here, and it is the mistake almost every learner makes once — **에** does not attach to **지금** (now), **오늘** (today), **내일** (tomorrow), or **어제** (yesterday). It is **내일 만나요**, never *내일에 만나요*.

You: **여기 몇 시부터 몇 시까지 해요?** — *yeogi myeot sibuteo myeot sikkaji haeyo?* — "What hours are you open here?"
Them: **열한 시부터 밤 열 시까지예요.** — *yeolhan sibuteo bam yeol sikkajiyeyo.* — "Eleven in the morning to ten at night."
You: **주말에도 해요?** — *jumaredo haeyo?* — "Are you open on weekends too?"

## When you don't catch the number

Numbers are the first thing that vanishes at real speed, and a half-heard time is worse than no time at all. Three rescues, in order of usefulness:

**천천히 말해 주세요** (*cheoncheonhi malhae juseyo*) — "Please say it slowly." Perfectly polite, and Koreans will usually slow right down and repeat the number by itself.

**다시 말해 주세요** (*dasi malhae juseyo*) — "Please say it again." Use this when you caught the hour but lost the minutes.

**여기 좀 써 주세요** (*yeogi jom sseo juseyo*) — "Please write it here." Hand over your phone or a receipt. Digits work across every language barrier, and nobody minds. ## What usually goes wrong

**Using Sino numbers for the hour.** **삼 시** is not a time. It is **세 시**. If you learned numbers by counting **일, 이, 삼, 사**, your instinct will pull you exactly the wrong way on the clock.

**Using native numbers for the minutes.** **스물 분** is not a time either — minutes are always **이십 분**. The rule flips halfway through the sentence, every single time.

**Saying 넷 시 instead of 네 시.** Before a counter, **하나, 둘, 셋, 넷** shorten to **한, 두, 세, 네**. Same for **열하나 → 열한 시** and **열둘 → 열두 시**.

**Confusing 몇 시 with 몇 시간.** **몇 시** asks what time it is; **몇 시간** asks how many hours something takes. **몇 시간 걸려요?** (*myeot sigan geollyeoyo?*) means "How long does it take?" — a very different question from the one you meant to ask.

**Saying 시간 있어요? when you want the clock.** **시간 있어요?** (*sigan isseoyo?*) means "Do you have time / are you free?" — it is an invitation, not a request for the time. For the clock, stick to **지금 몇 시예요?**

**Putting 에 on 지금.** **지금** already means "now," so **지금에** is ungrammatical. Time words that point at the current moment — **지금, 오늘, 내일, 어제, 언제** — refuse the particle.
