To say something in Korean with Google Translate, type a **short, complete English sentence with "please" in it**, check that the Korean ends in **-요** or **-세요**, and show the screen while you say one real Korean line out loud: **잠깐만요, 이거 보세요** (*jam-kkan-man-yo, i-geo bo-se-yo*), "One moment, please look at this." That pairing works far better than silently pushing a phone across a counter. The app handles the hard sentence, and your own voice handles the politeness that the app often gets wrong.

> - **잠깐만요** (*jam-kkan-man-yo*) — "Just a moment."
> - **번역기 좀 쓸게요** (*beo-nyeok-gi jom sseul-ge-yo*) — "I'll use a translator for a second."
> - **이거 보세요** (*i-geo bo-se-yo*) — "Please look at this."
> - **여기 말해 주세요** (*yeo-gi mal-hae ju-se-yo*) — "Please speak into this."
> - **여기 써 주세요** (*yeo-gi sseo ju-se-yo*) — "Please type it here."
> - **이거 맞아요?** (*i-geo ma-ja-yo?*) — "Is this right?"
> - **무슨 뜻이에요?** (*mu-seun tteu-si-e-yo?*) — "What does it mean?"

## Why the translation alone isn't enough

Koreans are used to translator apps. Pharmacists, taxi drivers, and hotel staff see a phone screen held up many times a day, and most will happily lean in to read it. The trouble is what the screen says.

Korean changes its sentence endings depending on who you're talking to. Google Translate has to guess. Sometimes it gives you stiff, textbook formality. Sometimes it gives you **반말**, the casual speech friends use with each other, which sounds rude coming from a stranger. And it loves the word **당신** (*dang-sin*) for "you," a word Koreans almost never use face to face with someone they just met.

So the screen carries the information, and you carry the tone. A polite spoken line before and after the phone smooths over whatever odd wording the app produced.

## Typing English so the Korean comes out polite

The single biggest improvement comes before you even hit translate. A few habits:

**One idea per sentence.** "Do you have this in a bigger size?" translates cleanly. "Hey, so I was wondering if maybe you've got this one but bigger, or else a different color" turns into a mess.

**Always include "please."** "Please give me a bag" usually comes out as **봉투 주세요** (*bong-tu ju-se-yo*), the natural polite request. "Give me a bag" can come out blunt. This is the same **주세요** you'll lean on all trip (**Pattern 01** in the SULSUL workbook), and the app produces it most reliably when your English sounds like a request.

**Drop "you" when you can.** "Is there a bathroom here?" gives **여기 화장실 있어요?** (*yeo-gi hwa-jang-sil i-sseo-yo?*), which is perfect. "Do you have a bathroom?" risks dragging in **당신**.

**No slang, no idioms.** "I'm starving" may be translated literally. "I am very hungry" won't be.

**Check the last syllable.** You don't need to read Korean to do this. If the sentence ends in **요** or **니다**, it's polite. If it ends in **어**, **아**, **야**, or **지** with no 요 after it, rephrase and try again.

## Lines to say before you hold up the phone

Silence plus a phone feels abrupt. Open with something short:

**저기요, 잠깐만요.** — *jeo-gi-yo, jam-kkan-man-yo.* — "Excuse me, one moment."  
**한국어를 잘 못해요. 번역기 좀 쓸게요.** — *han-gu-geo-reul jal mo-tae-yo. beo-nyeok-gi jom sseul-ge-yo.* — "I don't speak Korean well. I'll use a translator."  
**이거 보세요.** — *i-geo bo-se-yo.* — "Please look at this."

**번역기** (*beo-nyeok-gi*) is the everyday word for any translator app. Koreans also just say the app's name: **구글 번역** (*gu-geul beo-nyeok*) for Google Translate or **파파고** (*pa-pa-go*) for Papago.

A full exchange at a convenience store might look like this:

You: **저기요, 이거 보세요.** — *jeo-gi-yo, i-geo bo-se-yo.* — "Excuse me, please look at this." *(screen: 이 근처에 ATM 있어요? — "Is there an ATM near here?")*  
Clerk: **아, 밖에 나가서 왼쪽이에요.** — *a, ba-kke na-ga-seo oen-jjo-gi-e-yo.* — "Ah, go outside and it's on the left."  
You: **왼쪽이요? 감사합니다!** — *oen-jjo-gi-yo? gam-sa-ham-ni-da!* — "Left? Thank you!"

Notice you didn't need the app for the reply. You caught one word, **왼쪽** (left), repeated it as a question, and confirmed. That's often all it takes.

## Handing the phone over: 여기 말해 주세요

The answer is the hard part. When you can't follow, turn the phone around and use conversation mode, then ask them to speak into it:

**여기 말해 주세요.** — *yeo-gi mal-hae ju-se-yo.* — "Please speak into this."  
**여기 써 주세요.** — *yeo-gi sseo ju-se-yo.* — "Please type it here."  
**천천히 말해 주세요.** — *cheon-cheon-hi mal-hae ju-se-yo.* — "Please speak slowly."

These are all **Pattern 23**, **(V)아/어 주세요**, the polite "please do this for me" ending. If you want to sound a touch softer with an older person, swap 말해 for **말씀해**: **여기 말씀해 주세요** (*yeo-gi mal-sseum-hae ju-se-yo*).

## Checking the result: 이거 맞아요? and 무슨 뜻이에요?

Translations drift, so confirm the important stuff: times, prices you're about to pay, allergy information, addresses.

**이거 맞아요?** — *i-geo ma-ja-yo?* — "Is this right?"  
**이 말 맞아요?** — *i mal ma-ja-yo?* — "Is this wording right?"

Expect **네, 맞아요** (*ne, ma-ja-yo*), "Yes, that's right," or **아니요** with a correction. That's **Pattern 18**, **맞아요 / 아니에요**.

When the translated reply makes no sense, point at the strange word and ask with **Pattern 20**:

**이거 무슨 뜻이에요?** — *i-geo mu-seun tteu-si-e-yo?* — "What does this mean?"

People will usually try another word or act it out. More ways to untangle a confusing reply are in [what do you mean in Korean](/what-do-you-mean-in-korean).

## Where Google Translate gets Korean wrong

A few predictable trouble spots:

**Menus through the camera.** Camera translation of dish names is often literal. **물냉면** becomes something like "water cold noodles," and **육회**, beef tartare, has famously shown up as "six times" because **육** is also the word for six. Use the camera to get the gist, then ask **이거 뭐예요?** (*i-geo mwo-ye-yo?*), "What's this?" Our guide to [reading a Korean menu](/korean-food-phrases-reading-a-menu) covers the cooking words that do the real work.

**Allergies.** "I can't eat peanuts" is too vague. Type "I am allergic to peanuts. Peanuts are dangerous for me." Then confirm with **이거 맞아요?** For a full list, see [Korean phrases for food allergies](/korean-phrases-food-allergies-korea).

**Addresses.** Translated addresses lose the details drivers need. In a taxi, show the address in Korean from a map app instead of a translation. More in [how to ask for a taxi in Korean](/ask-for-taxi-in-korean).

**Tone.** As above, watch for **당신** and endings without **요**.

## Google Translate or Papago?

Many Koreans and longtime residents prefer **Papago**, the translator made by Naver, for Korean. It tends to handle everyday spoken phrases and honorific endings well, and it has a polite-speech toggle for Korean output. Google Translate is fine for most travel sentences and works smoothly with Google Lens on signs.

## Common mistakes with a translator app

**Showing the phone without a word.** Even **이거 보세요** changes how the moment feels.

**Translating long paragraphs.** Break them up. Three short screens beat one long one.

**Trusting it on anything that matters.** Medicine, allergies, and tickets deserve a quick **이거 맞아요?**

**Using it for words you already know.** **감사합니다**, **이거 주세요**, **얼마예요?** sound much warmer coming from you than from a speaker.

## Practice the lines around the app

The app can carry the long sentences, but the short lines around it are yours: **잠깐만요**, **이거 보세요**, **여기 말해 주세요**, **이거 맞아요?** They only help if they come out without hesitation while someone is waiting for you. Say each one out loud until it's automatic, then run them in order as a little scene: open, show, hand over, confirm, thank.
