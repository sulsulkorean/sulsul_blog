**자식** (*jasik*) means **"child"** or **"offspring"** — the sons and daughters of a parent. A mother who says **우리 자식들** (*uri jasikdeul*) is talking about "my kids." But put **이** ("this") in front of it and shout, and **이 자식!** (*i jasik!*) turns into **"you jerk!"** That second meaning is the one most K-drama fans hear first, which is why the word is so confusing.

> - **자식** — child, offspring — a parent talking about their kids
> - **내 자식 / 우리 자식** — my child — warm, protective
> - **이 자식 / 저 자식 / 그 자식** — this guy, that jerk — rude
> - **이 자식아!** — "you jerk!" — shouted straight at someone
> - **짜식** (*jjasik*) — "you rascal" — teasing, between close friends
> - **자녀** (*janyeo*) — children — the polite, formal word

Same word, three moods. Below: where each one comes from, the scenes where you hear it, how to tell them apart by sound, and what to say instead if you want to ask a real person about their kids.

## 자식 starts as a family word

The original meaning is completely neutral. **자식** comes from Chinese characters for "child" and "breath" or "offspring," and older Koreans use it every day:

| Korean | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **자식이 몇이에요?** | *jasigi myeochieyo?* | How many kids do you have? |
| **자식 키우기 힘들어요.** | *jasik kiugi himdeureoyo.* | Raising kids is hard. |
| **자식 걱정** | *jasik geokjeong* | worrying about your kids |
| **자식 자랑** | *jasik jarang* | bragging about your kids |

You hear these in family dramas constantly. A father at a wedding, a mother on the phone with her sister, two grandmothers comparing grandchildren on a park bench — **자식** is the word they reach for.

There is even a proverb every Korean knows: **자식 이기는 부모 없다** (*jasik igineun bumo eopda*), "No parent ever wins against their child." A mother says it with a sigh when her son refuses to come home for the holidays and she gives in anyway.

**자식** covers both sons and daughters. If you need to be specific, use **아들** (*adeul*, son) or **딸** (*ttal*, daughter).

## How 이 자식 became an insult

Here is the turn. When you point at a grown man with **이** ("this") and call him "child," you are treating him like a misbehaving kid. That is the insult. It is the same move as calling an adult "boy" in English.

- **이 자식** (*i jasik*) — this guy / you jerk, about someone right here
- **저 자식** (*jeo jasik*) — that jerk over there
- **그 자식** (*geu jasik*) — that guy, someone you are talking about

The last one is very common in breakup scenes. A friend comforts the heroine with **그 자식 잊어** (*geu jasik ijeo*), "Forget that guy." Nobody needs to say his name.

When the insult is shouted straight at someone, it usually picks up **아** at the end: **이 자식아!** (*i jasiga!*). Often the sentence never finishes. **이 자식이...** (*i jasigi...*) trailing off, with a clenched fist, is a classic K-drama beat right before someone grabs a collar.

How strong is it? Clearly rude, but milder than **새끼** (*saekki*), which you hear bleeped or cut on TV. **이 자식** is the version a scriptwriter can use on prime time.

## 짜식: the friendly rascal

Now the soft version. Say **자식** fast and harden the first sound and you get **짜식** (*jjasik*). Between close friends — especially men — it means something like "you rascal" or "you little punk," with a grin.

A: **나 합격했어!** — *na hapgyeokhaesseo!* — "I passed!"  
B: **짜식, 해낼 줄 알았어.** — *jjasik, haenael jul arasseo.* — "You rascal, I knew you'd do it."

An older brother or a coach might say **짜식...** while ruffling someone's hair. Dad characters in dramas use it with their sons the night before the son leaves for military service. In those scenes it carries a lot of love.

Hearing the difference is mostly about tone:

- **Shouted, short, sharp** — an insult. *이 자식아!*
- **Low, with a small laugh or a sigh** — affection. *짜식...*
- **Flat, inside a normal sentence** — just "children." *자식이 둘이에요.*

## A short scene with all three

Here is how one family drama episode could use the word three ways.

Mother, on the phone: **자식이 뭔지... 또 밥도 안 먹고 나갔어.**  
*jasigi mwonji... tto bapdo an meokgo nagasseo.*  
"Kids, honestly... he left without eating again."

Son's friend, seeing a guy who cheated on his sister: **저 자식 저기 있다.**  
*jeo jasik jeogi itda.*  
"That jerk is over there."

Father, hearing his son got the job: **짜식, 고생했다.**  
*jjasik, gosaenghaetda.*  
"You rascal. You worked hard."

Three lines, three completely different feelings, one root word.

## What to say to a real person instead

As a learner, keep **이 자식** and **짜식** for your ears, not your mouth. Even between friends, they sound strange from a foreigner who has known someone for a week. They work only inside a long, close relationship.

If you want to ask a Korean person about their kids, **자식** itself is a little too plain for someone you don't know well. Use these:

- **자녀가 있으세요?** — *janyeoga isseuseyo?* — "Do you have children?" Polite and safe.
- **자제분** (*jajebun*) — an honorific way to say "your children," for someone older. **자제분이 몇 살이에요?** — "How old are your children?"
- **아이** or **애** (*ai*, *ae*) — kid, child. **애가 몇 살이에요?** — casual and friendly.

And if you want the warm, family feeling without any risk, listen for **우리 애** (*uri ae*, "our kid") and **내 새끼** (*nae saekki*). That last one sounds alarming after what you just read, but when a grandmother hugs a baby and says **아이고, 내 새끼**, it means "oh, my precious baby." Again, tone does all the work.

## Mistakes learners make with 자식

1. **Reading every 자식 as an insult.** In a sentence like **자식 둘을 키웠어요**, it just means "I raised two children."
2. **Using 이 자식 as a joke with a new friend.** It may land as a real insult. Wait until they use it with you first.
3. **Calling your own parents' generation "자식."** **자식** is what parents call children, not the other way around.
4. **Missing the sound change.** **짜식** and **자식** look similar in romanization but feel different. The tense **ㅉ** is what makes it playful.

## Say it out loud

Practice the tone, not just the word. Say each line three times:

1. **자녀가 있으세요?** — calm, polite, a small rise at the end.
2. **자식 키우기 힘들어요.** — a tired parent's sigh.
3. **짜식, 해낼 줄 알았어.** — low voice, a smile in it.

Then rewatch a fight scene and listen for **이 자식이...** before the subtitle appears. Once you can hear which of the three meanings is on screen, you are hearing Korean the way Koreans do.
