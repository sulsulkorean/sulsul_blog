If you speak Urdu and watch K-dramas, a few Korean words will feel familiar from the first episode. **엄마** (*eom-ma*) means **امی** — "mom." **아빠** (*a-ppa*) means **ابو** — "dad." **차** (*cha*) means **چائے** — "tea." But one tiny word will fool you every time: **네** (*ne*) sounds like the start of **نہیں**, and it means **yes** — the Korean **جی**.

> - **엄마** (*eom-ma*) — امی — "mom"
> - **아빠** (*a-ppa*) — ابو — "dad"
> - **차** (*cha*) — چائے — "tea"
> - **네** (*ne*) — جی، ہاں — "yes" (not "no"!)
> - **아이고** (*a-i-go*) — ہائے اللہ — "oh no, oh my"

Below: the words that sound like home, the yes-and-no trap, the Korean versions of **باجی** and **بھائی جان**, how Korean politeness lines up with **تو، تم، آپ**, and the reactions you hear in every episode.

## Ammi, Abbu, Chai: Korean words an Urdu ear already knows

- **엄마** (*eom-ma*) — **امی** — mom. The word every drama character cries out when things fall apart.
- **아빠** (*a-ppa*) — **ابو** — dad. Close enough that you will never forget it.
- **차** (*cha*) — **چائے** — tea. Both words travelled from Chinese *chá*, one through Persian into Urdu, one straight into Korean. **녹차** (*nok-cha*) is green tea; **차 마실래?** (*cha ma-sil-lae?*) is "Want some tea?" Careful: **차** also means "car," so context does the work.
- **이리 와** (*i-ri wa*) — **ادھر آؤ** — "come here." The hero's line, arms open.

These matches are mostly coincidence or shared borrowing, not family ties. That is fine. A word you can hear in your own language is a word you keep.

## 네 means yes: the trap for every Urdu speaker

This is the one to learn first, because it can turn a conversation upside down.

| Korean | Sounds like | Means | Urdu |
|---|---|---|---|
| **네** (*ne*) | "nah," "nay" | yes | جی، ہاں |
| **예** (*ye*) | "yeh" | yes (more formal) | جی ہاں |
| **아니요** (*a-ni-yo*) | — | no | نہیں جی |
| **아니** (*a-ni*) | — | no (casual) | نہیں |

When a shopkeeper asks **카드 돼요?** — "Can I pay by card?" — and hears **네**, the answer is **جی، ہو جائے گا**. And **네** works just like **جی** does: it is not only "yes" but also "I'm listening," "okay," and "here!" when a teacher calls your name.

## باجی and بھائی جان in Korean: the word depends on who speaks

In Urdu you call an older woman **باجی** or **آپی**, and an older man **بھائی جان** — whether or not you are related. Korean works the same way, with one twist: **the speaker's gender changes the word.**

| Korean | Who says it | Urdu | English |
|---|---|---|---|
| **언니** (*eon-ni*) | a girl, to an older woman | آپی، باجی | older sister |
| **누나** (*nu-na*) | a boy, to an older woman | باجی | older sister |
| **오빠** (*o-ppa*) | a girl, to an older man | بھائی جان | older brother |
| **형** (*hyeong*) | a boy, to an older man | بھائی جان | older brother |
| **동생** (*dong-saeng*) | anyone, about someone younger | چھوٹا بھائی، چھوٹی بہن | younger sibling |

The big surprise for Urdu speakers: a Korean girl calls her **boyfriend** **오빠**. Nobody would call a boyfriend **بھائی جان**, so this is where the match stops. In Korean romance, **오빠** sounds sweet, close, and a little playful.

## تو، تم، آپ: Korean has three levels too

Urdu speakers already live with three ways to say "you." Korean has three ways to end a sentence, and they map neatly:

- **고마워** (*go-ma-wo*) — thanks, to a close friend — the **تم** level
- **고마워요** (*go-ma-wo-yo*) — thank you, everyday polite — the **آپ** level
- **감사합니다** (*gam-sa-ham-ni-da*) — thank you, formal — **آپ کا بہت شکریہ**

The same goes for sorry: **미안해** (*mi-an-hae*) to a friend, **미안해요** for everyday politeness, and **죄송합니다** (*joe-song-ham-ni-da*) — the Korean **معاف کیجیے** — to a boss or an elder.

Think of Korean **-요** as Urdu **جی** stuck onto the end of a sentence. Add it, and you sound respectful. Drop it with a stranger, and it lands like **تو** — too close, too fast.

## Same sentence order as Urdu

English says "I ate food." Urdu and Korean both save the verb for last:

> **나는 밥을 먹었어.** (*na-neun ba-beul meo-geo-sseo.*)
> **میں نے کھانا کھایا۔** (*main ne khana khaya.*)
> I — food — ate.

Both languages also put small markers after words instead of before. Korean **친구한테** (*chin-gu-han-te*) is **دوست کو** — "to a friend." Korean **집에서** (*ji-be-seo*) is **گھر پر / گھر سے** — "at home, from home." If you build the sentence in Urdu order, the Korean comes out right.

## Haye, Uff, Wah: K-drama reactions in Urdu

- **아이고** (*a-i-go*) — **ہائے اللہ** / **اف** — the all-purpose sigh: tired, shocked, sore back, big bill.
- **대박** (*dae-bak*) — **واہ! کمال ہے!** — "Amazing!"
- **헐** (*heol*) — **کیا؟!** — "Wait, what?"
- **진짜?** (*jin-jja?*) — **سچ میں؟** — "Really?"
- **어떡해** (*eo-tteo-ke*) — **اب کیا کروں؟** — "What do I do now?"
- **괜찮아** (*gwaen-chan-a*) — **کوئی بات نہیں** — "It's okay."
- **화이팅** (*hwa-i-ting*) — **شاباش! تم کر سکتے ہو!** — "You've got this!"

## کھانا کھا لیا؟ The Korean way of saying "I care"

An Urdu-speaking mother asks **کھانا کھا لیا؟** before anything else. So does a Korean one.

![Friends sharing drinks and talking at a Korean cafe](/assets/blog/scenes/cafe.jpg)

- **밥 먹었어?** (*bap meo-geo-sseo?*) — **کھانا کھا لیا؟** — "Did you eat?" It means "I was thinking about you."
- **밥 먹었어요?** (*bap meo-geo-sseo-yo?*) — **آپ نے کھانا کھا لیا؟** — the polite version.
- **조심해** (*jo-sim-hae*) — **اپنا خیال رکھنا** — "Take care."
- **잘 자** (*jal ja*) — **شب بخیر** — "Good night, sleep well."
- **보고 싶어** (*bo-go si-peo*) — **تمہاری یاد آ رہی ہے** — "I miss you."

A short exchange between friends:

> **A:** 밥 먹었어? (*bap meo-geo-sseo?*) — کھانا کھا لیا؟
> **B:** 아니, 아직. 너는? (*a-ni, a-jik. neo-neun?*) — نہیں، ابھی نہیں۔ تم نے؟
> **A:** 나도. 차 한잔하고 같이 먹자! (*na-do. cha han-jan-ha-go ga-chi meok-ja!*) — میں نے بھی نہیں۔ چائے پی کر ساتھ کھاتے ہیں!

## Three more sound-alikes that mean something else

**바지** (*ba-ji*) is **pants**, not **باجی**. Call an older woman **바지** and you have just called her "trousers." Use **언니** or **누나**.

**나** (*na*) means **میں** — "I," not **نہ**. **나 가** (*na ga*) is "I'm going," not "don't go." "Don't go" is **가지 마** (*ga-ji ma*).

**오빠 to strangers.** Urdu speakers say **بھائی** to any shopkeeper. In Korean, a girl saying **오빠** to a stranger sounds flirty. Use **사장님** (*sa-jang-nim*) for a shop owner and **저기요** (*jeo-gi-yo*) for "excuse me."
