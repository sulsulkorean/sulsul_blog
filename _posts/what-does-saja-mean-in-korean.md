**사자** (*saja*) is not one word. It is a handful of different words that happen to be spelled and said the same way, and Korean sorts them out by context alone. The most common one in a dictionary is **사자** = **lion**. The one that sent you searching is almost certainly the second half of **저승사자** (*jeoseung saja*), the black-hatted reaper who shows up in dramas to collect the dead — literally "the messenger of the afterlife." There is also **사자** meaning *envoy / messenger* on its own, **사자** meaning *the deceased*, and, in a completely unrelated corner, **사자** as a whole sentence: "let's buy it."

> - **사자** (*saja*) — lion.
> - **저승사자** (*jeoseung saja*) — the reaper; "messenger from the afterlife."
> - **저승** (*jeoseung*) — the world of the dead. Its opposite is **이승** (*iseung*), this world.
> - **사자** (*saja*, 使者) — a messenger or envoy sent by someone with power.
> - **사자성어** (*sajaseongeo*) — a four-character idiom. Different 사자 again: "four characters."
> - **사자!** (*saja*) — "Let's buy it!" from the verb **사다** (*sada*).

## 저승사자: the 사자 you met in a drama

When a drama character in a black **갓** (*gat*, the traditional wide-brimmed hat) and a black coat steps out of nowhere and everyone in the room goes quiet, that is a **저승사자**. Break it apart and the mystery disappears: **저승** is the afterlife, and **사자** here is the *messenger* 사자 — someone sent to carry out an order. He is not Death himself. He is staff. He has a list, he has a job, and in most modern dramas he is exhausted by it.

That framing matters, because English subtitles usually write "grim reaper," which imports a hooded skeleton with a scythe that Korean folklore never had. The 저승사자 is a bureaucrat of the underworld — closer to a civil servant than a monster — which is exactly why Korean writers can make him funny, romantic, or tragic without breaking anything.

Them: **저 사람 저승사자야.** — *jeo saram jeoseung sajaya.* — "That guy is a reaper."  
You: **진짜요? 무서워요.** — *jinjjayo? museowoyo.* — "Seriously? That's scary."  
Them: **무섭긴, 완전 순해.** — *museopgin, wanjeon sunhae.* — "Scary nothing, he's a total softie."

Two pieces of vocabulary travel with him. **저승** (*jeoseung*) is the world of the dead, and **이승** (*iseung*) is this one — you will hear both in the same breath in any drama that touches the afterlife. And if a Korean friend says **저승사자 같아** (*jeoseung saja gata*) about a real person, it is not supernatural; it means someone dressed in all black looking grim, the way English might say "you look like you came from a funeral."

**Saja Boys**, the group name that had half the internet typing this exact question after *KPop Demon Hunters*, is this 사자. Demon boys posing as an idol group, named after the messengers who come to collect souls. ## 사자 as "lion" — the dictionary's first answer

Type 사자 into a dictionary and you get **lion** before anything supernatural. This is the 사자 a Korean child learns first, the one on the zoo sign, the one in **사자자리** (*sajajari*), the star sign Leo.

You: **사자자리예요.** — *sajajarieyo.* — "I'm a Leo."  
Them: **아, 어쩐지.** — *a, eojjeonji.* — "Ah, that explains it."

It also lives inside **사자탈** (*sajatal*), the lion mask worn in **사자춤** (*sajachum*), the lion dance performed at festivals. So if someone mentions 사자 at a temple festival, nobody is talking about death — there is a person in a lion costume about ten meters away.

How do Koreans know which 사자 is meant, with zero spelling difference? Three things, in this order: the **compound**, the **verb**, and the **place**. 저승 in front means reaper. A zoo, a mask or a horoscope means lion. And a 사자 that *appears* (**나타나다**) is usually the reaper, while a 사자 that *roars* (**울부짖다**) is definitely the animal. Native speakers do not consciously choose; the sentence chooses for them.

## The 사자s nobody puts on a fan account

**사자 (使者), the envoy.** On its own this is formal, historical vocabulary — a messenger dispatched by a king or a government. You meet it in sageuk (historical dramas) and in set phrases, not in conversation. Its usefulness to you is indirect: it is the piece that makes 저승사자 make sense.

**사자 (死者), the deceased.** This is news-and-documents Korean, the word on an official report. Everyday speech uses **돌아가신 분** (*doragasin bun*), "the person who has passed," which is gentler and the one you should actually say if the subject ever comes up.

**사자성어** (*sajaseongeo*) is a different 사자 entirely — "four characters." These are the four-character idioms Koreans quote constantly, the way English quotes proverbs. Drama characters use them to sound wise, and variety shows use them as quiz material.

| Idiom | Romanization | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| 화룡점정 | *hwaryongjeomjeong* | The final touch that brings the whole thing to life |
| 역지사지 | *yeokjisaji* | Put yourself in the other person's place |
| 유비무환 | *yubimuhwan* | Prepare, and there is nothing to fear |
| 고진감래 | *gojingamnae* | Hardship first, sweetness after |

You do not need to memorize these to watch dramas. You do need to know that when a character says **사자성어**, they are announcing a proverb is coming — not talking about lions.

## 사자 that is a sentence, not a noun

Here is the one that trips up learners reading Korean comments online. **사다** (*sada*) means "to buy." Attach the casual propositive ending **-자** and you get **사자** — "let's buy it."

Them: **이거 사자.** — *igeo saja.* — "Let's buy this."  
You: **그래, 사자.** — *geurae, saja.* — "Okay, let's buy it."

Same two syllables, no relation to lions or reapers. This pattern is everywhere once you see it: **가자** (*gaja*) "let's go," **먹자** (*meokja*) "let's eat," **보자** (*boja*) "let's watch." It is **반말** (*banmal*), the casual speech level, so it works with friends and with people younger than you, and it needs an upgrade before you say it to a senior — **살까요?** (*salkkayo?*), "shall we buy it?" ## Mistakes K-drama fans make with 사자

**Calling a person 사자 to mean "reaper."** Drop 저승 and you have called them a lion. The full compound is not optional.

**Hearing 사자 in 사자후.** **사자후** (*sajahu*) is "lion's roar," used for someone delivering a furious speech. It is the animal 사자, not the reaper — a detail that flips the meaning of a scene.

**Pronouncing it "sah-JAH."** Korean does not stress syllables the way English does. Both beats are even and light: *sa-ja*. Pushing the second syllable makes it sound like a different word entirely.

**Assuming the spelling helps.** In modern Korean all of these are written 사자, with no accent mark and no hanja in sight. Even Koreans occasionally need a beat to sort them out — and they do it with context, which is the actual skill worth copying.

**Learning it only as trivia.** Knowing what 사자 means is worth something; being able to say **저승사자 같아** out loud at the right moment is worth much more.
