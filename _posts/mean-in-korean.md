The most common way to say "you're mean" in Korean is **나빠** (*na-ppa*) — literally "bad." It is what a K-drama character says, half-angry and half-hurt, when someone teases them, forgets a promise, or leaves without saying goodbye. If you want something stronger, **못됐어** (*mot-dwae-sseo*) means "you're a nasty person," and if someone is being petty or stingy, the word is **치사해** (*chi-sa-hae*). Which one you pick changes the whole feeling of the line, so here is how they work.

> - **나빠** (*na-ppa*) — "you're mean / that's not nice." Soft, often with a pout.
> - **못됐어** (*mot-dwae-sseo*) — "you're nasty / you're horrible." A real accusation.
> - **치사해** (*chi-sa-hae*) — "that's petty / you're so stingy." Small-minded meanness.
> - **심술궂다** (*sim-sul-gut-da*) — "spiteful, mean-spirited." Describes a person's nature.
> - **너무해** (*neo-mu-hae*) — "that's too much / that's harsh." Complains about an action.

## 나빠: the "you're mean" you hear in every romance

**나빠** comes from **나쁘다** (*na-ppeu-da*, "to be bad"). Said to a person, it does not mean "you are an evil person." It means "what you just did hurt me," and it often comes with crossed arms or a turned back. In a romance, the lead says **오빠 나빠!** (*oppa nappa!*, "You're so mean!") when the boyfriend teases her, and nobody thinks the relationship is over.

That softness is why 나빠 is the safe, everyday choice. Some common shapes:

- **나빠.** — "You're mean." (to a close friend or partner)
- **진짜 나빠.** — "You're really mean."
- **나쁜 사람.** (*na-ppeun sa-ram*) — "You bad person." Common in sad scenes, said quietly.
- **나빴어요.** (*na-ppa-sseo-yo*) — "That was mean." Polite, about something already done.

Notice the last one. With people you speak politely to, Koreans usually describe the *action* as bad (**그건 좀 나빴어요**, "that was a bit mean") instead of calling the person bad.

## 못됐어: when it stops being cute

**못됐어** is from **못되다**, "to turn out badly" — as in a person who did not grow up right. It is much heavier than 나빠. In a K-drama, this is what the heroine says to the scheming rival, or what a mother-in-law hears behind her back: **진짜 못됐다** (*jin-jja mot-dwaet-da*, "she's truly nasty").

Use it about behavior that is cruel on purpose: bullying, lying to hurt someone, saying something cutting in public. Two useful shapes:

- **너 왜 이렇게 못됐어?** (*neo wae i-reo-ke mot-dwae-sseo?*) — "Why are you so nasty?"
- **말을 왜 그렇게 못되게 해?** (*ma-reul wae geu-reo-ke mot-doe-ge hae?*) — "Why do you talk so meanly?"

Said to a friend as a joke, 못됐어 can still be playful, but it lands harder than you think. Save it for when you mean it.

## 치사해 and 너무해: petty and harsh

Not all meanness is cruelty. Sometimes it is just small and annoying. That is **치사해**. Your friend eats the last piece of fried chicken without asking, refuses to share their umbrella, or keeps score of who paid last time — **아, 치사해!** (*a, chi-sa-hae!*, "Ugh, so petty!"). Siblings in family dramas throw it at each other constantly. Its cousin **쪼잔해** (*jjo-jan-hae*) means "stingy, small-minded" and is a little more slangy.

**너무해** (*neo-mu-hae*) means "that's too much." It complains about what someone did, not who they are, which makes it a good middle choice. **너무해요** (*neo-mu-hae-yo*) is polite enough to say to a coworker who dumped extra work on you.

## 심술궂다: describing a mean person

When you are not talking *to* someone but *about* them, Koreans often use **심술궂다** (*sim-sul-gut-da*) or the noun **심술** (*sim-sul*, "spite"). **심술궂은 사람** is "a mean-spirited person" — the kind who hides your shoes for fun. **심술 부리지 마** (*sim-sul bu-ri-ji ma*) means "stop being spiteful," a line parents say to a child who knocks over a sibling's blocks.

Two other describing words you will see in subtitles:

- **성격이 나쁘다** (*seong-gyeo-gi na-ppeu-da*) — "has a bad personality," the plain adult way to call someone mean.
- **차갑다** (*cha-gap-da*) — "cold." A distant, unfriendly person is 차가운 사람, which is "cold" more than "mean."

## How it sounds in a scene

Here is a short scene between two friends, 하나 and 준호, after 준호 reads her diary out loud as a joke:

> **하나:** 야! 그거 내 일기잖아. 진짜 나빠.
> *(Hey! That's my diary. You're really mean.)*
>
> **준호:** 장난이야, 장난. 한 줄만 읽었어.
> *(It's a joke, a joke. I only read one line.)*
>
> **하나:** 한 줄이든 뭐든, 그건 좀 너무했어.
> *(One line or not, that was a bit much.)*
>
> **준호:** 알았어, 미안해. 떡볶이 살게.
> *(Okay, sorry. I'll buy you tteokbokki.)*
>
> **하나:** 치사하게 음식으로 넘어가려고? …그래도 먹을래.
> *(Trying to get out of it with food? That's cheap. …I'll still eat it, though.)*

Three different "mean" words, three different jobs: 나빠 for the hurt, 너무했어 for the action, and 치사하게 for the cheap move at the end. Nobody says 못됐어, because nobody here is actually cruel.

## Mistakes learners make

**Using 못됐어 for small things.** A learner hears it in a revenge drama and uses it when a friend is five minutes late. To a Korean ear, that sounds like a real insult. For small stuff, 나빠 or 너무해 is right.

**Saying 나빠 to someone older or in a formal setting.** 나빠 is banmal (casual speech). To a teacher or boss, calling them "bad" at all is risky. Talk about the situation instead: **좀 서운했어요** (*jom seo-un-hae-sseo-yo*, "I felt a little hurt").

**Translating "mean" as 의미.** 의미 (*ui-mi*) is "meaning," as in "what does this word mean." "You're mean" has nothing to do with it. If you type "mean" into an app with no context, check which one you got.

**Forgetting the tone does half the work.** 나빠 with a smile and a pout is flirting. 나빠 said flat and quiet, eyes down, is a real hurt. Dramas lean on this constantly — listen for it.
