The Korean word for love is **사랑** (*sarang*), and the sentence you are probably looking for is **사랑해** (*saranghae*) — "I love you." That is the short answer. The longer one is more useful: Koreans say 사랑해 far less often than subtitles suggest, and it almost never shows up at the start of a relationship. The two words carrying most of the romantic weight in a K-drama are **좋아해** (*joahae*), "I like you," which is what a confession actually sounds like, and **보고 싶어** (*bogo sipeo*), "I miss you," which is how affection gets said out loud on an ordinary Tuesday. If you learn 사랑해 and stop, you have the word everyone knows and none of the words anyone uses.

> - Love (the noun) — **사랑** (*sarang*)
> - I love you — **사랑해** (*saranghae*)
> - I like you — **좋아해** (*joahae*)
> - I miss you — **보고 싶어** (*bogo sipeo*)
> - One-sided love, a crush — **짝사랑** (*jjaksarang*)
> - First love — **첫사랑** (*cheotsarang*)
> - The talking stage — **썸** (*sseom*)
> - Dating, as an activity — **연애** (*yeonae*)
> - That fluttery feeling — **설렘** (*seolleom*)

## 사랑 is the feeling, 사랑해 is the sentence

**사랑** on its own is a noun. It goes in song titles, on café signs, in **첫사랑** (*cheotsarang*), "first love," and in **사랑 이야기** (*sarang iyagi*), "a love story." You cannot say 사랑 at someone and have it mean anything, the way "love" alone works in English texting.

To aim it at a person you need the verb **사랑하다** (*saranghada*), and the form you hear is **사랑해** — the casual present tense. Notice what is missing: there is no "I" and no "you." Korean drops both when the situation makes them obvious, which is almost always. The whole sentence is three syllables, evenly weighted: *sa-rang-hae*. English speakers tend to lean on the last one and stretch it into "hay." It is closer to a short "heh."

One thing that surprises learners: 사랑해 is not reserved for romance. Children say it to parents, parents say it to children, and close friends say it to each other, especially among women. Hearing it does not automatically mean what it would mean in English.

## 좋아해 versus 사랑해: the line Korean draws

In English, "I like you" is a weaker version of "I love you." In Korean, **좋아해** is not weaker — it is the one that changes everything, because it is the standard confession. A character who has spent twelve episodes saying nothing finally says **나 너 좋아해** (*na neo joahae*), "I like you," and the show treats it as the turning point. That is not a translation problem in the subtitles. 좋아해 really is the line.

Then there is a sequence, and it matters:

1. **좋아해** — the confession. The moment the feeling gets admitted.
2. **우리 사귈래?** (*uri sagwillae?*) — "want to go out with me?" The word **사귀다** (*sagwida*) means to be in a relationship, and this question is the official start.
3. **사랑해** — weeks or months later. It is the deeper word, kept for a relationship that already exists.

Say 사랑해 to someone you have known for three days and a Korean person will hear it as either a joke or something slightly alarming. Say 좋아해 and you have made a clear, serious, perfectly normal move.

A related pair that trips people up: **좋아** (*joa*) and **좋아해** (*joahae*). 좋아 means "it's good" or "I like it," pointed at a thing or a situation. 좋아해 means "I like" and takes a person or an interest. Telling someone **좋아** when you meant 좋아해 lands as "yeah, fine" instead of a confession, which is a rough way to lose a moment.

## 보고 싶어 is the one you will actually use

**보고 싶어** breaks down as 보다 (*boda*), "to see," plus -고 싶다 (*-go sipda*), "want to." Literally, "I want to see you." It translates as "I miss you," and it is the single most-used affectionate phrase in Korean — between couples, friends, family, and fans and idols.

Pronunciation note: 싶어 comes out as something like *shi-peo*, not *sip-uh*. **보고 싶어요** (*bogo sipeoyo*) is the polite version, and **보고 싶었어요** (*bogo sipeosseoyo*) is "I missed you," which is what you say when you finally see someone again.

There is a second word for missing, **그리워** (*geuriwo*), from 그립다. It is wistful and distant — for a place, a season, a time in your life, a person you may not see again. Using 그리워 about a partner who is away for the weekend sounds like you are narrating a memoir. Use 보고 싶어.

## Choosing between 사랑해, 사랑해요, and 사랑합니다

Same verb, three levels, three completely different rooms.

| Form | Romanization | Where it belongs |
|---|---|---|
| 사랑해 | *saranghae* | A partner, close friends, family. Casual and warm. |
| 사랑해요 | *saranghaeyo* | Polite and soft. What fans call out to an artist, and what an artist says back. |
| 사랑합니다 | *saranghamnida* | Formal. Stage announcements, speeches, a performer thanking a full venue. |
| 사랑한다 | *saranghanda* | Plain declarative. Song lyrics, and often how fathers and older men say it. |

The level is not politeness decoration — it tells the listener what kind of relationship you think you are in. 사랑해요 from a stranger is sweet. 사랑해 from a stranger is presumptuous.

## The words for being in love

**연애** (*yeonae*) is dating as a whole activity — **연애하다** (*yeonaehada*) is "to be dating," and **연애 중** (*yeonae jung*) is "in a relationship." **애인** (*aein*) is a romantic partner with the gender left out, which is why it shows up in dramas when the speaker is being coy; everyday speech usually says **남자친구** (*namjachingu*) or **여자친구** (*yeojachingu*).

**썸** (*sseom*) is clipped from English "something," and **썸 타다** (*sseom tada*) — literally "to ride a something" — is the talking stage: mutual interest, lots of texting, nothing declared. **짝사랑** (*jjaksarang*) is love that only one side feels, built from 짝, "one of a pair," and 사랑. And **설렘** (*seolleom*) is the flutter itself, the feeling a drama is engineering when the camera holds on someone's face a beat too long.

Here is roughly how the confession scene sounds, stripped of the music:

Them: **나 할 말 있어.** — *na hal mal isseo.* — "I have something to tell you."  
Them: **나 너 좋아해.** — *na neo joahae.* — "I like you."  
You: **나도 좋아해.** — *nado joahae.* — "I like you too."  
Them: **우리 사귈까?** — *uri sagwilkka?* — "Should we go out, then?"

Four lines, no 사랑해 anywhere. That word comes later, and it lands harder because of the wait.

## Four ways learners get these words wrong

**Adding 당신.** Textbooks gloss **당신** (*dangsin*) as "you," so learners build **당신을 사랑해요** (*dangsineul saranghaeyo*). Real Koreans use 당신 between older married couples, in song lyrics, or when starting an argument. Drop the pronoun entirely: 사랑해요 is complete.

**Starting at 사랑해.** It is the word every fan knows, so it is the word every fan reaches for first. 좋아해 is the one that fits the beginning of something.

**Using 그리워 for a person.** Save it for places and times. People get 보고 싶어.

**Treating 사랑해 as purely romantic.** When a Korean friend sends you 사랑해 after you helped them move apartments, nothing has changed between you. It is closer to "you're the best."
